# 1. HENRY CORBETT

<table><tr><td colspan="5"><p>STANDARDIZED PATIENT SIMULATION GUIDELINES</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><p>HENRY CORBETT</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ol><li>Case Overview</li></ol></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ul><li><ul><li>Henry Corbett is a senior adult who presents to an outpatient office with complaints of dizziness, fatigue, low energy and loss of appetite.</li></ul></li></ul></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ol><li>ENCOUNTER SETTING</li></ol></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ul><li>Exam room of an ambulatory (outpatient) medical practice.</li><li>You have no prior relationship with the student doctor.</li></ul></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ol><li>WHAT GOES ON IN THIS ENCOUNTER</li></ol></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><p>Student doctor is expected to review the medical chart before entering the room, take your medical history, perform a physical examination, present a diagnosis and lay out a treatment/work-up plan. After leaving the room, s/he documents the encounter in a patient note.</p><p>SP Actor is expected to provide the history provided in this document in response to the student doctor’s questions, cooperate with the physical exam expressing pain and presenting/acting physical limitations as directed in this document.</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><p>TIMING</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><p>Encounter: 14 minutes</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ol><li>PATIENT CHARACTER PRESENTATION: HENRY CORBETT</li></ol></td></tr><tr><td><p><strong>Age Range</strong></p></td><td colspan="4"><p>75 years old</p></td></tr><tr><td rowspan="3"><p><strong>Presentation</strong></p></td><td><p><strong>Body Language/ Symptom Presentation</strong></p></td><td colspan="3"><ul><li>No distress, but low energy level – not peppy or particularly jovial.</li></ul></td></tr><tr><td><p><strong>Affect</strong></p></td><td colspan="3"><ul><li>Good eye contact, not depressed.</li><li>Clearly frustrated and concerned about your condition --- somewhat fearful that it may be permanent – which is brought out in part of your opening statement.</li></ul><p><em>This is no way to live!!</em></p></td></tr><tr><td><p><strong>Appearance/ Costuming</strong></p></td><td colspan="3"><ul><li>Gown</li><li>Otherwise, well groomed.</li></ul></td></tr><tr><td><p><strong>Starting Position</strong></p></td><td colspan="4"><p>Sitting in the chair.</p></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><ol><li>HENRY CORBETT’S STORY</li></ol></td></tr><tr><td colspan="5"><p>Starting position for each encounter: Seated in chair slouched over – indicative of your current state of always being tired and having little energy. When asked to move to the exam table, you do it in a way that illustrates this lack of pep.</p><p><strong>Chief complaint</strong>: <em>I’ve been feeling dizzy and lightheaded.</em></p><p><strong>History of Present Illness</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Dizziness</strong><ul><li>Feel dizzy all the time -- a fairly constant sensation of lightheaded dizziness. Room DOES NOT spin.</li><li>You have the sensation that you are going to faint pretty much all the time, but you haven’t fainted yet or fallen, although you are now very fearful of falling. Because of this problem, you can not engage in your normal activities, such as bowling, or even driving to get out of the house. In short, your life has been markedly impacted.</li><li><strong>If asked</strong>: Aside from this sensation, your balance is pretty good.</li><li><strong>If asked</strong>: Nothing seems to make this feeling better. It may be a little worse when you stand. Changing your position or moving your head around does NOT seem to have any effect.</li><li>Symptoms began about 2 months ago, and before then you never experienced anything like this.</li><li>Getting worse these last 2 weeks. You feel like this all the time.</li><li><strong>If asked</strong>: NO heart palpitations, chest pain, shortness of breath, coughing, wheezing.</li></ul></li></ul><p><strong>Other Medical History – ONLY IF ASKED</strong></p><ul><li><strong>High blood pressure</strong></li><li><strong>Leg Swelling</strong></li><li>Your get swelling in your legs, mostly at your ankles and feet. When this happens, you take an extra furosemide pill (brand name: Lasix; commonly referred to as a “water pill”), and this does bring down the swelling. <strong>You took 2 yesterday.</strong> [Note: This is a key piece of information in this case. Do not reveal that you take extra medicine unless the students ask the right questions, such as WHAT DO YOU DO WHEN YOUR LEGS SWELL? Or HOW MUCH LASIX/FUROSEMIDE DO YOU TAKE? Do not give this information away otherwise.]</li><li>Reveal the leg swelling as soon as students ask you if you have ANY OTHER SYMPTOMS OTHER THAN DIZZINESS.</li></ul><p><em>Well my legs sometimes swell up a bit, but I don’t know if that has anything to do with anything.</em></p><ul><li>Alternately, students might not ask you about “any other symptoms”. Instead, they may start by asking you what medication you are on. When they see Lasix/furosemide on your list they may ask you WHY ARE YOU TAKING LASIX? before they ask you about “other symptoms.” At any time that they ask you about taking Lasix, explain that</li></ul><p><em>I take it for the swelling in my legs.</em></p><ul><li><strong>Bowel/Bladder</strong></li><li><strong>Only if asked</strong>, DO YOU HAVE ANY URINARY PROBLEMS?</li></ul><p><em>I have an enlarged prostate.</em> For this, you take Tamsulosin.</p><ul><li><strong>If asked</strong> about frequent urination (DO YOU URINATE FREQUENTLY?):</li></ul><p><em>Just when I take my extra water pill.</em> (See medications.)</p><p>If student follows with WHY DO YOU TAKE AN EXTRA WATER PILL?</p><p>Answer: <em>When my legs swell up.</em></p><ul><li><strong>If asked</strong> about pain/burning with urination or incontinence: There is neither.</li><li><strong>If asked</strong> about constipation:</li></ul><p><em>I have been a little constipated. Usually, I go every day, but not lately.</em></p><ul><li><strong>If Asked about Stool.</strong></li></ul><p><em>I have noticed my stool has been a little harder and darker lately.</em></p><ul><li><strong>Digestion/Food/Fluid</strong></li><li><strong>If asked,</strong> you have reflux.</li><li><strong>If asked</strong> about your appetite: it’s somewhat diminished of late, and you’ve been getting indigestion, so you have been eating less.</li></ul><p><em>I used to eat dinner with my daughter, but she just got a new job so she is too busy.</em></p><p><em>But I don’t seem to have much of an appetite anyway.</em></p><ul><li>If asked about fluid intake: You drink water to take your medicine and several glasses during the day. You also drink 1-2 cups of coffee each morning.</li><li>No nausea or vomiting.</li><li><strong>General Constitution:</strong></li><li>No depression, chest pain, palpitations, cough, wheeze, shortness of breath, difficulty sleeping. No hearing loss or changes in vision.</li><li>You’ve been feeling more tired than usual lately.</li><li><strong>Medications</strong></li><li>When – and only when – the student doctor asks about <strong>prescription medication</strong>, provide the <em>Henry Corbett Prescription Medication</em> card to the student doctor. [No need for you to memorize these drugs or the dosages, but do learn what disorder is being treated by them.]</li><li>Amlodipine 10 mg po daily -- to treat your high blood pressure.</li><li>Furosemide 40 mg po daily – a diuretic to help rid your body of extra fluid.</li><li>Enalapril 10 mg qd – to treat your high blood pressure.</li><li>Tamsulosin 0.4 mg qd (30 minutes after a meal) – to treat your enlarged prostate.</li></ul><p><em>I’ve been on the same stuff for years.</em></p><ul><li>You take all of your medications as prescribed, except you double your furosemide (Lasix, “water pill”) if legs are swollen.</li><li>You find that you have been taking that extra pill more frequently of late – 2-3 times/wk -- to keep the swelling in your legs down. In fact, you took 2 yesterday.</li><li>When – and only when – the student doctor asks about <strong>over-the-counter medication</strong>, hand over the <em>Henry Corbett Over-the-Counter Medication</em> card to the student doctor.</li><li>ASA 81mg po daily [baby aspirin] -- for heart health, as advised by your doctor.</li><li>Mylanta -- for heartburn, which has been happening more lately.</li><li>Advil -- 1 each day and more when your back is bothering you.</li></ul><p><strong>Home Life</strong></p><ul><li>Live alone since wife died (heart attack).</li></ul><p><em>My wife died a couple of years ago. Now it's just me puttering around the house.</em></p><p><em>My daughter lives pretty close, though she just started a new job and is pretty busy.</em></p><ul><li>Hobbies:</li></ul><p><em>I enjoy reading, especially Civil War history. I just started a book about Jefferson Davis.</em></p><p><em>My wife and I used to play bridge at the senior center; sometimes, I go over there to play cards.</em></p><ul><li>Exercise:</li></ul><p><em>I bowl in a senior league. I think that's why my back sometimes hurts, from the twisting.</em></p><ul><li>Function:</li></ul><p><em>I can get dressed and go to the bathroom myself.</em></p><p><em>I take my medications.</em></p><p><em>I can drive and do my own shopping, keep up with the bills, all that.</em></p><ul><li>Occupation:</li></ul><p><em>I'm retired. I was an accountant. Sometimes, I help out during tax season, just to keep my head in the game.</em></p><ul><li><strong>Substances</strong></li><li>Alcohol: <em>Sometimes, I have a beer with dinner.</em></li><li>Coffee: <em>I don’t drink much coffee, just a cup or two in the morning.</em></li><li>Tobacco, street drugs: Never.</li><li>No allergies, surgeries or other illnesses.</li><li>You are up to date on your flu vaccine. You received a pneumonia shot about a year ago.</li><li><strong>Family History</strong></li><li>Mother died at age 69 of complications of a stroke.</li><li>Father lived to age 94 -- died of old age.</li><li>Brother: 79 – has high blood pressure (just like you).</li><li>Sister: 73. Had a stroke last year. Lives in assisted living facility.</li></ul><p><em>She’s close so I try to get out to see her once a week.</em></p><p><strong>SCRIPTED REVIEW OF THE HISTORY</strong></p><p>Ask about the onset of symptoms: <em>The dizziness started about 2 months ago, I guess. It has been on and off, but fairly constant over the past 2 weeks.</em></p><p>Ask if room spins: <em>No. The room doesn't spin.</em></p><p>Ask about lightheadedness or fainting: <em>Sometimes, I feel like I’m going to faint, but I don’t.</em></p><p>Ask about passing out: <em>No I have never passed out.</em></p><p>Ask about duration of symptoms: <em>I feel this way all the time.</em></p><p>Ask about falls: <em>No, I haven't fallen.</em></p><p>Ask about what makes the problem worse: <em>I am dizzy all of the time, but it seems worse when I stand. I often have to hold on to something.</em></p><p>Ask about prior episodes: <em>I don’t remember anything like this before.</em></p><p>Ask about medications and medication compliance: <em>Well, I have been taking an extra water pill more frequently to keep the swelling down in my legs. Maybe 2 to 3 times per week. I took two yesterday.</em> <em>I’ve been on the same stuff for a while now.</em></p><p>Ask about over-the-counter medications: <em>I take Mylanta when I get heartburn and I take an Advil every day, a few extra when my back bothers me.</em></p><p>Ask about other, associated symptoms: <em>No.</em></p><p>Assess oral intake of food / water; assess diet: <em>Okay, but I have been getting indigestion lately so I have been eating less.</em> <em>Normal stuff – maybe a little cereal in the morning. A sandwich at lunch. Some kind of dinner at night. A TV dinner sometimes. My daughter often sends me care packages. Occasionally, I’ll make eggs or something easy for dinner.</em> <em>I use to eat dinner with my daughter, but she just got a new job so she is too busy.</em> <em>I drink a glass of water to take my medicines and several glasses during the day. I have a cup or two of coffee in the morning.</em></p><p>Ask about living situation: <em>Nope, been on my own since the wife died.</em></p><p>Ask about symptom impact on patient's function: <em>I can get dressed and go to the bathroom myself. I take my medications also. I drive and do my own shopping.</em></p><p>Check lower extremities for pitting edema: <em>My legs get swollen, mostly at my ankles and feet. But, I take an extra pill to keep it down.</em></p><p>Checked for epigastric tenderness: <em>Yes, it is a little tender.</em></p><p><strong>PHYSICAL EXAMINATION</strong></p><ul><li>Allow your body to respond normally and naturally to any physical examination techniques utilized by the student doctor.</li><li>Auscultate (listen to) your heart.</li><li>Check lower extremities for edema. Properly done, this should involve their pushing against the skin of your legs/ankles with a finger tip and observing whether the finger tip leaves an impression when it is removed.</li><li>Check your blood pressure in supine (lying down) and standing positions. Only if the student doctor measures BOTH of these blood pressures do you hand him the prop <em>Henry Corbett BP</em> card.</li><li>Palpate your upper abdomen for tenderness. When pressed, you do have a small amount of tenderness in the Upper Middle Area of the Abdomen (Epigastrium).</li></ul><p><strong>CLOSING</strong></p><ul><li>After completing the history and the physical examination, the student doctor should summarize his findings of your condition and lay out a plan of care. You have no questions.</li></ul><p><strong>Osteopathic treatment</strong></p><p>No osteopathic treatment is indicated in this encounter. None is expected by students. If students do wish to treat you osteopathically, agree to their requests.</p></td></tr></table>
