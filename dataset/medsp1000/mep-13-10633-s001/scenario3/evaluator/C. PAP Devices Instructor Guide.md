**Learning Objectives**

By the end of this session, learners should be able to

* Identify the most common uses of PAP therapy encountered in general outpatient medical practice
* Understand conceptual differences between CPAP and bilevel & their major uses
* Perform a basic interpretation of a PAP download to assess usage and efficacy

**Patient Scenario:** Mrs. X presents to your clinic as a new patient transferring care from another provider. She has no records with her. She says to you “I feel really tired all the time. I had a sleep study and they just told me I had to use this machine – no one ever told me what my results were. But I really don’t know why I’m using it. I feel really tired all the time; I don’t think it’s working. Do I still need to use it?”

Two questions should arise when evaluating a patient on a PAP device:

1. What am I treating? diagnosis
2. How is it working? Settings and efficacy

| **Label** | **Device** | **Description** | **Used to treat:** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **B** | Bilevel without backup rate | Provides pressure support (PS) during inspiratory phase to increase VT and therefore MV. | Hypoventilation. Can sometimes be used for severe COPD as a QoL measure to reduce work of breathing. |

When approaching a patient, I think about:

1. Settings: Are they correct? Are they appropriate?
2. Are there any problems/complications limiting efficacy?
   1. Compliance: is the patient using it? ≥4hr per night recommended if symptomatic, ideally all night long
   2. Mask issues (old, poor fit, claustrophobia)
   3. Emergence of central apneas
3. PAP machines use sophisticated algorithms to provide specific target pressures and then evaluate their efficacy as well as common hindrances to therapeutic delivery of pressure.
4. Typical measures provided on almost all current device downloads are:

* Settings: are they correct?
* Settings: are they adequate?
* Is the patient using the device
* Mask leak
* Pressures actually delivered
* Apnea Hypopnea Index (AHI)
* Bilevel devices can also *estimate* minute ventilation and RR, though they can’t tell you if that is actually sufficient for your particular patient.

**What is an AHI?**

Apnea-Hypopnea Index, used to describe sleep apnea severity. **Conceptually it is the number of times/hour there is a respiratory event (reduction or cessation in airflow)**. AHI is the major parameter on a sleep study used to diagnose sleep apnea.

AHI = number apneas and hypopneas per hour

AI = number apneas per hour

Some devices will breakout the AI into categories: obstructive (OAI) vs central (CAI) vs unknown (UAI)

simplified concept/definitions:

apnea = cessation of airflow

hypopnea = reduction of airflow

**AHI Goal with therapy is <=5 events/hr [normal range]**

* Device B-↑ AHI with mask leak.
  + BONUS: Fix the patient’s mask. Don’t increase the pressure.
