# Case 1- Abdominal mass

Chief Complaint: Sam Smith is an 18 month old boy who has been sent to the emergency department by his pediatrician for an abdominal mass.

History of Present Illness: The abdominal mass was discovered during his routine well child check today. After the mass was identified on his exam, his mother/father mentioned that they thought his belly had looked a little bigger over the past month but had just thought it was normal weight gain. In retrospect they also have noticed a slight decrease in his appetite in the past two weeks and he has seemed more tired. He has not had any recent fevers, emesis or diarrhea. He has had a normal amount of wet diapers, and there has been no blood in the urine. He has been growing normally. His grandmother mentioned he looked pale earlier this week. He has been reaching his developmental milestones on time. He walks and has 4-5 words.

Past Medical History: Born at 39 weeks by normal spontaneous vaginal delivery. No medical issues

Immunizations: Up to date

Family History: 6 year old brother with asthma, maternal grandfather died from lung cancer

Social History: He lives with his parents and 6 year old brother. He has never traveled outside the United States. There are no pets at home.

Medications: Multivitamin

Allergies: None

Review of Systems: Negative other than as reported in HPI

# Physical Exam:

Vitals: HR 132 BP 92/49 RR 20 O2 sat $100 \%$ on RA Temp 36.9 Wt 12.5kg

Gen: Alert, no acute distress, somewhat paler in complexion than his parents

HEENT: Normal

Pulm: Clear to auscultation, comfortable respirations, no crackles/rhonchi/wheeze

CV: Regular rate and rhythm, no murmurs, $^ { 2 + }$ pulses

Abdomen: Normal bowel sounds, soft, non-tender, a firm mass in the RUQ approximately 5 cm in diameter. No hepatosplenomegaly appreciated.

Back: Normal, no CVA tenderness

GU: Normal, testes down bilaterally

Extremities: Normal

Neuro: Exam limited by age. PERRL, face symmetric, moving all extremities well. Able to sit and stand. Normal reflexes

Skin: Pallor, no bruising or petechiae

Labs (from primary doctor in clinic today):
CBC: WBC 12.1 $45 \%$ PMNs, $52 \%$ lymphs, $2 \%$ monos, $1 \%$ eos), Hbg 10.4, platelets 498
Chemistry: Na 140, K 4.0, Cl 115, HCO3 25, BUN 8, Cr 0.4, Glu 112
Urinalysis: normal

US abdomen: 6x8cm solid mass arising in or adjacent to the superior pole of the right kidney with extension to the inferior aspect of the liver. The inferior vena cava and left kidney appear normal.

Consult: Based on your initial evaluation and work-up you are suspicious for a malignant tumor and would like to consult hematology/oncology about what further hematologic and radiographic evaluation is needed. You are planning on sending a liver function panel and repeating the CBC and electrolyte panel. You know that urine should be sent for homovanillic acid and vanillylmandelic acid to evaluate for neuroblastoma but are not sure if this should be sent from the emergency department. You would like to know if there are any additional diagnostic labs that should be sent now. You would also like recommendations about further imaging. You feel the child needs a CT of the abdomen to further evaluate the mass but wonder if they would recommend more extensive imaging to evaluate for other tumors and metastases.

# Purpose- What do we want?

• Elicit recommendations for diagnostic evaluation or therapeutic management of a specific problem Secure a diagnostic or therapeutic procedure for a patient • Provide appropriate follow-up for a patient

# Components of a Consult Request

Your name, service/department and role
• Name, age and gender of the patient Relevant history and exam findings Pertinent results- labs, radiographic studies, etc. Your impression
• A clearly framed question Urgency of the consultation Clarification on how consultation will be communicated
Be courteous ☺

# Example

“This is John Doe. I’m an intern in the ED and am calling you for a consult. There is a previously healthy 4 year old boy here, Peter Jetson, with high fever and refusal to walk since this morning. His right knee has been very red and swollen. There is no other joint involvement. There is a scab over the knee from a fall he had a week ago but no other recent trauma. His white count is 21 with a left shift, his CRP and ESR are both elevated at 5.3 and 113. We are getting x-rays of the knee, but I am worried that he might have septic arthritis. We would like you to see him and help us decide if he needs an US or if you should tap the knee without any other tests. He is stable, but we would like to start antibiotics soon if it is, in fact, a septic joint. We can discuss your recommendations in the ED after you have seen the patient.”

# What is difficult news?

• Malignancy
Life-long chronic illness Degenerative disease Devastating neurologic outcome
• Death
• Concern for abuse Painful or invasive diagnostic or therapeutic intervention
Inability to find an organic cause for chronic pain
Medical error

# Goals During the Discussion of Difficult News

• Gather information
• Transmit medical information
• Provide support Elicit collaboration and support in developing further diagnostic and/or treatment plans

# SPIKES Protocol

• Setting up the interview
• Assessing the patient’s perceptions
• Obtaining the patient’s invitation Giving knowledge and information to patient Addressing emotions with empathy Strategy and summary

# Setting Up the Interview

• Mentally prepare yourself
• Provide privacy Determine who should be present
. • Sit down
Connect with the patient via eye contact and body language
• Minimize interruptions

# Assessing the Patient’s Perceptions

• Gauge understanding based on concerns that have been raised

Use open ended questions to determine what the patient understands

• Why tests were done? • What results they may know about? • What are their biggest concerns?

# Obtaining the Patient’s Invitation

• Establish that you are prepared to discuss medical information with the patient Assess whether the patient is prepared to proceed • Warn the patient you have difficult news

# Giving Knowledge and Information to Patient

• Use nontechnical words
Avoid excessive bluntness Give information in small pieces and assess comprehension in between
Avoid insinuating that there “is nothing left to do”

# Addressing Emotions with Empathy

• Look for emotion
• Identify the emotion
• Identify the reason for the emotion
• Validate the emotion

# Strategy and Summary

• Establish that there will be more opportunities to review
• Elicit patient’s goals
• Discuss options or planned course
• Identify resources for patient
• Encourage patient to gather questions for next meeting
