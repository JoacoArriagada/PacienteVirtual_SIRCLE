# INSTRUCTOR’S GUIDE FOR TOTAL ANOMALOUS PULMONARY VENOUSCONNECTION SIMULATION SCENARIO

Title: “28-day-old male with non-obstructing supracardiac Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection and atrial septal defect presenting with pulmonary overcirculation”

Target Audience: Emergency Medicine and Pediatric Residents and Fellows, Critical Care Fellows, Physician Assistants, Nurse Practitioners

# III. Overview:

Case participants are presented with an infant in respiratory distress and circulatory compromise with evidence of increased work of breathing, poor oxygen saturation, and central cyanosis. They will be expected to obtain an initial set of complete vital signs, perform a focused examination, place the infant on monitoring, obtain a history of present illness from the parent and stabilize the patient. They should articulate a differential diagnosis and establish a plan, but may initially proceed along multiple pathways (considering metabolic, infectious, cardiac, pulmonary, possibly endocrine and toxicologic etiologies). Participants should employ some variant of the hyperoxia test to help differentiate pulmonary from cardiac disease, as well as four extremity (or right upper and lower) blood pressures in consideration of coarctation of the aorta.

Participants may respond to the poor initial oxygen saturation by administering oxygen by nasal canula or facemask, but this will result in a slight worsening of hypoxia $\left( 1 { - } 2 \mathrm { m m } \right.$ Hg) due to lowering pulmonary vascular resistance and increasing pulmonary overcirculation as might be expected in a case of Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection (TAPVC). The patient will deteriorate over the transition period, prompting endotracheal intubation that will somewhat improve the patient’s status due to positive pressure ventilation. Definitive pharmacologic treatment will include a diuretic and a vasoactive agent and/or positive inotrope.

Participants will be expected to interpret the available data, including the electrocardiagram, laboratory results, chest radiograph, physical exam signs/symptoms, and hyperoxia test, and conclude that the infant has some form of congenital heart disease and is in a state of pulmonary overcirculation or congestive heart failure. They are not expected to specifically diagnose TAPVC. The significance of this particular variant, a non-obstructing supracardiac type with concurrent atrial septal defect will be discussed in the debriefing lecture. A treatment plan will be expected to address pulmonary edema, which may occur in conjunction with the consultation of a pediatric cardiologist. The case ends when endotracheal intubation, treatment with furosemide, a vasoactive agent and/or positive inotrope, and cardiology consultation are completed.

# IV. Purpose

This simulation teaching case is intended to train the Emergency Medicine or Pediatric Resident or Fellow, or Critical Care Fellow, to rapidly assess and manage a critically ill infant with a congenital heart disease (CHD), in this case, TAPVC. Because undifferentiated clinical presentations of CHD are relatively uncommon and residents and fellows have less clinical experience to draw upon, it is important to offer a form of

teaching that will allow the learner to engage the clinical process, potentially make mistakes that can be learned from without adverse affects on an actual patient, and utilize acquired skills to problem-solve in the absence of direct supervision. It has the potential to be a memorable learning experience because, as in real life, both mistakes and successes are hard-earned and less easily forgotten.

# V. Conceptual background

In populations with excellent prenatal care and sonographic fetal assessment during pregnancy, congenital heart defects are often identified, referred to, and managed by pediatric cardiologists or surgeons before delivery. Minor cardiac abnormalities may not be readily identified on ultrasound evaluation initially, but with adequate primary care, symptomatic patients or those with abnormal examination findings may be identified early and referred to an appropriate specialist. The typical presentation of a pediatric patient with congenital heart disease to the Emergency Department is one with an acute exacerbation of a known underlying problem. At risk, however, are those patients who do not receive timely prenatal care and those with significant, undetected cardiac abnormalities who present acutely to the Emergency Department, as the current case exemplifies.

# VI. Implementation

Preparation for this case involves a review of the accompanying study materials and program files and training of confederates to portray a parent, a nurse, and a pediatric cardiologist. The simulation utilizes Laerdal’s SimBaby software version 1.6.2. An appropriate number of active learner-participants for this case would be two or three, however, a larger audience of learners may also watch the scenario in real time and receive learning benefit from the simulated encounter and lecture thereafter. The entire real time encounter transpires over 15-20 minutes. The facilitator may serve multiple roles: operating the high fidelity manikin by computer control, acting as the pediatric cardiologist via telephone, and serving as lecturer for the debriefing session that includes a 20 minute Powerpoint presentation. Rehearsal of the program handlers, familiarity with the embedded trends, briefing actors on the scenario and its progression, and anticipation of participant requests for medications and resources will help the scenario run smoothly and successfully.

# VII. Limitations

A case of TAPVC is a plausible acute presentation for undiagnosed congenital heart disease that may present with respiratory or circulatory compromise in the Emergency Department. Although it is a rarely encountered scenario, basic assessment and management strategies centered upon airway, breathing, and circulation concerns that are applicable to many critical care cases can be taught using it as an example. Information specific to TAPVC, such as appreciating the various anatomic types and potential surgical management strategies are less important to the Emergency Physician and are therefore outlined or mentioned, but not detailed in the accompanying lecture. In general with simulated learning cases, the potential for active learning is limited to the few participants who are involved in the scenario who have the ability to engage as active players. One may argue that they benefit more than the audience of passive learners

watching the scenario, however, audience members have the advantage of being removed from the stress of the scenario and can reflect on both the scenario parameters and the actions of their actively-learning peers.

As the availability of ultrasound has steadily increased in the Emergency Department, a future improvement for this scenario might include simulation of bedside echocardiography. Also, adjustments could be made to the vital signs and trends so that the patient’s hemodynamic parameters would allow consideration of a broader range of possible pharmacologic agents to improve perfusion.

# VIII. Learning Objectives::

a. Primary:

i. When presented with an infant in respiratory distress, participants will be able to assess airway, breathing, and circulation, identify key signs and symptoms such as retractions, grunting, cyanosis, and initiate stabilization with oxygenation, ventilation, and circulation support.
ii. When presented with an infant in respiratory and circulatory distress with clinical, radiographic, and electrocardiographic findings suggestive of congenital heart disease and pulmonary edema, the learner will initiate a treatment plan that may include positive pressure mechanical ventilation, diuretics, inotropic or vasoactive agents, and consultation of a pediatric cardiologist.

# b. Secondary:

i. When presented with the patient’s parent, a participant on the team will obtain a complete medical history including history of present illness and review of systems, birth, pre-natal, maternal, feeding/stooling, family, social, and growth histories, and allergies, exposures, immunizations, medications, and surgery information.

ii. Given an undifferentiated case of infantile respiratory distress, the learner will auscultate for heart murmurs and lung sounds, employ hyperoxia testing to help differentiate between pulmonary and cardiac etiologies, and measure both upper and lower extremity blood pressures in consideration of coarctation of the aorta.

iii. When presented with clinical data supporting a cardiac cause, after or while stabilizing the patient, participants will consult a pediatric cardiologist for expert opinion on more advanced management of pulmonary overcirculation, blood pressure control, and indications for inotropic support or a vasoactive agent as needed.

# c. Critical actions checklist:

(Critical actions are expected to be completed in an orderly sequence, pausing only to manage abnormal findings)

i. Examine airway for possible obstruction and auscultate lungs
ii. Check brachial pulse within first 10 seconds of assessment in accordance with PALS guidelines
iii. Check capillary refill iv. When attempt at peripheral intravenous access fails due to poor perfusion, rapid placement of intraosseous access is expected v. Peform thorough physical exam, including auscultation and characterization of heart sounds vi. Removal of infant’s clothing to fully examine patient vii. Obtain from parent items not documented on the Emergency Medical Services (EMS) run sheet pertaining to history of present illness and review of systems viii. Place patient on telemetry and pulmonary monitoring ix. Administer oxygen for initial hypoxia x. Obtain upper and lower extremity blood pressures xi. Employ hyperoxia testing xii. Bolus with IV fluid for hypoperfusion and concern for sepsis
xiii. Secure airway with endotracheal intubation after worsening respiratory distress and hypoxia with non-invasive oxygenation
xiv. Obtain electrocardiogram and chest plain film radiograph xv. Order serum tests, including blood gas, chemistry panel, lactic acid, complete blood count, blood cultures
xvi. Support circulation with intravenous fluid, inotropic agent, or vasoactive agent as needed
xvii. In response to radiograph and findings suggestive of congestive heart failure or pulmonary overcirculation, administer furosemide and start positive pressure ventilation
xviii. Consult Pediatric Cardiologist
xix. Admit to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit or transfer patient to appropriate care facility

# iv. Initial State Summary:

1. Significant exam findings: Tachypneic, tachycardic, borderline hypotensive, crying, intercostal retractions, poor perfusion, central cyanosis, cool extremities, thready pulses, significant systolic and diastolic murmurs, widely split and fixed S2.

2. Information:

a. Chronically: poor feeding with history of poor weight gain
b. Acutely: 2 days fussiness and difficulty breathing today with new cough and respiratory distress but afebrile
c. Vital signs from EMS significant for tachycardia, tachypnea, and hypoxia. No pre-hospital glucose measurement was obtained

# 3. Expected behaviors/actions:

a. Obtain missing history of present illness and review of systems elements
b. Place patient on monitoring and obtain initial ED vital signs
c. Attempt to correct hypoxia with nasal canula or facemask
d. Appreciation of abnormal heart sounds
e. Attempt to obtain intravenous access. Due to poor peripheral access, after failed attempts, subsequent prompt intraosseous access is expected
f. Obtain fingerstick glucose, chemistry panel, complete blood count, lactate, venous blood gas, coagulation panel, urinalysis, blood cultures, chest radiography, electrocardiogram
g. Treat hypoglycemia (fingerstick glucose $= 5 0 \ : \mathrm { m g / d L }$ )
h. Hyperoxia test performed (results return during the Transition State with other lab values – post oxygenation does not improve hypoxemia)
i. Upper and lower extremity blood pressure check for coarctation (results will demonstrate borderline hypotension for age, but will not suggest coartation)
j. Verbalize differential diagnosis and communicate initial plan to team
k. Console parent and inform about treatment plan

# 4. Response to treatment:

a. Oxygen administration does not improve saturation
b. Treating hypoglycemia will not result in clinical improvement, but a follow-up fingerstick will demonstrate resolution of hypoglycemia (fingerstick glucose $\# 2 = 8 8$ )

5. Trigger to advance:

a. Advance to a transition state (deterioration of vital signs) occurs at 5 minutes from start of scenario. The following are expected to occur within the first minutes of the scenario:

i. Intraosseous access obtained
ii. Appropriate clinical assessments are performed which may (significantly) include hyperoxia test, four extremity blood pressures
iii. Laboratory tests, chest radiography and electrocardiogram are ordered (no results offered until within the Transition State)
iv. Differential diagnosis is proposed and discussed among team members and commitment is made to a treatment strategy
v. Communication with parent. Assessment and tentative plan explained.

# c. Transition state

i. Vital signs: Begin trend to increase heart and respiration rates and decrease oxygen saturation over a five minute period (heart rate increases by 33 beats per minute, respiration rate increases by 11 breaths per minute, systolic and diastolic blood pressure decreases by $6 \ : \mathrm { m m H } \mathrm { g } .$ , and oxygen saturation decreases by $2 { - } 4 \%$ ). At end of five minute transition, heart rate will be as high as 243, respiration rate as high as 81, oxygen saturation as low as $8 1 \%$ .

ii. Exam: work of breathing appears to increase (infant is now grunting).

iii. Diagnostic test results return Electrocardiogram findings: sinus tachycardia, right atrial enlargement, and right ventricular hypertrophy Chest radiograph findings: pulmonary edema, cardiomegaly Hyperoxia test (bedside): oxygen by face mask does not improve saturation; if formal blood gas obtained, oxygen administration does not improve hypoxemia

Laboratory, imaging, and electrocardiographic findings are provided for interpretation

1. Blood gas: uncompensated respiratory acidosis with metabolic acidosis; hyperoxia test suggests cardiac etiology (repeat blood gas relatively unchanged after oxygen administration)
2. Hyponatremia due to water retention
3. Elevated serum lactate and decreased serum bicarbonate due to tissue hypoxia
4. Elevated blood urea nitrogen due to decreased intravascular volume and dehydration
5. Chest plain film radiography demonstrates cardiomegaly, increased pulmonary vascular markings, and pulmonary edema suggestive of congestive heart failure or pulmonary overcirculation
6. Electrocardiogram demonstrates sinus tachycardia, right atrial enlargement, and right ventricular hypertrophy

# iv. Expected behaviors/actions:

1. Reassessment of vital signs and work of breathing
2. Analysis of laboratory data, chest radiograph, electrocardiogram
3. Team communicates revised differential diagnosis and treatment plan
4. Preparation of rapid sequence intubation medications and endotracheal intubation due to increased work of breathing, hypoxia, and deterioration (grunting, worsening vital signs, less active)

5. Recognition of pulmonary edema on chest plain radiograph and atrial enlargement on electrocardiogram and hyperoxia test results, in conjunction with physical examination findings, that all suggest Congenital Heart Disease

# v. Response to treatment:

1. Oxygen saturation and heart rate improve somewhat after intubation

vi. Trigger to advance: endotracheal intubation, furosemide, and pediatric cardiology consultation, $+ / -$ vasoactive agent, $+ / -$ positive inotrope.

i. Vital signs: Interval improvement from Transition State. Heart rate decreases to 180 beats per minute, respiration rate is controlled by mechanical ventilator (patient paralyzed and sedated), oxygen saturation increased to $9 0 \%$ with positive pressure ventilation.

# XII. Instructor’s Notes

a. Tips to keep scenario flowing:

i. Chest radiography and electrocardiography will not require a technician to be present or the actual physical act of obtaining these studies. After they are requested and the scenario is set to advance, the nurse will present the results to the participant during the Transition State

b. Tips to direct actors:

i. If participants do not verbalize their differential diagnosis or plan or their interpretation of the data, the nurse actor may ask “what do you think is going on?” as a stimulus
ii. If audio from the manikin is compromised during the scenario and a cue for deterioration (e.g. grunting) is lost, an actor (nurse) may provide a stimulus to trigger reassessment of the patient (“Does he look worse to you?”) to keep the scenario moving forward, only as needed

iii. Pediatric Cardiology consultant: Consultation is by telephone. Consultant will require the participant to present a focused history, including testing and treatments in the ED, as well as the reason/question(s) for the consultation

c. Scenario programming:

i. The transition/patient deterioration trend begins 5 minutes from the start of the scenario. There is no pathway that will result in the death of the patient.

ii. When appropriate cardiac medication therapy is ordered by a participant, the operator may select “Appropriate Therapy” from within the program, which will result in an improvement in vital signs.

iii. Chest radiography and electrocardiogram results are delayed until the Transition State

iv. If an echocardiogram or bedside ultrasound is requested, the nurse will say that she/he will call for one and will tell participants after calling that “it may take about 15-20 minutes to get here”

v. The participants may administer empiric antibiotics, but this will not harm or help the patient in the scenario.

vi. Alternative treatments, such as prostaglandins in consideration of a ductal dependent lesion, if inappropriately administered by the participants, will not improve or harm the patient

vii. An initial single intravenous saline bolus in response to tachycardia and hypotension does not improve patient, but also does not result in deterioration. $> 2 0 \mathrm { c c } / \mathrm { k g }$ will cause worsening hypoxia and accelerated deterioration to transition state

viii. Vasoactive or inotropic agent may not be administered until the very end of the case (after cardiology consultation) depending upon the learners’ initiative, but it must be administered prior to the case concluding formally

# XIV. Pilot Testing and Revisions

a. This simulation case has been run twice at our institution. On each occasion, two Emergency Medicine residents (a PGY3 and PGY2 team for one occasion, and a PGY2 and PGY1 team for the other) were active participants. The senior resident coordinated the resuscitation effort and as a team, the senior and junior residents gathered the HPI and ROS, performed the physical examination, formulated a differential diagnosis and plan, ordered therapy, and performed endotracheal intubation. An actual Emergency Medicine nurse played the role of the nurse and a Pediatric Emergency Medicine attending physician played the role of an assistant nurse. The role of the parent was played by a Pediatric Emergency Medicine attending physician. The lead author, via telephone communication from the control room, played the role of the Cardiology consultant. A group of 10 additional learners (2 medical students on their Emergency Medicine clerkship rotation, 7 Emergency Medicine residents, and a physician assistant) watched the simulation in real time. The instructors were the same for both occasions. The lead instructor who led the debriefing was the lead author of the simulation case, a PGY4 Emergency Medicine Resident. Additional instruction was provided by the two Pediatric Emergency Medicine attending physicians who participated in the case, one of whom is a coauthor, and two Adult Emergency Medicine attending physicians. A modification to the case debriefing included detailed feedback on teamwork in resuscitation. The role of the Adult Emergency Medicine instructors was to focus on and analyze the case from the perspective of effective teamwork and provide this feedback to help improve clarity of communication and efficiency. An additional modification made from the original design of the case was to add a “nurse trainee/assistant nurse” to the scenario who wore a headset and was able to receive communication from the manikin control room. When the learners requested a STAT bedside echocardiogram, it was this assistant nurse who relayed information from listeners in the control room that ultrasound was unavailable at that time. A single nurse actor can also facilitate this role, as in the original case design.

b. Participants were expected to identify the case as a presentation of a congenital heart disease (CHD), but were not expected to specifically diagnose TAPVC. On both occasions the learners had CHD at the top of their differential diagnosis, with additional concern and empiric treatment for sepsis.

c. It is expected that participants may respond to hypotension and tachycardia with an intravenous fluid bolus. If only one bolus is administered this will not cause rapid deterioration or worsening pulmonary edema. A second bolus, however, may prompt the operator to manually alter vital signs to demonstrate clinical worsening. On both occasions each team administered a single $2 0 \mathrm { c c / k g }$ normal saline bolus as an initial action of resuscitation. Neither team in the pilot cases ordered a second bolus. Both teams initiated positive pressure ventilation as a therapy. Neither team initiated a diuretic or inotrope independently. These therapies were initiated after discussion with the cardiology consultant (phone conversation with the lead author playing role of cardiologist). Both cases ended after the junior resident called to the PICU and relayed a detailed sign out of the case.
