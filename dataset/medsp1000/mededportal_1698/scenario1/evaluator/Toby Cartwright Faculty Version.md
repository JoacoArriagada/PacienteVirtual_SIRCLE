<u>**1:20-1:40 SUBGROUP 1:**</u>

You will teach your group pertinent information about duodenal atresia (DA) as a possible cause of this infant’s presentation. Please cover the following key points in 15 minutes or less:

1. Definition of DA and the anatomic site at which it occurs, noting frequency of occurrence and gender predominance. **DA is a congenital obstruction of the second part of the duodenum. It occurs in about 1/5000-10,000 births, more commonly in males, and is associated with Down syndrome and congenital heart disease.**
2. Embryologic cause of this malformation—what type of tissue is involved? Are there different forms of this malformation? When does this abnormal development occur? **The cause is likely a failure of recanalization of this bowel segment (endoderm component) during the early gestational stage. Two types of this malformation predominate: either a membranous obstruction, or a true interruption of the lumen of the bowel, usually at the location of the major duodenal papilla (papilla of Vater). In 80% of patients, the ducts draining at the papilla empty proximal to the obstruction, which is why the character of the emesis in this disorder is usually bilious. This failure of recanalization takes place toward the end of the second month of gestation.**
3. Symptoms or signs that may be noted prenatally with this malformation. **Polyhydramnios (excessive amniotic fluid) is noted during pregnancies that are complicated by fetal bowel obstruction. Since the normal fetus swallows amniotic fluid, inability of the fetus either to swallow or retain and process the swallowed fluid results in excessive amniotic fluid. It is important to note here that polyhydramnios is not specific or diagnostic for fetal bowel obstruction, but may be associated with an array of other developmental problems or fetal malformations, as well as with completely normal fetal life.**
4. Associated syndromes to consider when duodenal atresia is diagnosed. **Down syndrome and congenital heart defects, as outlined above. DA may also be present as a feature of multiple other rare chromosomal disorders or congenital malformations that will not be discussed here.**
5. Typical clinical patient presentation in duodenal atresia including symptoms, onset, age, physical exam findings. **A newborn with DA will not be able to keep down any food, with emesis usually occurring within 30 minutes of feeding including the first feeding of life. The emesis is likely to be bilious, as discussed above. The stomach may become dilated, but diffuse abdominal distention usually is not a feature.**
6. Recommended method of diagnosing suspected duodenal atresia. **Plain X-ray films of the abdomen may be sufficient for diagnosis if the characteristic “double bubble” sign is present (air in the stomach and the proximal duodenum, separated by the pylorus). If the plain films are not compelling enough to make a diagnosis, upper GI with oral contrast agent is indicated to evaluate for DA versus other disorders that require emergent intervention (see malrotation below). Failure of the contrast agent to progress past the obstruction is diagnostic.**
7. Typical plain X-ray findings in duodenal atresia (please find and bring examples—a laptop is required to show what you find). **As above. A good teaching film also may be found at**

[**http://www.e-radiography.net/radpath/d/duodenal\_atresia.htm**](http://www.e-radiography.net/radpath/d/duodenal_atresia.htm)

1. Describe initial stabilizing treatment versus definitive surgical treatment and prognosis in duodenal atresia. Please address the acceptable time recommended from the time of diagnosis to surgical treatment in this discussion. What is the prognosis for this disorder? **The prognosis for DA is generally very favorable depending, of course, on whether other congenital abnormalities exist. Initial stabilizing treatment focuses on relieving gastric and duodenal distention by placing a nasogastric tube to low wall suction and providing intravenous fluid support as needed until surgery. Surgery may be delayed for as long as 24 - 48 hours following the diagnosis to allow for transport to a pediatric surgeon and adequate fluid resuscitation.**

<u>**1:40-2:00 SUBGROUP 2:**</u>

You will teach your group pertinent information about midgut malrotation and volvulus (MMRV) as a possible cause of this infant’s presentation. Please cover the following key points in 15 minutes or less:

1. Definition of MMRV and anatomic area involved. **MMRV denotes two separate problems. The first, malrotation, denotes the failure of the midgut to rotate a full 270º as it herniates into the umbilical cord and returns to the abdominal cavity. This disrupts the normal placement of the small and large bowel in the abdomen and results in suspension of the entire midgut around a narrow axis, the origin of superior mesenteric vessels. The bowel can still function normally, and malrotation without volvulus is asymptomatic. Volvulus describes an intra-abdominal catastrophe that occurs as a result of malrotation. The mobile and improperly positioned bowel can twist repeatedy on the narrow axis of the superior mesenteric vessels and occlude vascular supply and drainage for the midgut portion of the bowel (from the middle of the duodenum through approximately the splenic flexure of the colon). Midgut volvulus does not occur in the absence of malrotation, but malrotation can occur and be undetected unless volvulus occurs.**
2. Embryologic cause of this malformation: please provide a visual illustration of the pertinent concepts discussed here. When is the normal sequence of events related to this malformation complete? When does this abnormal development occur? **During the 6****th** **week of embryonic life (8****th** **week of gestation by obstetric convention), the developing intestine becomes too large to stay in the abdominal cavity and herniates into the umbilical celom within the umbilical cord. As it grows outside the embryo, the intestinal loop rotates counterclockwise about 90º. The bowel returns to the abdominal cavity and completes another 180º of rotation in the same counterclockwise rotation for a total of 270º by the 10****th** **week of embryonic life (12****th** **week of gestational age.)**

**In malrotation, the bowel does not rotate the full 270º, resulting in misplacement of bowel within the abdomen. The cecum is placed in the right midline instead of the right lower quadrant and the mobile bowel is suspended by the narrow axis of its vascular supply as described above.**

1. Typical clinical patient presentation in MMRV: symptoms, onset, age, physical exam findings. **Volvulus can occur at any age in a patient with malrotation, but the classic patient description is that of an infant who presents during the first week of life with feeding intolerance and bilious emesis. No physical exam findings are characteristic of this disorder, and the presence or absence of abdominal distention should not be used to rule this disorder in or out. As the bowel becomes more ischemic, patients will become critically ill with resultant metabolic acidosis and hemodynamic instability, or evidence of bowel perforation.**
2. Recommended method of diagnosing suspected MMRV: this may include newly emerging methods as well as the standard approach to diagnosis. **The traditional approach to diagnosis of MMRV is an upper GI with oral contrast, which would show obstruction at the second portion of the duodenum, with failure of the duodenal “C” loop to cross the midline. A “corkscrew” pattern of bowel may also be seen, indicating the twisting of the bowel around its vascular bed. Ultrasound is also emerging as a method of diagnosis, because it may reveal malposition of the mesenteric vessels as seen in malrotation, but debate continues about use of this modality, because malposition of the vessels is diagnostic of malrotation, but not volvulus.**
3. Typical upper GI findings in MMRV (please find and bring examples: a laptop is required to show what you find). **As above - if students have not found a useful site, one can be found by clicking the link below; it also has a great explanation of the abnormal findings that I will not reiterate here:**

[http://www.learningradiology.com/archives03/COW%20054-midgut%20volvulus/malrotationcorrect.htm](http://www.learningradiology.com/archives03/COW%20054-midgut%20volvulus/malrotationcorrect.htm)

1. Initial stabilizing treatment versus definitive surgical treatment for MMRV. What are the general actions performed at the time of surgery? Is the surgery performed at a single time or in stages? **MMRV is a medical emergency with an overall mortality rate of 28% in one retrospective series. Nasogastric tube placement with suction to relieve luminal distention, aggressive fluid resuscitation and support of hemodynamic status should occur simultaneously with immediate transport of the patient to a pediatric surgeon. At the initial surgery, a fibrous band overlying the bowel (Ladd’s band) is divided and the malpositioned bowel carefully unwound and placed in its proper position, followed by closure of the incision. About 24 hours later, the abdomen is reopened to look for viable versus necrotic bowel. This staged approach allows salvage of as much viable bowel as possible to try to prevent short-gut syndrome. Necrotic bowel is resected and an enterostomy is created, with eventual re-anastomosis of the bowel at a later time.**
2. Please address the acceptable time recommended from the time of diagnosis to surgical treatment in this discussion. Does efficiency of diagnosis affect the prognosis for this disorder? **Because this is a true emergency, minutes may make the difference between survival or necrosis of bowel. Delay of surgery is never an acceptable option for patients with volvulus. Prompt imaging, diagnosis, and transport of these patients are essential for their survival and to avoid the disastrous consequences of a TPN-dependent short-gut patient. Volvulus is the reason for the urgent triage and evaluation of any infant who presents with bilious emesis.**

<u>**2:00-2:20 SUBGROUP 3:**</u>

You will teach your group pertinent information about jejunoileal atresia (JIA) as a possible cause of this infant’s presentation. Please cover the following key points in 15 minutes or less:

1. Definition of JIA and anatomic site that it occurs, noting subtypes of this malformation if applicable. **JIA is interruption of the lumen of the bowel anywhere from the origin of the jejunum to the terminal ileum. Four subtypes of this malformation exist: membranous (type I), interrupted (type II), discontinuous including apple-peel atresia (Type III) and multiple (Type IV.) Membranous indicates that only a thin membrane separates otherwise normal portions of bowel. Interrupted type describes the complete lack of a segment of bowel, with normal appearing bowel proximally and distally and normal mesentery. Discontinuous describes complete lack of bowel continuity, with a mesenteric gap associated with the atretic segments. Apple-peel may be a type discussed by students; it refers to very small caliber atretic bowel that is coiled around its mesentery, thus resembling a continuous strip of apple peel and inspiring the name. Multiple type describes JIA with multiple affected segments.**
2. Embryologic cause of this malformation; how does the mechanism differ from duodenal atresia? When does this malformation occur? **The mechanism of this malformation is associated with disruption of vascular supply to the bowel during intrauterine life and resultant infarction and resorption of the bowel. JIA may be a result of intrauterine volvulus, intussusception, or internal hernia. The vascular disruption may occur at any point during intrauterine life.**
3. Describe the typical clinical patient presentation in JIA: symptoms, onset, age, physical exam findings. **Abdominal distention and bilious emesis typically develop during the first 24 hours of life. Initial feedings may be tolerated, but the more proximal the obstruction, the earlier emesis develops. The presence of abdominal distention is indicative of obstruction more distally than the first two bowel obstructions discussed today.**
4. Describe the recommended method for diagnosing suspected JIA. **Plain films in patients with JIA show markedly dilated loops of bowel with air-fluid levels, with otherwise normal bowel positioning. If these characteristic findings are present, a contrast study may not be necessary. If a contrast study is performed, it may be a barium enema or an upper GI. Passage of the contrast medium to the level of the obstruction with interruption of flow is diagnostic of the obstruction, although the entire bowel must still be examined at the time of surgery to eliminate the possibility of multiple atresias.**
5. Typical X-ray findings in JIA (please find and bring examples - a laptop is required to show what you find). **Three helpful films with brief explanations are available at:**

[http://www.virtualpediatrichospital.org/providers/CAP/Case23/Case23.shtml](http://www.virtualpediatrichospital.org/providers/CAP/Case23/Case23.shtml)

1. Initial stabilizing treatment versus definitive surgical treatment and prognosis in JIA. Please address the acceptable time recommended from the time of diagnosis to surgical treatment in this discussion. What is the prognosis for this disorder? **Bowel decompression via NG tube placement and necessary fluid or electrolyte resuscitation should occur prior to surgery. A 12-24 hour period prior to operative repair is acceptable, because the bowel involved is not ischemic. The details of surgery depend on the type of atresia present, and the prognosis is generally good unless large areas of bowel were affected and little useful short bowel remains.**

<u>**2:20-2:40 SUBGROUP 4:**</u>

You will teach your group pertinent information about Hirschprung’s disease (HD) as a possible cause of this infant’s presentation. Please cover the following key points in 15 minutes or less:

1. Definition of HD and anatomic site that it occurs, noting frequency of occurrence, gender predominance, and subtypes of this malformation if applicable. **HD, also called congenital aganglionic megacolon, occurs in 1/400 live births. It is responsible for up to 25% of neonatal bowel obstruction. It is four times more common in males, and is associated with Down syndrome. It usually affects the transition zone of the rectosigmoid colon for a short segment, although longer segments of the bowel can be affected and it can occur in other portions of the bowel.**
2. Embryologic cause of this malformation; what type of tissue is involved? When does the malformation occur? **HD results from a failure of neural crest cells to migrate into the wall of the segment of bowel distal to the portion that is dilated. This results in failure to develop the normal parasympathetic innervation to the affected segment of colon. Auerbach’s plexus (intermuscular plexus) and Meissner’s plexus (submucosal plexus) are required for normal peristalsis. Interruption of this innervation results in bowel that cannot relax and dilate. This results in dilated bowel proximal to theaffected bowel, with small caliber bowel in the area that lacks innervation.**
3. What other syndrome is commonly associated with HD? **Down syndrome**
4. Describe a typical clinical patient presentation in HD: symptoms, onset, age, physical exam findings. **The presentation in HD can be variable, because the length of bowel involved is a large factor in the severity of the presentation. Short-segment HD can present as refractory constipation that requires enema administration to facilitate stool passage The diagnosis may be missed until children are school age or older unless the clinican is suspicious for this disorder. Longer segment Hirschprung’s presents early in infancy, with emesis and failure to pass a meconium stool within the first day of life. The abdomen may be distended, with palpable stool on exam. The anal sphincter is tight on examination, with palpable stool in the rectal vault if the defect is limited to the anal verge.**
5. What complications are associated with HD? **HD with significant stool retention/failure to pass stool in the neonatal period can be associated with toxic megacolon and necrotizing enterocolitis. In addition, if the colon becomes distended enough, perforation and peritonitis can occur. These are the major life-threatening complications of HD.**
6. Recommended method(s) of diagnosing suspected HD. **Anal manometry or contrast enema are two important tools for diagnosing HD. In anal manometry, the sphincter tone stays abnormally high without the reflexive relaxation that normally occurs in response to distention of the rectum. (absent anosphincteric reflex). Contrast enema shows either narrowing of the anal verge in short-segment HD or distended colon proximal to the aganglionic segment with empty, small caliber colon including and distal to the aganglionic segment.**

1. Initial stabilizing treatment versus definitive surgical treatment and prognosis in HD. Please address the acceptable time recommended from the time of diagnosis to surgical treatment in this discussion. What is the prognosis for this disorder? **Other than the complications discussed above, HD has a favorable prognosis that is influenced by the amount of colon involved. When diagnosed, bowel cleanout with enemas is undertaken prior to surgical repair. The aganglionic segment is resected and the bowel is re-anastomosed if possible. The time until surgery is not critically important if bowel distention is relieved and none of the above complications are present.**
