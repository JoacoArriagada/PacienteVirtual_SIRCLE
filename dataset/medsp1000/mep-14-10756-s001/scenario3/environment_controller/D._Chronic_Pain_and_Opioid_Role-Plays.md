CHRONIC PAIN AND OPIOID ROLE PLAYS

**Case 2: Discussion of Monitoring for Risk/Safety**

It’s Friday afternoon. The patient, new to the practice, is a 32-year-old M who tells you that he has been struggling with severe lower back pain which began after a motor vehicle accident 5 years prior. When you ask about his function, the patient states that he has been unable to work and is now on disability. He states that he cannot stand or walk for more than 5-10 minutes at a time before having to sit and that is fiancée assists him with cooking and cleaning his home. When you ask about sleep he tells you that he sleeps a few hours a night, wakes up with pain, takes pain medicine and then goes back to sleep for a few more hours. The patient states that he has been taking hydrocodone 5 mg/APAP 325 mg “for years” for the pain and that it isn’t helping. He states that his previous PCP office “shut down.” When you ask what else he has tried to help with the pain he replies that he did 8 sessions of physical therapy, which might have helped a little, but his insurance would not cover any more sessions. His musculoskeletal and neurologic examinations, while slow, are normal.

The participant playing the role of the physician/provider should attempt to discuss how the practice monitors the risks of OPM including: obtaining previous medical records for review and using UDS and PDMP as risk monitoring tools. The participant playing the role of patient should be instructed to be anxious to get his OPM refilled and angry that the physician/provider “doesn’t believe him.”

Doctor: Mr. Smith I want to take a few minutes to discuss how I myself and the other doctors in my practice handle opioid prescriptions. As we discussed these medications have a lot of risks so it’s important that I monitor them so we can keep you safe.

Patient: Whatever…I am not an addict and I don’t take any other drugs or drink.

Doctor: I understand, but I need to explain our practice policy to everyone. Because taking more of these medications than I prescribe for you is a risk for unintentional overdose and death it’s our policy that you get opioid prescriptions only from me or one of my partners. The way that we monitor that is by regularly checking our state’s prescription monitoring system. I can access on-line all prescriptions that you receive for opioids and other controlled substances. If a patient gets prescriptions from another doctor we can’t continue to safely prescribe them. The other important way we monitor the safety of these medications is asking you to do urine drug screens at each visit. If we find someone is taking other drugs that can increase the risk of these medications, like cocaine or heroin, then we can’t continue to prescribe them.

Patient: I told you I’m not an addict.

Doctor: As I mentioned I discuss our practice policy with everyone who we prescribe these medications to so that I can keep people safe. Are you agreeable to our policy?

Patient: You know, I’m just so tired of people not believing I’m not in pain and not trusting me.

Doctor: Mr. Smith, it’s not about believing you are having pain, I believe it. It’s about the safety of these types of medications and keeping you safe. That’s my number one obligation as a physician.

Patient: (pouting) Okay.
