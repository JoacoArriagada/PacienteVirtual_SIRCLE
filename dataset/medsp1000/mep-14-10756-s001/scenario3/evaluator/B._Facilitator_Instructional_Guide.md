**APPENDIX B: EFFECTIVE CHRONIC PAIN MANAGEMENT AND RESPONSIBLE OPIOID PRESCRIBING FACILITATOR/INSTRUCTIONAL GUIDE**

**Effective Chronic Pain Management and Responsible Opioid Prescribing – Facilitator/Instructional Guide**

**Overall Goals:**

The overall goals for our workshop are to review best practices in chronic pain management and principles of responsible opioid prescribing, discuss implementation strategies, and enhance communication with patients. The workshop needs one facilitator for each small group of 5.

**Workshop objectives:**

1. Identify best practices in chronic pain management including non-opioid and non-pharmacologic treatments.
2. Develop strategies to efficiently and effectively implement principles of responsible opioid use in practice.
3. Apply patient-centered communication skills when treating patients with chronic pain.

**Slide 24:** Introduce monitoring strategies for risk of opioid misuse and abuse. Using the risk-benefit framework, the first step before considering a trial of OPM for a patient with chronic pain is to assess him/her for risk of OUD. There are several available screening tools, but we use the opioid-risk tool (ORT) due to it’s ease in the primary care setting. While pre-adolescent sexual abuse is listed as a risk only for women, it is likely that it is a significant risk for men as well, it has just not been studied to a similar degree. It is also important to remember that active tobacco use counts as a personal history of substance-abuse. Patients who score in the high-risk category should not be prescribed OPM and referral to addiction and/or pain medicine specialist should be considered. Patients who are at intermediate risk should have risk monitoring more often than the those at low risk.

**Slide 25:** Patients should be clearly informed of the practice policy for monitoring the risk/safety of OPM. Regularly checking the state’s prescription drug monitoring program (PDMP) allows monitoring to ensure the patient is obtaining prescriptions from only one physician (“getting additional prescriptions from other doctors will increase the chance of you dying from an unintended overdose. If that happens I cannot safely prescribe you OPM any longer.”). Urine drug screens (UDS) are also important in monitoring for risk in patients on OPM. Patients should be informed how often you will check UDS and asked, “Will the results show anything unexpected?” before administration. Patients should also be clearly informed of the policy of acting on UDS results, e.g. “If your urine shows that you are taking other drugs that could increase the risk of overdose and death, like tranquilizers or opioids that aren’t prescribed, I will no longer be able to prescribe OPM for you because the risk is too high.” Other risk monitoring strategies include the patient-provider agreement, which should act as an informed consent document, regular follow-up visits, and enlisting the patient in monitoring for signs of opioid misuse/OUD.

**Slide 29:** State prescription drug monitoring programs are an essential component of monitoring risk of OPM. 49 states and the District of Columbia have a well established PDMP that can be accessed by providers. The state of Missouri’s “PDMP” is only accessible by government entities and not by providers. Many states have or will soon have laws requiring providers to check the PDMP regularly for patients they prescribe OPM. Patients who are prescribed OPM should be informed that the provider will regularly be checking the PDMP to monitor risk of the medications and informed that if it reveals multiple prescribers that it is very likely that risk of the medication outweighs any potential benefit.

**Slide 30:** All patients prescribed OPM should have a patient-provider agreement. These documents should serve as an informed consent document outlining potential benefit and all risks/safety issues of OPM. They should also include required risk monitoring practices, what behaviors will result in opioid discontinuation, appropriate storage and disposal of opioids, and a clear exit strategy. These documents should provide a basis for patient discussion and should be kept in the electronic medical record.

**Slide 34:** Interactive question 6; A new patient presents with chronic pain and requests OPM. Answer: A. The patient should be informed that all patients with chronic pain require a complete assessment, including history, physical exam, and review of past records before a patient-centered chronic pain management plan can be developed. The patient should be informed that OPM is only prescribed for chronic pain in certain circumstances and not before all potentially available and effective non-opioid and non-pharmacologic treatments have been exhausted.
