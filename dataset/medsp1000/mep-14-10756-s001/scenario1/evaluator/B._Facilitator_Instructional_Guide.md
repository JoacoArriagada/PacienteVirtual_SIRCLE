**APPENDIX B: EFFECTIVE CHRONIC PAIN MANAGEMENT AND RESPONSIBLE OPIOID PRESCRIBING FACILITATOR/INSTRUCTIONAL GUIDE**

**Effective Chronic Pain Management and Responsible Opioid Prescribing – Facilitator/Instructional Guide**

**Overall Goals:**

The overall goals for our workshop are to review best practices in chronic pain management and principles of responsible opioid prescribing, discuss implementation strategies, and enhance communication with patients. The workshop needs one facilitator for each small group of 5.

**Workshop objectives:**

1. Identify best practices in chronic pain management including non-opioid and non-pharmacologic treatments.
2. Develop strategies to efficiently and effectively implement principles of responsible opioid use in practice.
3. Apply patient-centered communication skills when treating patients with chronic pain.

**Slide 10:** Chronic pain case 1 – used to discuss principles of appropriate pain management and responsible opioid prescribing.

**Slide 14:** Regarding assessment of chronic pain, numeric scores are not helpful. Rather, patients should be informed honestly and without leading to increased catastrophizing that chronic pain usually lasts for a prolonged period and it is unlikely that it will be completely relieved. The management goals for chronic pain should be improvement in function and quality of life. There are validated scores (e.g. PEG-3, PROMIS, BPI) that can be used to assess these, but it is often more effective to ask patients, “What do you really miss doing that you could do before the pain that you can no longer do? I want you to think about your life with your family and friends, your work, sleeping, your sex life, and your spiritual life.” The patient’s answers are often extremely revealing and can be used to help the patient set goals for pain management.

**Slide 15:** As is the case for other chronic diseases, patients often require our help to set SMART goals for chronic pain management. Based on the information that the patient discusses, suggestions can be made to the patient. For example, if a patient replies that she takes care of her grandchildren (as might be the situation in case 1) and wishes to be “more active” with them, a helpful response might be: “What does that look like for you?” Unrealistic or unachievable goals can be modified, for example: “I think being active with them for several hours in a row might be too much. How about playing with them for 10-15 minutes?” It should be explained to the patient that achievement/lack of progress of SMART goals will be the way you will together measure the effects of the treatment modalities.
