**PAT FERN**

**Case Summary**

Pat Fern is married, has three school age children, and works as an accountant. Pat Fern was referred to the psychiatry outpatient clinic by the primary care physician for an evaluation of possible depression. This psychiatry clinic is embedded in the primary care office, so the student has received the PCP’s referral note. Pat Fern went to the new PCP **a week** ago because Pat Fern felt depressed since running out of the old medicine when the previous physician retired.

**Presentation/emotional tone**

You are dressed in your street clothes and are seated on a chair. You should dress casually. You will sit on the edge of your seat; not smile; make eye contact most of the time. You are cooperative at first but can become irritable if you think the doctor is keeping you from your medicines; answering questions appropriately but interrupting sometimes and sighing occasionally.  You should perseverate (answer the same thing over and over, keep “harping on” the same topic) on these being “MY medicines,” and you should be irritable and indignant that the doctors aren’t giving them to you.  You feel that their not giving you the medicine is equivalent to their stealing it from you.  You resent that Dr. Smith retired because he was the best doctor you ever had.

Patient affect:

-You are irritable, prickly, anxious, guarded. You are not animated.

-You are also confused- “W*hy do I have to go through this now? It used to be so easy.”* 

-You are offended if the student insinuates you did something wrong.

-You are NOT hostile, aggressive or crying.

-You can be defensive, but <u>not hostile</u>, if the student implies you are an addict.

**Presenting complaint**

When the student asks you why you are in the clinic today, you answer:

**“I was referred to you by my new doctor.  He didn’t want to treat my depression and wanted me to see you.”**

If asked an additional open-ended question like “tell me about your depression” or “tell me why you went to your primary care doctor”:

**“I’ve been depressed but I was better when I saw Dr. Smith, my old doctor.  I just need you to put me back on my pills.”**

**Medical History**:

**About a month** ago you started feeling “not right,” just not like yourself.  You date the beginning of these symptoms to Dr. Smith’s retirement **1 month** ago. During this time you noticed a decrease in your appetite; food just wasn’t appealing. You noticed you cried more easily than usual. You haven’t had much energy. You have a hard time falling asleep and you wake up at night.  Since you aren’t getting enough sleep, you have a hard time getting up and taking care of your everyday tasks. Your spouse noticed that you “just aren’t yourself.” You’ve tried praying and you think that helps a little but you really haven’t felt like yourself.

You have three school age children and your spouse travels a lot so you are their primary caretaker. You feel you are really “letting the family down” because there are times you are so worried that you don’t want to go to work and you make the kids walk to school.

In addition to these problems, you are also concerned that your thinking has been “a little cloudy” lately. Since you ran out of medicine, you have noticed some problems with your memory.  For example, you tend to misplace things more frequently (e.g., your purse/wallet, your house keys, etc.). You have a hard time remembering phone numbers and some people’s names. Last week you forgot to pick up your daughter after soccer and a neighbor brought her home, which was very embarrassing. Eventually you do remember whatever you lost or forgot.

You were willing to come see the psychiatrist as your PCP suggested. If asked if your PCP prescribed you any medications for your symptoms, you angrily respond that you want to be on your old medicines. You are willing to take only your previous medications and don’t want to get psychotherapy. You are to remain resistant to change well into the encounter (8-10 minutes). You are frustrated, impatient, and a bit demanding.  You can still question them, challenge them, bargain with them, etc. to help them convince you to consider change. By the end of the encounter you will have reluctantly and skeptically agreed to try suggested medications. You are still resistant to psychotherapy <u>at this time</u>.

Dr. Smith prescribed the medications after you were in a car accident 10 years ago; you did not lose consciousness and you were not hospitalized.  *(You were involved in a rear end collision, someone hit you. There was no physical therapy)*. You had some anxiety about having another car accident after that, but your fears resolved in less than one month.  You have never had any other anxiety symptoms, except anxiety that you get your medicine back.  You had some neck pain after the accident, but that also resolved in less than one month.  You don’t have any physical or psychological symptoms or scars related to the accident.  You don’t know why Dr. Smith kept you on the medicine for so long, but you think it was to keep you from **being anxious** about the accident or in pain. It had never come up in conversation to ever get off the medicine, it just kept getting refilled.
