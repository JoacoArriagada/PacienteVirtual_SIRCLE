**APPENDIX B**

**Transitions in Care: Discussion Leader’s Guide**

**Vignette 1: Transition from Outpatient to Emergency Services**

This vignette describes a clinical encounter in which a provider realizes that an outpatient is at high risk for harm to self and requires transfer to emergency services. Guiding questions for group discussion are included after each vignette. The case and guiding questions are designed to elicit discussion of two critical areas: a) logistical steps of transfer, including legal considerations, supervision, and safety, and b) identification of critical information that needs to be transferred to the receiving care team. For the latter, use of a standardized handoff system (e.g. I-PASS) can be integrated if used by your training program or your program’s institution.

After 6-7 minutes for participants to discuss the vignette and guiding questions in small groups, the discussion leader should bring all participants together for further discussion and sharing of key points. Depending on the overall size of the group, this may be the small group re-convening for discussion with the addition of the workshop leader to guide the participants on identifying the key points, or it may be convening a collection of small groups into a large group to share impressions from each small group.

When beginning the large group discussion, it is recommended that the discussion leader first ask each small group to share the most salient learning points of the vignette discussion from their small group. This is likely to elicit many of the key points and permits the small groups to learn from each other. Thereafter, the discussion leader should guide the large group to answer each of the four guiding questions together, with the opportunity to share expertise and talk through differing opinions. This will likely draw out the majority of the remainder of any key points that were not highlighted during the first portion of the large group discussion.

The key points that should be highlighted over the course of the discussion are as follows:

**1)** **Logistical**: What steps should the outpatient provider take?

Key points to consider include:

* Is the trainee required to review a case like this with a supervisor immediately? Which supervisor, and how is s/he reached?
* Who, if anyone, should the outpatient provider call in the emergency room to provide a verbal handoff? How does the OP provider reach that person?
* Is public safety/security involved?
* How is the patient physically transported to the emergency room (e.g. ambulance, own transportation, etc)?
* Is nursing or administrative staff involved?
* Are there other contacts for the patient that should be alerted or involved (e.g. family or support agencies)?
* Is there any required paperwork? Does state legal paperwork (e.g. an involuntary hold form or involuntary transport form) need to be filled out by the provider?

*Example: In the authors’ system, the trainee provider would be expected to involve a faculty supervisor immediately. S/he would request help from the clinic front desk in calling an ambulance to transport the patient to the emergency room, which is at a different physical location from the outpatient clinic. S/he would enlist help from on-site clinic Public Safety officers to maintain safety and prevent elopement while waiting for the ambulance, and s/he would call a specific providers’ number for Psychiatric Emergency Services to provide a verbal handoff prior to the patient’s arrival to the ED. S/he would complete appropriate state legal paperwork to accompany the patient in the ambulance, but does not need to fill out any other forms. These details may vary from system to system and should be adapted for your own system’s procedures.*

2) **Communication and** **Information Transfer**: What information needs to be transferred? How should this happen?

This section should highlight any protocols or templates you want trainees to follow to ensure that sufficient information is transferred. Examples of details the authors consider critical to cover include the following:

* Patient name, age, gender
* Past diagnoses, including both relevant psychiatric and mental health diagnoses
* Past hospitalizations
* Brief summary of current issue of concern
* Current treatment including any current medication
* Safety: Any history of suicide attempts, aggression or homicidal ideation, or inability to care for self? Do you think they are at risk for elopement during the transport or in emergency services?
* Recommendations: Do you [the outpatient provider] think they need to be hospitalized? If you are not sure yet, this can also be shared with the Emergency Room staff.
* Any other highly pertinent medical, psychiatric, or social history, such as
  + - * useful PRNs
      * adverse medication reactions
      * past history of trauma or traumatic restraint
      * primary language of patient and any caregivers

*This portion of the discussion is also an excellent place to highlight I-PASS, SBAR, or other standardized approaches to handoffs, if your institution uses them. An example of how these key points could be highlighted if your system uses the I-PASS framework is as follows:*

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **I:** Illness Severity | *“I am calling to let you know about a patient with suicidal ideation that I’m very concerned about and think may require inpatient level of care.”* |
| **P:** Patient Summary | *“Jerry is a 19yo M with a developmental delay, history of depression and anxiety, no prior hospitalizations or suicide attempts, no history of aggression, whom I see for outpatient pharm management. He is currently treated with fluoxetine 40mg PO daily. He gets support from his family, whom he lives with and relies upon for help with iADLs, as well as his therapeutic school. Jerry is very attached to his teachers at school and today disclosed hopelessness and suicidal ideation, with vague thoughts of overdosing on acetaminophen but no known attempts, in the context of one of his teacher’s departure from his school.”* |
| **A:**Action list for next team | *“I was not able to complete a full safety assessment with collateral within the outpatient setting, so he will need a* ***full emergency psychiatric evaluation****. I* ***recommend making sure you get collateral*** *from his mother and his school.”* |
| **S:** Situation awareness/contingency planning and **Special needs\*** | *“Jerry is currently agreeable with the plan for PES evaluation and is coming voluntarily, but I am concerned enough about his safety that* ***if*** *he changes his mind,* ***then*** *he should be held involuntarily until full collateral can be obtained.* ***If*** *you ultimately decide he can be discharged,* ***then*** *he will need a safety plan regarding the acetaminophen and follow-up. Please page me so that I can schedule him for follow-up before the end of the week if he is discharged.”*  *“Jerry does have a history of paradoxical reaction to Benadryl, so* ***if*** *he does need medications for agitation,* ***then*** *I would recommend considering a low-dose benzodiazepine as an alternative.”*  *“Jerry’s primary language is Spanish, so an interpreter should be offered, though he usually declines this when seeing me.”* |
| **S:** Synthesis | *Clinician receiving the handoff repeats the critical information back to the trainee initiating the handoff.* |

*\* The authors incorporate “special needs” into the first “S” of the mnemonic, to remind trainees to include special needs of the patient that may not otherwise be as readily captured by the I-PASS structure, e.g. language needs, history of traumatic restraint experience, paranoia resulting in fear of public safety, etc.*

To wrap-up the large group discussion prior to the next vignette, the instructor should summarize key points from the large-group discussion either verbally or via use of a PowerPoint slide. For instructors who prefer the latter, the key points highlighted should include your institution’s specific procedures for the questions under (1) as well as the critical information that should be transferred under (2).

**Vignette 2: Transition from Emergency Services to Inpatient Medicine**

In this portion of the case, the patient is evaluated by emergency services, and it is discovered that he actually took an overdose of acetaminophen at home earlier in the day. He becomes symptomatic, and is admitted to the inpatient medical service for treatment after ED evaluation by both emergency medicine and emergency psychiatry. This case highlights communication between the psychiatric emergency services trainee and the emergency room physician, and subsequently between the psychiatric emergency services trainee and the inpatient medical team, consult-liaison team, and relevant supports for the patient (including the family and outpatient provider who sent the patient). Communication with the patient also happens during this portion of the vignette but is not the focus of this exercise.

As with Transition #1, the vignette should first be discussed in small groups; after small group discussion, small groups re-convene for large group discussion, with the discussion leader guiding discussion toward key points. Again, it is recommended that the discussion leader first ask each small group to share the most salient learning points for them in order to teach each other. After sharing of small groups’ learning points, the discussion leader should then lead the large group through a discussion of each of the guiding questions, eliciting and highlighting key points as they come up. Key points to highlight include the following:

**1)** **Logistical**

* Sharing care of a patient with both emergency medical and emergency behavioral health needs requires close and careful communication between the medical and psychiatric teams, even if both have personally evaluated the patient. Sometimes critical information is shared by the patient or family with the psychiatric team but not shared with the medical team, and vice versa. It is the responsibility of each to ensure all critical information is communicated to the other party. This is often best done *in person* rather than solely by a note in the patient’s chart.
* Depending on the system, multiple different parties may need to be involved in or notified of the admission. In the authors’ system, the psychiatric emergency services resident would provide a verbal handoff to inpatient medicine and inpatient consult-liaison colleagues, update the outpatient provider, and alert family of the disposition. Given the need for multiple communications, some systems may implement a checklist to ensure all necessary communication occurs; if such a checklist exists, this would be the time to highlight its existence and contents.
* Any other communication issues specific to the interface between emergency medicine, emergency psychiatry, inpatient medicine, and the outpatient psychiatrist within your own system should be highlighted here.

**2) Communication and Information Transfer**

* If disagreement arises, a trainee provider should directly involve his/her supervisor, if this has not already been done, who may be able to help the care team reach resolution on a supervisor-to-supervisor level.
* All communications between services should remain collegial and respectful, even when expressing differing opinions.
* What information should be included in the handoff to CL and inpatient medicine?
  + - * + Again, this may be system-specific but at a minimum will likely include the points already highlighted in Vignette #1, plus ED course, whether the patient needs a 1:1 sitter while on inpatient medicine, and how quickly he should be seen by CL.
* I-PASS, SBAR or any other handoff tools specific to your system may also be re-emphasized here.

**Transition 3: Inpatient Medicine to Inpatient Psychiatry**

This portion of the case follows the patient as he is medically treated and cleared, and a plan is made for transfer to inpatient psychiatry. This section of the evolving vignette emphasizes a grey area of this case that may affect transition between services: is this patient considered an adult or an adolescent? Should he be transferred to an adult or adolescent psychiatry bed? Though at an age in which he would commonly legally be considered an adult, he is developmentally delayed, is still in high school, and his parents are his legal guardians. This vignette prompts discussion of how these grey areas are handled within one’s own system, and may vary from system to system or state to state.

Key points should again be elicited via group discussion, similar to the process used for the first two transitions. Critical questions to answer together through discussion of this case include the following:

**1) Logistical**

* Can the patient consent to his own admission and treatment? If not, can his parents (his legal guardians) do so, or would other parties (e.g. court) need to become involved?
* In your system, would the patient be considered most appropriate for an adolescent or adult inpatient unit?
* With this “grey area” patient, which faculty supervisors should be involved in the trainee provider’s decision-making process? (Examples include Adult or Pediatric CL faculty, Adult and/or Child Inpatient Psychiatry faculty.)
* Who is your system’s internal legal counsel? How is s/he reached if areas of confusion regarding legal status and consent arise?
* What paperwork needs to be completed for transfer to the inpatient psychiatric setting?

**2) Communication and Information Transfer**

* Again, what information should be transferred from Dr. C to the receiving providers, and how should it be communicated?
  + - * This should include the previously highlighted items plus the patient’s inpatient medicine course, including successful interventions for de-escalation. Trainees may again practice this transfer via use of a standardized handoff system if that is your institution’s practice.

**Transition 4: Inpatient Psychiatry to Outpatient Psychiatry**

In this final vignette, the patient is treated on inpatient psychiatry and is ready for discharge after a one-week admission. He and his family are eager to return to his usual routine, though his school has some hesitation regarding his readiness to return. This vignette highlights inpatient-to-outpatient communication and opportunities for step-down in level of care. The instructor should lead small and large groups through discussion of the case and guiding questions in the same step-wise fashion as in the first three vignettes. Key points to be highlighted in discussion of this vignette include the following:

1. **Logistical**
   * Multiple options for stepdown may exist and help Jerry transition back to the community. The exact types of stepdown available will vary by community, but may include partial hospitalization or day programs, mobile crisis team visits, an intermediate (unlocked) inpatient transitional unit, or intensified outpatient services such as increasing frequency of visits, adding group therapy or more individual therapy, etc.
   * Communication with parties outside of the hospital system – in this case, Jerry’s school – will require a signed release of information by Jerry and/or his family. The exact details of this form and who may sign it will vary from state-to-state and institution-to-institution. Your institution’s procedures for this should be discussed here.
2. **Communication and Information Transfer**

- The inpatient team should communicate with the outpatient team early and often. Trainees working on their inpatient rotations may forget that that outpatient team knows the patient best and will have to follow up on any plans developed on the inpatient unit, and therefore should be involved in planning as much as possible.

- The content of information transferred from the outpatient team to the inpatient team should include:

* all pertinent past psychiatric history, including past medication history
* course of treatment
* patient’s baseline level of symptomatology and functioning
* all pertinent history of stressors and supports in the community
* anything that would be helpful for inpatient team to know to be better support the patient throughout admission (ex. most effective coping strategies)
* all pertinent legal/logistical issues (e.g. guardianship paperwork)

- The content of information transferred from the inpatient team to the outpatient team should include:

* the details already highlighted regarding the ED and inpatient course
* any newly gained information (e.g. new testing results or new information gathered from the patient or family)
* items to be followed up (e.g. new referral for additional community supports) after discharge
* discharge mental status and safety plan
* Ideally, communication form the inpatient team to the outpatient providers would happen both verbally and by sharing a discharge summary, presuming the outpatient team is part of the same care system as the inpatient team or a release has been signed.
* Again, standardized institutional handoff templates may be used here to practice the transfer of information, particularly the final transfer from inpatient to outpatient teams.
