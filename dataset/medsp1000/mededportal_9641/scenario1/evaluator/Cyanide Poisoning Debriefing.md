**Cyanide Poisoning: Debriefing Guidelines**

This debriefing is best performed as a group discussion immediately following the simulation. Suggested points for debriefing are summarized below.

**Pathophysiology**

Cyanide (CN) binds cytochrome oxidase a3 of the electron transport chain and inhibits the generation of ATP by oxidative phosphorylation. With aerobic respiration halted, the body increases its rate of anaerobic respiration, resulting in the development of an anion gap lactic acidosis. Ultimately, the cells' inability to utilize oxygen and generate ATP results in significant stress on the central nervous and cardiovascular systems. Thus, CN is dubbed a "cellular asphyxiant," meaning that it causes the failure of aerobic respiration and adequate energy generation in spite of a normal or high blood oxygen concentration.

**Clinical Presentation**

*History of Present Illness*

CN, though rare, should be considered in certain clinical scenarios. House fires, resulting in smoke inhalation from the combustion of wools, silks, plastics and polyurethane are the most common cause of CN poisoning in the United States. Additionally, CN has industrial uses such as in the electroplating of jewelry and photography development and is readily available to professionals in these fields. Third, CN poisoning should be considered in the medical setting following a prolonged infusion of sodium nitroprusside, an anti-hypertensive medication that contains cyanide moieties which are liberated over time.

*Physical Signs and Symptoms*

Initial neurological manifestations of CN poisoning include headache, dizziness, confusion and seizures. Cardiovascular findings vary depending on the stage of illness. Initially, the patient may exhibit hypertension with bradycardia but then develop hypotension with a reflex tachycardia. Severe poisoning may result in bradycardia, hypotension, and an array of dysrhythmias ranging from heart block to ventricular tachycardia. Gastrointestinal symptoms are nonspecific and include abdominal pain and vomiting. Dermatologic examination may be notable for a "cherry-red" skin color. This classic finding differs from the "cyanosis" or bluish discoloration often expected by providers from the name "cyanide." It reflects the elevated venous oxygen concentrations due to cellular inability to extract and utilize oxygen.

*Laboratory evaluation*

A metabolic acidosis (often severe) and an elevated lactate are suggestive of CN poisoning in the right clinical scenario. A plasma lactate > 10mmol/L in the setting of smoke inhalation or >6 mmol/L in the setting of a cyanide salt ingestion should be concerning for significant cyanide exposures.

Additionally, a reduced difference (<10%) in the oxygen saturations of an arterial blood gas and a venous blood gas is suggested of cyanide poisoning.

**Antidotes**

*Hydroxocobalamin*

Hydroxocobalamin is the first-line therapy for cyanide poisoning and should be administered promptly when the diagnosis is suspected. It is a vitamin B12 precursor with a central cobalt moiety that exhibits a higher affinity for cyanide than does mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase. Thus, it binds the cyanide, enabling cellular respiration, and forming cyanocobalamin (vitamin B12.)

Side effects include transient hypertension (often welcomed in these hypotensive poisoned patients) as well as a reddish discoloration of body fluids. Some colorimetric laboratory tests may be affected by the discoloration.

Dose: 5g IV over 15 min or bolus in cardiac arrest (Peds 70mg/kg, max 5g IV)

*Sodium Thiosulfate*

Sodium thiosulfate donates a sulfur moiety to increase the rate of endogenous cyanide detoxification by the enzyme rhodanese. The resulting thiocyanate is nontoxic and cleared by the kidneys. Sodium thiosulfate also enhances our ability to use our endogenous mechanism for cyanide detoxification via the rhodanese pathway.

Dose of Sodium Thiosulfate 25% solution (250 mg/mL): 50 mL (12.5g) IV over 10-30 min (Peds 0.5 g/kg or 2mL/kg of 25% solution not to exceed 12.5 g)4

*Amyl Nitrate and Sodium Nitrite*

Both amyl nitrate and sodium nitrite are used to generate a methemoglobinemia such that the methemoglobin will draw cyanide off of cytochrome oxidase and restore aerobic respiration. These should be used with caution in smoke inhalation patients who already have decreased oxygen carrying capacity due to carbon monoxide toxicity. Amyl nitrate comes in pearls that are broken and inhaled as a temporary measure while intravenous access is obtained. Side effects include hypotension.

Dose of Sodium Nitrite 3% (30mg/mL): 10ml (300 mg) IV over 2-4 min3

(Peds 0.2mL/kg of 3% solution not to exceed 300 mg.)3.

It should be stressed to participants that hydroxocobalamin is the first line antidote for cyanide poisoning. It has few and relatively minor side effects and is the most effective at removing CN from cytochrome oxidase and restoring normal cellular respiration.

Amyl nitrate and/or sodium nitrate should only be administered if hydroxocobalamin is not available. These antidotes are not as effective at detoxifying cyanide and run the risk for generating excessive methemoglobin and further reducing the oxygen carrying capacity in a patient who may already have some carbon monoxide toxicity due to fire exposure.

**Take home points:**

1. Suspect CN poisoning in smoke inhalation victims, especially patients who have altered mental status or are hypotensive.
2. Lactate >10 is highly suggestive of the diagnosis
3. Hydroxocobalamin is the first line treatment for CN poisoning.
