**Atrial fibrillation-dehydration simulation case debrief notes**

Organized by objective

* **Identifying rapid atrial fibrillation**
* EKG findings:
* Irregularly, irregular rhythm
* Absence of discrete P waves
* Irregular QRS complexes
* Ashman phenomenon: wide QRS following long-short R-R cycles
* May see bundle-branch block
* **Considering the broad spectrum of symptoms atrial fibrillation may present with**
* More commonly seen symptoms:
* Fatigue/weakness
* Shortness of breath
* Palpitations
* Dizziness/lightheadedness
* Exercise intolerance
* Less commonly seen symptoms:
* Chest pain
* Increased urination (likely due to decreased sodium reabsorption)
* Edema (decompensated congestive heart failure)
* **Understanding the management of rapid atrial fibrillation**
* Main components to consider: rate control, rhythm control, anticoagulation
* Rate versus rhythm control strategy (after sinus rhythm has been achieved; outpatient considerations):
* Rate control usually improves presenting symptoms.
* The decision of which strategy to adopt often based on the presence of symptoms, the degree of LV function impairment caused by the arrhythmia, comorbidities
* Rate control:
* Medications that show AV nodal conduction: β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, digoxin
* Amiodarone is usually used when the arrhythmia is refractory to the other agents
* Rhythm control:
* Electrical cardioversion
* Antiarrhthmic medications, ablation and/or surgical procedure
* Anticoagulation:
* Many patients with more than one episode of atrial fibrillation require anticoagulation.
* For new-onset atrial fibrillation, a duration of greater than 48 hours increases the risk of embolization.
* Anticoagulation is recommended for these patients if benefits outweigh risks if cardioversion is planned.
* CHADS2 score:
* Clinical decision rule that can be used to determine which patients benefit from anticoagulation.
* Congestive heart failure
* Hypertension (140/90 mmHg or on treatment)
* Age ≥ 75
* Diabetes
* Prior Stroke or TIA or Thromboembolism
* Any of the first four criteria is given a score of 1 and the last criteria receives a score of 2
* Patients with a score of 0 do not need to be anti-coagulated; may or may not receive aspirin
* Patients with a score of 1 should be started on aspirin or anticoagulation; risks should be weighed
* Patients with a score of 2 or higher should be anticoagulated (unless contraindicated)
* **Understanding the importance of identifying and treating the underlying cause of rapid atrial fibrillation, if possible**
* Administering medication will not necessary effectively rate control the patient if there is an underlying cause that is driving the arrhythmia.
* Treating the underlying cause, if possible, can be more effective.
* Possible causes:
  + Thyroid dysfunction
  + Alcohol use
  + Infection/sepsis
  + Dehydration
  + Anemia
  + Pulmonary embolus
  + Drug use (i.e. cocaine)
  + Emotional stress
  + Family history
  + Coronary artery disease – Patients do not need to be routinely evaluated for ischemia every time they present with atrial fibrillation. Risk factors and associated symptoms should drive the decision to test for this.
* **Obtaining a complete history and performing a complete physical examination to identify a possible cause of atrial fibrillation**
  + History should specifically be asked to evaluate the likelihood of the processes listed above.
  + Physical examination should also be conducted to look for signs of an underlying cause such as thyromegaly or asymmetric lower extremity edema.
