| **PATIENT NAME: James Banks**  **PATIENT AGE: 56**  **CHIEF COMPLAINT:** "I have a lot of questions about my discharge, but I don't know where to begin. I feel overwhelmed with all this information!" (Shows brochures). "I don't know if I can do all this insulin stuff, please help me, I am told to take Lantus 60 units at bedtime and Lispro 10 units before meals." | |
| **Brief narrative description of case** | This is an OSCE case that requires a standardized patient actor. James Banks, a 56-year-old white male, is newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. At 210 lbs, he has a history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia. He was admitted 2 days ago for uncontrolled blood glucose levels requiring IV insulin. His AIC is 11% and has not responded to PO diabetic medications in the past. He is now ready to be discharged. The nurse provided teaching on how to manage diabetes at home. She has given him instructional materials, including the brochures "Facts about Diabetes" and "How to manage diabetes at home." Mr. Banks has also watched videos that cover basic information about diabetes management, such as how to use the blood glucose meter. Mr. Banks is ready to be discharged but he feels very overwhelmed by the information provided to him. You are the day team on the general medicine floor. Your task is to make sure he is all set to go home and answer any questions he may have. The overall goal is for the learner to demonstrate patient teach back and safe discharge planning. | |
| **Primary Learning Objectives** | This case focuses on key components of a safe discharge. The IDEAL Discharge Planning strategy highlights the key elements of engaging the patient and family in discharge planning. | |
| **Critical Actions** | Safe Discharge   * Include patient as a full partner in the discharge process * Identified barriers to care * Reviewed medication regimen * Highlight warning signs and problems (hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia symptoms) * Discuss follow up appointments * Educate the patient in plain language about condition (no medical jargon used) * Assess patient understanding/patient teach back (asked patient to repeat back) * Provide information in small chunks and repeat key pieces of information |

Information for the Standardized Patient/Actor:  The standardized patient (SP) is a pleasant, middle class man who wants to please his doctor. He is very non-compliant with his diabetic diet and does not routinely check his blood sugars at home. He checks only when he doesn't feel so well. He feels guilty about not routinely checking his sugar and often "forgets" his log at home on days he has an appointment even though his doctor has asked him to bring this with him every visit. He is well groomed, dressed in pants, a shirt and practical shoes. He appears healthy, with no visual signs of illness. He is pleasant, and smiling and cooperative. He makes little jokes and changes the subject when he is asked about his diet and blood glucose monitoring. The standardized patient is given the following list of questions to ask with suggested appropriate answers from the learners:  Q: "I don't understand why all of a sudden I need insulin, I have been trying very hard to eat right and exercise and I don't know what I am doing wrong"  A: Learners should explain the need for insulin based on his AIC and educate the patient on diabetic treatment  Q: "Should I be concerned about any side effects of taking insulin?"  A: Learners should discuss warning signs of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia  Q: "I am not sure how to use this machine (shows the glucometer)"  A: Nursing learners will demonstrate use of glucometer, timing and frequency of blood sugar monitoring.  Q: "Do I continue to take my metformin with the insulin?"  A: Learners will explain his medication regimen and the need for both insulin and metformin | | |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

The learners enter the room with a standardized patient. They introduce themselves and participate in a team-based discussion by interacting with the patient and address his concerns. They assess his level of understanding; provide instructions in laymen's terms and conduct teach back to ensure patient understanding.
