Mary Jones Bio

Mary Jones is a 50-year-old woman referred to the learner for post-operative adjuvant therapy after debulking surgery for stage III ovarian cancer.

**Background:** She began experiencing vague abdominal discomfort and bloating about 8 weeks prior to her initial visit with the learner. She made an appointment with her internist, Dr. Robbins, when the discomfort persisted and increased in intensity; she had also noticed a 5-pound weight gain despite maintaining her diet and exercise regimen. Dr. Robbins had seen Mary for a comprehensive examination a year ago after assuming her care year due to a change in Mary’s health insurance plan.

A CT scan was ordered which revealed peritoneal carcinomatosis, ascites, and an ovarian mass. Dr. Robbins called Mary into his office and, in the presence of her brother Paul, told her “Mary, I’m sorry, it’s cancer,” and then gave her the contact information of an oncology surgeon, Dr. Jane Taylor. She was told by Dr. Taylor that her cancer was stage III and she would need a major operation. She then had successful optimum debulking surgery 4 weeks ago. She has had her final post-operative visit with Dr. Taylor who has told her that she requires chemotherapy in order to get rid of any residual cancer.

**Home and living**: She is a divorced, single mother who lives with her 17-year-old son, Tom who is a high school junior. Mary was married for 18 years until becoming divorced 3 years ago. The relationship had been strained for a while and then her husband threatened an affair. There was an amicable parting of ways. She is not close to her ex-husband. She met Peter, an electrical contractor, over a year ago. They have been seriously dating and are talking about getting engaged. They have a sexual relationship.

She has a very close relationship with her mother, age 78, who lives in an apartment close by. Her mother is independent in activities of daily living but relies on Mary and Tom for shopping and transportation. Lately her mother is displaying some mild memory issues. As a result, Mary has taken over her financial affairs. Mary’s father is deceased 10 years ago from heart disease.

She is one of 4 children. Her younger sister Laura and her older brother Paul are both a 30-45 minute drive away. She is very close to them and enjoys seeing her 4 nieces and nephews. Laura or Paul will come with her to visits; Peter less so as he owns his own business and works very long hours. Another brother, Richard, lives in LA and they talk rarely.

**Occupation, religion, hobbies**: She works as a secretary in a large accounting firm and has been in that position for twenty years. She has a close friend who is a colleague from work who she sees socially as well. She also has one other close friend from her church. She is very physically active, doing spin class in the morning three times a week and running 5 miles on the weekend. She is a practicing Catholic who attends services every week.

**Psycho-social** She describes herself as a strong person and generally optimistic. Her religious faith and church community are a major source of strength and hope. Her family is also a source of support. She knows how deeply they are affected by her diagnosis. Both Paul and Laura have expressed to her the unfairness of being diagnosed with cancer after the difficulty of her divorce.

She is grappling from the transition from being so healthy and physically fit to being afflicted by cancer. She had seen an internist in the community, Dr. Phelps for 5 years but due to a change in insurance plan had to switch to Dr. Robbins. Due to the insurance plan change, however she had not seen a gynecologist for almost two years. She does not drink, smoke, or use illicit drugs.

**Concerns:** Tom, her son, was 14 at the time of the divorce. The trauma of the divorce impacted his grades in school as well as his mood – he became depressed and distrustful in relationships. His mood now improved after therapy and medications. He is doing better academically in high school now and seemed to be finding himself again. Mary knows he is devastated by her diagnosis and is trying to be as optimistic as possible for him. Peter is a great help as he and Tom get along very well.

She is also concerned about both her mother and her relationship with Peter. Mary worries about how to handle information about her disease with her mother. Should she give her as little information as possible? What if she has a setback? Mary has the comfort of knowing that Paul and Laura will look after their mother if she no longer can.

She worries about Peter; he is never married and has only had two relationships prior to meeting Mary. How will her illness impact their relationship? Will it strengthen? Or will Peter withdraw knowing that Mary’s future is now uncertain and that she could die from her cancer?

Two years ago, a member of her church whom Mary knew peripherally died of metastatic breast cancer. That person had been on chemotherapy for some time; towards the end she had to stop treatment since it was making her too ill, and she then entered hospice care.

Module Three Role Play Scenario Two

**Facilitator (to learner)**: You are seeing Mary on a follow up visit after her third cycle of chemotherapy. She is handing the therapy well, but despite Cold Cap there is major alopecia. After doing her physical examination and checking her CBC, you think to assess how the illness and treatment are affecting her relationship with Peter.

*Learner should use open ended question, ideally with lead-in comment: “There’s a lot going on with you right now; I was wondering how things are going with Peter.”*

**Mary**: “We’re hanging in there, he’s so supportive, but…” or “We’re hanging in there, some weeks are better than others. Peter is so supportive, but…”

*Learner should echo “but…” or ask for more information. If no prompting or if the learner appropriately gives space/silence, continue.*

**Mary**: “It’s just not the same… this is hard to talk about.”

*“This” is intentionally vague. The learner should ask what “this” is. If they as for more information, steer them in the right direction. If they assume “this” means “cancer,” respond with “No, not that.” Ideally, given the cue at the beginning, the learner should realize the conversation is about relationship troubles with Peter.*

**Mary**: It’s ok, I can talk about it now. Remember that I divorced my ex-husband 3 years ago? The year before our divorce we grew apart. Our sex life had never been that great but that year it became mechanical. He wouldn’t care if I was in the mood or if I had a climax. Then we stopped having sex. He then threatened to have an affair. I had no subsequent relationship until I met Peter. We were attracted to each other right away, but I was hesitant to have sex because of my prior experience. So it took a while but when it first happened it was totally different. He made me feel special. He was kind and he was gentle. *Conversation transition – give the learner emotional cues that this is a highly sensitive topic.* We were planning to get engaged! We were going to have a special life together. And now... I’m tired from the treatment. My hair is gone. My last period was a little over a year ago and I was having a few flashes but since my ovaries were taken out, I’ve been having more flashes. I don’t feel that I’m attractive. I don’t feel the desire for sex, but I want to have it for him. We tried two weeks ago but there was some discomfort; he is so understanding. I know that there’s much more to a relationship than just having sex. But it was a good part of ours and now it’s gone.

*Responses to look for from learner:*

**Validation**: Your sense of loss is understandable/valid/normal.

**Reflection**: A lot of issues are having an impact on your sexual relationship. You are fatigued and have the hair loss. Because of the hair loss you feel unattractive. When your ovaries were taken out you lost more estrogen, which is the reason you are having more flashes and you can get vaginal dryness also which causes discomfort.

**Reassurance**: Remember that when you complete treatment your hair will grow back, and the fatigue will resolve. Remember how good shape you were in, so you should come back to yourself pretty soon.

**Planning/problem-solving**: For right now, you could try some lubricants to help with the discomfort the next time. I think the best way to go now is for both of you to see a sex therapist, especially one who specializes in cancer patients. There is a lot that can be done in this situation to re-establish the physical closeness you had before.
