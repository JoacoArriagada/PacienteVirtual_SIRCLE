Mary Jones Bio

Mary Jones is a 50-year-old woman referred to the learner for post-operative adjuvant therapy after debulking surgery for stage III ovarian cancer.

**Background:** She began experiencing vague abdominal discomfort and bloating about 8 weeks prior to her initial visit with the learner. She made an appointment with her internist, Dr. Robbins, when the discomfort persisted and increased in intensity; she had also noticed a 5-pound weight gain despite maintaining her diet and exercise regimen. Dr. Robbins had seen Mary for a comprehensive examination a year ago after assuming her care year due to a change in Mary’s health insurance plan.

A CT scan was ordered which revealed peritoneal carcinomatosis, ascites, and an ovarian mass. Dr. Robbins called Mary into his office and, in the presence of her brother Paul, told her “Mary, I’m sorry, it’s cancer,” and then gave her the contact information of an oncology surgeon, Dr. Jane Taylor. She was told by Dr. Taylor that her cancer was stage III and she would need a major operation. She then had successful optimum debulking surgery 4 weeks ago. She has had her final post-operative visit with Dr. Taylor who has told her that she requires chemotherapy in order to get rid of any residual cancer.

**Home and living**: She is a divorced, single mother who lives with her 17-year-old son, Tom who is a high school junior. Mary was married for 18 years until becoming divorced 3 years ago. The relationship had been strained for a while and then her husband threatened an affair. There was an amicable parting of ways. She is not close to her ex-husband. She met Peter, an electrical contractor, over a year ago. They have been seriously dating and are talking about getting engaged. They have a sexual relationship.

She has a very close relationship with her mother, age 78, who lives in an apartment close by. Her mother is independent in activities of daily living but relies on Mary and Tom for shopping and transportation. Lately her mother is displaying some mild memory issues. As a result, Mary has taken over her financial affairs. Mary’s father is deceased 10 years ago from heart disease.

She is one of 4 children. Her younger sister Laura and her older brother Paul are both a 30-45 minute drive away. She is very close to them and enjoys seeing her 4 nieces and nephews. Laura or Paul will come with her to visits; Peter less so as he owns his own business and works very long hours. Another brother, Richard, lives in LA and they talk rarely.

**Occupation, religion, hobbies**: She works as a secretary in a large accounting firm and has been in that position for twenty years. She has a close friend who is a colleague from work who she sees socially as well. She also has one other close friend from her church. She is very physically active, doing spin class in the morning three times a week and running 5 miles on the weekend. She is a practicing Catholic who attends services every week.

**Psycho-social** She describes herself as a strong person and generally optimistic. Her religious faith and church community are a major source of strength and hope. Her family is also a source of support. She knows how deeply they are affected by her diagnosis. Both Paul and Laura have expressed to her the unfairness of being diagnosed with cancer after the difficulty of her divorce.

She is grappling from the transition from being so healthy and physically fit to being afflicted by cancer. She had seen an internist in the community, Dr. Phelps for 5 years but due to a change in insurance plan had to switch to Dr. Robbins. Due to the insurance plan change, however she had not seen a gynecologist for almost two years. She does not drink, smoke, or use illicit drugs.

**Concerns:** Tom, her son, was 14 at the time of the divorce. The trauma of the divorce impacted his grades in school as well as his mood – he became depressed and distrustful in relationships. His mood now improved after therapy and medications. He is doing better academically in high school now and seemed to be finding himself again. Mary knows he is devastated by her diagnosis and is trying to be as optimistic as possible for him. Peter is a great help as he and Tom get along very well.

She is also concerned about both her mother and her relationship with Peter. Mary worries about how to handle information about her disease with her mother. Should she give her as little information as possible? What if she has a setback? Mary has the comfort of knowing that Paul and Laura will look after their mother if she no longer can.

She worries about Peter; he is never married and has only had two relationships prior to meeting Mary. How will her illness impact their relationship? Will it strengthen? Or will Peter withdraw knowing that Mary’s future is now uncertain and that she could die from her cancer?

Two years ago, a member of her church whom Mary knew peripherally died of metastatic breast cancer. That person had been on chemotherapy for some time; towards the end she had to stop treatment since it was making her too ill, and she then entered hospice care.

Module One Role Play Scenario One

**Facilitator (to learner)**: Your task is to establish a close relationship with Mary by using her illness narrative surrounding her initial diagnosis. After introducing yourself, ask her to tell the story from the beginning.

**Mary**: I was in really good health until I was noticing some stomach discomfort and bloating. I took some over the counter stuff like Pepto-Bismol; it got a little better but then got worse again. So then, when I didn't feel better, I set up an appointment with Dr. Robbins, my new internist. He took over from my prior doctor when my health plan changed. My weight was up seven pounds; I was surprised because I do spin and run and take care of my health. Dr. Robbins examined me; he seemed concerned about the weight gain and thought my abdomen was a little swollen. He then ordered a CT scan.

**Note:** *At this point, hesitate. If learner does not comment, go on. If learner asks what you were thinking, say “The thought it was cancer did cross my mind, but I was hoping it wouldn’t be the case.”*

**Mary:** The CT scan was done on a Friday. On Monday he told me to come in with my brother Paul. That’s when I knew.

**Note:** *Become tearful now. Learner should recognize and reflect your intuition it was cancer. Then you can recompose and go on.*

**Mary:** Dr. Robbins told us the CT scan showed fluid and a mass in the ovary and small masses around the intestines. He said it was ovarian cancer and the cancer was advanced. He told me I needed to see Dr. Jane Taylor at University Hospital, and that she’s a cancer surgeon. That’s all I remember from that visit. He was sympathetic, but I wish I had my old doctor at that visit. He would have been more compassionate.

**Note:** *Become distressed now. Learner needs to reflect feeling of lack of a pre-existing doctor-patient relationship, new diagnosis, advanced disease at diagnosis. Re-compose and go on.*

**Mary:** Dr. Taylor was nice and professional. She explained all about ovarian cancer, that stage III cancer is rarely caught early, that there was a chance for cure but that I needed an operation to remove as much cancer as possible.

I had the surgery about 4 weeks ago. I understand it took seven hours. Dr. Taylor told Paul, Laura, and I the surgery was successful, that she removed as much cancer as she could but couldn’t get it all. She was happy about that. And now I’m here to see you.

**Note:** *The focus is on Mary’s illness narrative to date. Learner should not go on to discuss perspective for visit but should revisit Mary’s narrative with her from the beginning, exploring and empathizing with:*

* Trauma of abrupt change from excellent health to illness;
* Self -blame regarding ignoring symptoms;
* Trauma of lack of relationship with doctor giving bad news;
* Stress of being told she has stage III cancer;
* Worry about not having entire tumor removed.

Example phrases/responses to further exploration:

I used to exercise and watch what I ate – I don’t smoke or drink or do drugs. I can’t believe this could happen to someone like me.

I’m so aware of my body, how could I not notice that I was gaining weight?

I wish Dr. Phelps had been the one to break the news. I think he would have known what to say or do better than my new doctor.

I haven’t seen a GYN for 2 years because of my insurance; what if this cancer could have been picked up earlier if I had been going to a GYN regularly?

Dr. Taylor didn’t get all of the cancer – she was happy about the surgery results, but why didn’t she ask me how I felt? I was pretty scared after the surgery!

*Remember from didactic session that the illness narrative involves active LISTENING and OBSERVING as well as RAP and validation.*

Module One Role Play Scenario Two

**Facilitator (to learner):** Now that you have listened to her illness narrative, your task is to establish a close relationship with Mary by exploring her perspective on treatment. She has stated that Dr. Taylor told her to come see you. Start from her perspective regarding the reason for seeing you.

*Learner should start with “What are you here for today?” or something similar.*

**Mary:** Dr. Taylor told me I **might** need chemotherapy.

**Note:** *It is very important to stop here and focus on learner’s reaction. Your perspective is that you MIGHT need chemotherapy, not that you DO need chemotherapy. The learner should now start thinking why you said* ***might****. Based on your previous role play, the learner should know that Dr. Taylor did not get all of your tumor out, and you shouldn’t be surprised to learn that you need chemo. Based on response, the facilitator might ask learner to think aloud. In this phase Mary should appear earnest and sincere, not fearful; although fear is her underlying emotion, she is trying to contain it and learner must uncover it.*

Possible learner reactions and how you can handle them:

If learner reflects: You might need chemotherapy? Why do you say that? You seem skeptical about that.

**Mary**: Yes, I was hoping I could get away without chemotherapy.

If learner assesses for denial: Didn’t Dr. Taylor tell you she couldn’t get all the cancer out?

**Mary:** Yes, I did understand that, it’s just that…

Iflearner asks your understanding of chemo**:** You seem concerned about chemotherapy; why is that? What is your understanding of chemotherapy?

**Mary:** I know chemotherapy destroys the good cells as well as the bad cells.

Hopefully, learner will explore your perspective regarding chemotherapy:

**Learner:** Tell me why you are concerned about chemotherapy.

**Note:** *Learner feedback - use neutral “concerned,” not “afraid” when possible; the patient/Mary can better describe the emotion.*

**Mary:** (distressed, fearful) I knew this woman from church. She had breast cancer and it spread to her bones and her lungs. For a while she was on pills but then she was on chemotherapy for a couple of years. She lost her hair. She was tired all the time. Some weekends she didn’t come to church. Then she got really ill and they stopped the chemo. She went home and then she died.

*Learner should now follow principles of addressing patient perspectives, with the goal of re-orientation.*

Reflect and explore your perception: “You’re concerned about chemotherapy given that woman’s experience. Not only was she very affected by the treatment but she got progressively worse; you’re thinking that the chemotherapy also contributed to her deterioration at the end?”

**Mary**: Yes, that does concern me and honestly, it’s the main thing I fear/worry about with treatment.

Align with your perception by validating it: “That woman’s illness, her deterioration, and role of chemotherapy in it made a very powerful impression on you. And now you need chemotherapy, so you are thinking back to that person and worrying that the same thing will happen to you.”

**Mary**: You’re right, watching her suffer made a large and negative impression on me. I don’t want to go through the same thing.

Recall the fact that you were in excellent health prior to being diagnosed and start to re-orient perception: “I think you are also worried about whether you will be able to recover from treatment in order to assume your active life – is that right?”

**Mary:** I don’t want to lose the health I have right now. If I have to go through chemotherapy, though, I want to know that I will get my health back.

**Note:** *the focus should not be on details of chemotherapy, but rather the context of treatment (curative rather than palliative); the learner should address Mary’s two major fears of debilitation from chemotherapy and chemotherapy irrevocably affecting her health. Ideally, the learner should avoid over-reassurance, mobilize support, and end with a partnership statement. Use some of the phrases below as examples for feedback for the learner.*

In addition to surgery, chemotherapy is required to put your cancer into a complete remission and some people in complete remission are cured. It is important that you keep this goal of cure in mind.

The person in your church was in a different position than you. Her cancer had spread and the spread of her cancer itself may have caused her to have many of the symptoms that she had.

Even in that situation where cancer has spread, studies have shown that patients who are on chemotherapy actually feel better than patients who were not because the chemotherapy is controlling the cancer.

Given all that, it is true that chemotherapy medicines have side effects and some of them are significant, some even more so than hair loss. There is a way of reducing hair loss from chemotherapy that we could discuss. There are other side effects that we need to discuss in more detail and we can do that on a visit in the future.

With regards to the ability to your recovery from chemotherapy and going back to your active lifestyle, I can say with a very high level of confidence that you will be back to your previous lifestyle soon after you complete your treatment, with some physical conditioning and motivation. Your health prior to treatment may be helpful in keeping you in good shape throughout and after treatment.

While you are on treatment I will be monitoring you very closely and be adjusting things as we go to make sure that you get through this treatment successfully.

Remember, you have many people who love you on your side and I know their support will also help you while you are on treatment.

I could also have a patient who went through treatment like yours and is doing well speak to you.

Did I address all your concerns regarding chemotherapy or are there others?
