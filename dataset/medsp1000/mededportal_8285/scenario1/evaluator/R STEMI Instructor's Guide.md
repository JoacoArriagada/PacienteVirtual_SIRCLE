      1. If a student does not recognize the presenting diagnosis or initial treatment, the middle and end the case may not be reached, limiting intended teaching points.       1. An alternative scenario requiring TPA could be added, although may be unnecessary as a first year introduction to the right ventricular STEMI
   3. **Expansion for medical, intensive care, or cardiology students**
      1. A second section of the case covering management after disposition from the emergency department may be added. This provides teaching points from initial presentation to discharge from the hospital, combining many aspects often not seen or treated by the same practitioner.

**Appendix B – Critical Action Teaching Points**

Initial management – Oxygen

While oxygen has been standard of care in acute myocardial infarction for decades, a recent meta-analysis raised questions as to its efficacy. The same article showed a trend toward harm, although chance (according to the authors) and confounding factors may explain this trend. Future study is needed to determine if oxygen will remain standard of care in the setting of MI.

1. Burlis A, et al. (2011) Oxygen therapy for acute myocardial infarction: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Emerg Med J.* Online pub.

Chest pain management – Aspirin

Aspirin, a cyclooxygenase inhibitor, reduces platelet function, limiting further clot build up. Clinical efficacy in improving outcomes in acute myocardial infarction is well established. A recent trial in the New England Journal of Medicine supports this benefit. However, the dosing remained controversial. The study found no increased risk or benefit beyond 75-100mg. However, the authors studied dosing daily for 30 days and not the initial dose at presentation. Currently, 325mg of aspirin is given in most North American emergency departments.

1. Antman EM, et al. (2004) ACC/AHA guidelines for the management of patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction: a report of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Task Force on Practice Guidelines (Committee to Revise the 1999 Guidelines for the Management of Patients with Acute Myocardial Infarction). *Circulation*. 110(9):e82-292.
2. The CURRENT-OASIS 7 Investigators. (2010) Dose Comparisons of Clopidogrel and Aspirin in Acute Coronary Syndromes. *New Engl J Med*. 363:930-942.

Right STEMI management – Need for volume; Avoid beta-blocker, nitro, and opiates

A right ventricular infarction usually accompanies an inferior infarction. The resulting decrease in RV ejection fraction greatly reduces the preload available to the left ventricle. Cardiac output and systemic blood pressure, as a result, are linked tightly to the patient’s volume status.

When a patient presents with an RV infarction, attention must be paid to this volume status. When a patient presents, IVF, usually normal saline, should be established. A patient with hypotension or evidence of hypovolemia should be bolused, often 1-2 liters depending on the clinical scenario. Increasing preload and thus LV ejection fraction is a necessary step to controlling hypotension in this scenario.

When a patient is exhibiting signs of congestive heart failure, the practitioner must balance the need for fluid and pulmonary congestion. When increased volume is a necessity, intubation will likely be necessary.

Medications commonly used in the presence of acute myocardial infarction, including nitrates, morphine, and beta-blockers can decrease preload or LV ejection fraction by a variety of mechanisms. In the setting of right ventricular myocardial infarction, these should be used with caution, always with IVF established. It is likely prudent to avoid their use all together in this scenario.

1. Antman EM. (2007) ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction: Management in Libby P, et al. *Braunwald's Heart Disease: A Textbook of Cardiovascular Medicine, 8th ed.* Saunders Eselvier. Online Ed.
2. Brown JE, Hamilton GC. (2006) Chest Pain in Marx, et al. *Rosen's Emergency Medicine: Concepts and Clinical Practice, 6th ed.* Philadelphia, PA: Mosby Elsevier. Online Ed.

Right STEMI management – Heparin

Heparin inhibits conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin, preventing further clot formation. The use of unfractionated heparin is standard of practice, with its efficacy well established. Current debate involves the potential use of low-molecular weight heparins. A trial in the New England Journal of Medicine reported improved outcomes and increased major bleeding with the use of enoxaparin versus unfractionated heparin with fibrinolysis treatment for acute myocardial infarction. Fondaparinux may reduce major bleeding.

Current guidelines, developed prior to much of this research, recommend the use of unfractionated heparin.

1. Antman EM, et al. (2006) Enoxaparin versus Unfractionated Heparin with Fibrinolysis for ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction. *N Engl J Med*. 354:1477-1488.
2. Antman EM, et al. (2004) ACC/AHA guidelines for the management of patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction: a report of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Task Force on Practice Guidelines (Committee to Revise the 1999 Guidelines for the Management of Patients with Acute Myocardial Infarction). *Circulation*. 110(9):e82-292.
3. The Fifth Organization to Assess Strategies in Acute Ischemic Syndromes Investigators. (2006) Comparison of Fondaparinux and Enoxaparin in Acute Coronary Syndromes. *N Engl J Med*. 354:1464-1476.

Right STEMI management – Disposition to PCI

The benefit of percutaneous cardiac intervention in the initial management of an acute myocardial infarction is well established. Fibrinolytic therapy, however, is necessary in a subset of patients that cannot receive rapid PCI. Fibrinolytics used prior to PCI when patients will have a delay to treatment (facilitated PCI) has been shown to have an increased rate of complications without improved outcomes. However, more recent data suggests early PCI after fibrinolysis may reduce ischemic complications compared with fibrinolysis alone when immediate PCI is not available.

1. Cantor WJ, et al. (2009) Routine Early Angioplasty after Fibrinolysis for Acute Myocardial Infarction. *N Engl J Med*. 360: 2705-2718.
2. Ellis SG, et al. (2008) Facilitated PCI in Patients with ST-Segment Myocardial Infarction. *N Engl J Med*. 358:2205-2217.
3. Keeley EC, et al. (2003) Primary angioplasty versus intravenous thrombolytic therapy for acute myocardial infarction: a quantitative review of 23 randomized trials. *The Lancet*. 361:13-20.

**Appendix C – Additional Teaching Points**

ACS / MI History\*

**P**lace – Left side, substernal – usually not peripheral or tightly localized

**P**alliation / **P**otentiation – Worse with activity

**Q**uality – Squeezing, crushing, pressure

**R**adiation – Back, arm, neck, jaw

**S**everity – Usually severe, but variable

**S**ymptoms – Shortness of breath, lightheadedness, nausea, diaphoresis, parasthesia

**T**ime – Sudden onset, persistent

\*Poor sensitivity/specificity

CAD risk factors (for patients without known CAD)

Age: Men > 45, Women > 55

Family History: MI or sudden cardiac death at age < 55 in male, < 65 in female 10 relative

Smoking

Hypertension > 140/90

HDL < 40 (HDL > 60 is a negative risk factor)

LDL greater than cut-off established by the above factors\* or medication treatment for hyperlipidemia

\*Third Report of the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) Expert Panel on Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Cholesterol in Adults (Adult Treatment Panel III) Final Report. (2002) *Circulation*. 106;3143.

Initial Management of STEMI

IV, O2, Monitor, ABCs

12-lead ECG

ASA 324mg, chewable

Beta-Blocker (unless evidence heart failure or hemodynamic instability)

Hypotension, Bradycardia, Reactive airway disease, Acute CHF

Nitrates, morphine – Unless RV infarct suspected

Reperfusion <90 minutes – PCA vs. TPA

Absolute Contraindications for TPA

History of ICH

Cerebral vascular lesion

Malignant intracranial neoplasm

Ischemic stroke within 3 months EXCEPT acute ischemic stroke within 3 hours

Suspected aortic dissection

Active bleeding or bleeding diathesis (excluding menses)

Significant closed-head or facial trauma within 3 months

Right ventricular infarct

PE: Hypotension, JVD, Kussmaul's sign, tricuspid regurgitation

ECG: ST-elevation III>II, AVF, RV4 (often ST-depression V2, 3, ST-elevation RV5, 6)

Tx: Support preload / reduce afterload

* IVF – primary preload
* Inotropes (only with sufficient IVF) – Dobutamine ideal if BP tolerates (added vasodilatation)

Pressors – Potential increase to afterload and worsening cardiac function
