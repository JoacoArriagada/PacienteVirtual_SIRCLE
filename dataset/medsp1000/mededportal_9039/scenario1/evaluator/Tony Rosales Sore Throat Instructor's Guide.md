**Tony Rosales, Sore Throat - Instructor's Guide**

**Resource**: Tony Rosales, Sore Throat

**How to use the resource file**: The resource file contains information necessary for training standardized patients, presented in a table format. It describes the case, the way used/validated, contains SP training notes and additional details.

**Purpose of the resource**: To enrich the sources of case scenarios available to standardized patient trainers and medical school faculty for objective structured clinical examinations.

**Educational Objectives**:

* Recognize the symptoms of sore throat
* Correctly apply the Centor clinical prediction rule
* Effectively use verbal communication to build a supportive relationship and gather clinical information
* Effectively use non-verbal communication to enhance relationship building

**Conceptual background**: This case was created for use with standardized patients, for teaching and/or evaluation purposes. The case is intended for teaching/examination of medical students, years 1 through 4 (depending on medical school's type of curriculum), as well as residents and faculty development.

**Practical implementation advice**: All original materials/patient exam room needs are described in the case, users are free to change the original case to better suit their needs, as long as due credit is applied.

**Description of Centor Rule**: Reference: Centor RM, et al., Centor Clinical Prediction Rule for Acute Sore Throat, Med Decision Making 1981; 1:239-46. It was postulated that the presence of three out of four of the symptoms listed below in the unwell patients is predictive of Group A haemolytic streptococcus (GAS):

• Temperature > 38C (100.4F)

• Absence of cough

• Swollen, tender anterior cervical nodes

• Tonsillar swelling or exudates

If the patient has 0 of the aforementioned symptoms, then there is <1% chance of Streptococcal infection (Group A -haemolytic); if 3 out of 4 are present, then the patient has approximately 40% chance of Streptococcal infection (Group A-haemolytic). An automated calculator is available online, at:

<http://www.mdcalc.com/modified-centor-score-for-strep-pharyngitis>

**Use of section "Standardizing the Unknown"**: This section is intended for use when training multiple SPs for portraying the same case. Its purpose is two-prong:

1. It is very hard to predict and standardize the answers to each and every question learners might decide to ask of the SP. Having more details to the portrait makes the character more complete, and the SPs can easier associate with and think as the patient portrayed.
2. The details referred to in this section might be quite different for different states, cities and schools. These questions are intended for the group of SPs to decide on. This gives them the chance to partake in the creation of the portrayal, bring local detail to the role, and take ownership of the role. The SP trainers should feel free to add/delete questions in this section to make it most applicable to their local specific circumstances.

In my experience with adding this section to the SP case, it takes less than 10 minutes of allotted training time for the SP group to decide on the details, and on numerous occasions has meaningfully contributed to the case presentation. Usually, the last question related to "What else would you like to know about this person?" brings up any parts of the role that might have not been clear or well described during training.

The notion of training standardized patients in the context of a role, rather than for specific interrogative props is gaining momentum across the nation and is considered a possibility for SP training for the USMLE Step 2 CS examination. Karen Lewis, Board of Directors Member for the Association for Standardized Patient Educators (ASPE), ASPE Publications Committee member and Director of the SP Program at George Washington University, wrote in the September 2011 issue of "ASPE News": "In my experience, simulated activities where the SP can perform within the confines of a behavior profile almost always accomplish better learning for our students, clearer educator understanding of what they have learned, and more informed ideas about the next steps."

**Could a SOAP Note and Evaluation Rubric Be Used?**

If a SOAP note is necessary to be used for evaluative purposes, the faculty designing the evaluative experience has the freedom and responsibility to decide on the additional details related to its evaluation rubric.

One simplified schedule for SOAP note evaluation I have used with novice learners includes:

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **#** | **Scoring Rubric** | **Yes** | **No** |
| 1 | Information content was recorded within the appropriate part of the note; i.e. subjective information was recorded in section "History" etc. |  |  |
| 2 | Information content was recorded correctly; i.e. all facts were correct, examinations not performed were not recorded, etc. |  |  |
| 3 | Differential diagnosis list logically followed (was supported by) the recorded information |  |  |
| 4 | Immediate diagnostic workup list logically supported the considered differential diagnosis list |  |  |
| 5 | The note was well organized, was written with clarity and without typos |  |  |

This scoring schedule could be the starting point for any faculty wishing to further personalize the assessment of SOAP notes within OSCE exams, especially for cases used with more advanced learners.

**Recommended length of session:**

In simulated environments, the most learning occurs during the debriefing of a scenario. In general, it is recommended that the length of the debriefing session is as long as or longer than the time devoted to patient encounter and note writing. Such debriefing may be purposefully omitted if the case is used for high-stakes exams.

As this case is designed, a debriefing session including a 15-minute patient encounter, followed by a 45 minute debriefing is recommended, allowing the faculty to provoke in-depth discussion of the case and demonstration of (correct) clinical examination maneuvers.

In an OSCE setting, planning for 15 minutes for the learner in patient room, 10 minutes for patient note, and 30 min for debriefing with faculty-observer (for teaching purposes only) would be sufficient.

The debriefing session may also include:

* Discussion of what went well
* Discussion of what could be improved
* Review of questions that presented most difficulty for the student(s)
* Specific recommendations on:
* Time management
* Patient examination approaches
* Data gathering approaches
* Closing of the encounter with provision of summary to the patient
