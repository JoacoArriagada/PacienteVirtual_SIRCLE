**Ideal Scenario Flow**

*Provide a detailed narrative description of the way this case should flow if participants perform in the ideal fashion.*

*As the students enter the room, they find the patient in the hospital bed that is a bit uncomfortable but is in no apparent distress. On further discussion with the patient, he/she informs the students that he/she are continuing to have ongoing chest pain and feel a little short of breath. Only if prompted and asked will the patient elaborate that the chest pain is left sided, anterior chest pain and sharp in character that gets worse with taking a deep breath. Ideally, the students will inquire about the chest pain (onset, location, quality, etc.) and begin to suspect that this is more than a low risk rule out MI case. The students should review the admission H&P, review the original chest radiograph and ECG and repeat the studies. The repeat studies are the same as original ECG and chest radiograph. In addition, the student should uncover the recent travel history and findings of left calf pain and tenderness. The students should order a D-Dimer (which will be elevated), followed by a CT angiogram of the thorax and will find out that the patient has a pulmonary embolism and should initiate anticoagulation. Some students may decide to directly order a CT angiogram and forego the D-Dimer test.*

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

*Provide a list of management errors or difficulties that are commonly encountered when using this simulation case.*

1. *Anchoring error: This is the most common error / difficulty that we see with this case. As we developed this case, we suspected that this would occur. We have used this aspect of the case as a salient teaching point during the debriefing. For this case, the students reevaluate a patient who was admitted to the hospital as a low risk rule out MI case. Because this patient has a preliminary diagnosis, we frequently see that the students will cling to this initial impression despite the development of conflicting information (ongoing sharp, pleuritic CP, SOB, recent travel, leg pain and tenderness). This is in part due to some of this information not being clearly delineated in the admission H&P. We purposefully omitted some key information, this was not done to confuse or misdirect anyone. A number of students initially continue along the pathway that the presenting complaint is cardiac in nature. Some groups continue to administer NTG (with no relief of CP) and or repeat cardiac enzyme testing. If the students do not initially pick up on the missing key information, they may in turn miss the diagnosis.*
