# Cardiac Arrest, Code Blue Situation

The entire scenario, including feedback, is to be completed in 30 minutes.

# Setting

Inpatient room in Clinical Simulation Center (will use one of the large rooms).

# Participants

Code Team:

Senior resident (R-2)

Junior resident -- portrayed by a third-year resident, MD

Respiratory therapist – portrayed by a respiratory therapist or standardized professional

ICU charge nurse – portrayed by attending doctor, a nurse, or standardized professional

iStan mannequin or similar model

Patient’s wife -- portrayed by standardized patient

Ward resident – portrayed by standardized professional

# Scenario

The patient is an 85-year-old man who had a large anterior myocardial infarction four months ago. Since then, he has had two hospital admissions for congestive heart failure, including this one. He had an ejection fraction of $18 \%$ on an echocardiogram performed two weeks ago, and he really hasn’t felt at all well since his myocardial infarction. He has a past medical history of hypertension for 20 years and type II diabetes mellitus for 15 years. He also has a history of prostate cancer treated with radiation five years ago.

# Medications

Lisinopril 10 mg p.o. daily

Coreg $3 . 1 2 5 \mathrm { ~ m g }$ twice daily

Lasix $8 0 ~ \mathsf { m g }$ twice daily

Aspirin 325 mg once daily

Lipitor 40 mg once daily

Lupron injections once monthly

Insulin 70/30 30 units in the morning and 15 units in the evening, sq

Current Illness. The patient was admitted to the hospital from the clinic yesterday for a three-day history of weight gain, worsening dyspnea, orthopnea, and fatigue. Chest x-ray showed cardiomegaly and pulmonary edema, and he is being diuresed in the hospital. Blood pressure has been running 90-100 systolic, and although his initial dyspnea has improved, he remains short of breath at rest. The resident and the code team were called to see the patient because of unresponsiveness and pulselessness. As you (the senior resident) enter the room with the code team, his wife, who is very distraught, stops you and says “He’s been fine until he had that heart attack a few months ago. He just hasn’t had a chance to recover from it.” She asks you to “try everything possible.” “I don’t know what I’ll do if he doesn’t make it.”

Initial rhythm is v-fib unresponsive to defibrillation. Rhythm deteriorates to asystole, and in spite of full resuscitative measures (including endotracheal intubation), and continuous CPR for 8 - 12 minutes, no electrical rhythm, pulse, or spontaneous respiration is present. Pupils are fixed and dilated. ICU charge nurse, after about 8 – 10 minutes says, “I think we should call the code. He’s been down for over 10 minutes and we’re not getting anywhere.” “He’s got such a bad heart, I don’t think anything is going to work.” The respiratory therapist agrees with the charge nurse “We’ve done everything in the ACLS protocol, and have not been successful.” “He’s been in asystole for 10 minutes. The chance of a successful outcome is pretty much zero” (and other similar sentiments.) The intern on the code team disagrees, “I understand what you’re saying, but his wife has a point – he’s been doing well until his MI, and maybe if he pulls through this, he’ll be fine. Besides, I’ve never been in a code where the patient dies and gets pronounced and I don’t want this to be my first time.“ “I don’t think we should be so hasty. Anyway, I think his chart says ‘full code’ and we haven’t tried everything yet. We could put the external pacer on.”

At this point, the ward intern caring for the patient enters the room, “Oh my. I talked to the patient yesterday when he began feeling better. He confided in me that he didn’t want to have CPR if it ever came to that, and he definitely didn’t want to be on a ventilator. I had planned to go over the advanced directive papers with him today, but I know for sure he wanted to be a DNR. I should have gotten the advanced directives to him yesterday.”

The code intern asks the senior resident, “What do you think we should do? What do you think is best for him? Are you sure?” At the nurse’s prompting, the team agrees that the wife should be called into the room. After an explanation and a discussion with the senior resident, the wife requests that resuscitative efforts cease.

# Standardized Patient Training Notes

The Wife, Age 83

Presenting Situation. Your husband had a serious heart attack four months ago. His recovery has been slow; this is the second time he has had to be readmitted to the hospital with congestive heart failure: “Fluid in lungs,” “Congestion in lungs.”

After three days of worsening chest pain and shortness of breath, yesterday he finally agreed to go see his doctor who admitted him to the hospital. X-rays were taken and the doctor told you that the patient was retaining water, which created problems with his blood pressure, breathing, and caused his heart to work harder. This fluid build-up is being treated and he seemed to be feeling a little better, so you went home to get a few hours of rest before returning this morning. You have been sitting in the room this morning as he was resting. A few minutes ago, the machines to which he is connected alarmed. There always seems to be some kind of alarm going off, so you weren’t too worried at first, but when you couldn’t get your husband to respond, you called for the nurse (“I need help in here!”). The nurse called for “help” and suddenly people came running into the room and began working on your husband. You are standing at the foot of the bed, extremely distraught, and wringing your hands.

Knowing the doctor and team are here to take care of him, you leave the room and turn back to look at him with a worried expression on your face—then say to the resident:

“Doctor, he’s been fine until he had that heart attack a few months ago. He just hasn’t had a chance to recover from it. Please do everything possible – I just don’t know what I’ll do if he doesn’t make it!”

“Do everything you can; he’s all I’ve got!”

If asked about his health history: “He’s got a bad heart…the doctor said only $1 5 \% "$ “He’s had trouble breathing at home, and sometimes it’s pretty bad. He’s a little better today, though.”

If asked if you want to stay in the room: “Maybe I’ll just wait outside by the door…you’ll come get me if…” Exit the room wringing your hands and fighting back the tears.

# <><><><><><><>

A few minutes later, you may be called back into the room to discuss your husband’s condition, what the team has determined, and how you would like to proceed. If you are told about the conversation your husband had with the ward intern regarding your husband’s desire for NO life-prolonging efforts, you say “Well, we never exactly talked about that kind of thing, but it sounds like what my husband would want.” After some convincing, you sadly agree for the doctor and the rest of the team to cease resuscitative efforts.

If any medical terms are used, or if the resident says anything you don’t understand, ask “What do you mean?”

After code is called, ask “What happens now? I didn’t expect this.”

This scenario provides an opportunity for the resident to broach the topics of autopsy, funeral home, pastoral care, etc.

Social History. You and your husband have been married for 63 years. You are hard-working people who lived modestly, investing time and money into a small, but successful stationery store here, which you owned and operated for over 50 years. You worked and played together and saved for college educations for your two children and for a wonderful retirement. You sold the store and retired 10 years ago and have purchased a small condo in Tucson, Arizona where you normally spend the winters. You have taken several trips and made plans to go to Rome in the Spring.

You and your husband have been in relatively good health. You eat well, exercise moderately on a regular basis, play board games, visit and dine out with friends and family often. You also are active in your church and participate in many of the activities offered at the senior centers both here and in Tucson, Arizona.

# Standardized Professional Training Notes

Ward Intern, J. Vetter, MD

Presenting Situation. You are the Ward Intern who has been caring for the patient since he was admitted yesterday. He had a heart attack four months ago and his recovery has been slow. He has been readmitted to the hospital twice with congestive heart failure.

His wife brought him to Dr. X’s office yesterday, after three days of worsening chest pain and shortness of breath. He was admitted and started on medications to reduce the fluid build-up and swelling in his chest, but he remains unstable. You have been caring for him since he was admitted and had a chance to talk with him candidly. He confided in you late yesterday evening after his wife left that he did not want “heroic” measures taken to prolong his life. You had planned to bring him copies of the advanced directives, which he could read and sign, after discussing it with his wife of 63 years.

The patient’s condition has worsened and the resident and code team have been called in to handle the case. He is unresponsive and they cannot get a pulse. The Code Team has been working with him for several minutes when you enter his room. When you see what is happening, you say:

“Oh, my, I talked to the patient yesterday when he began feeling better. He confided in me that he didn’t want to have CPR if it ever came to that, and he definitely didn’t want to be on a ventilator. I had planned to go over the advanced directive papers with him today, but I know for sure he wanted to be a DNR.”

If asked if you spoke with his wife, “No, she wasn’t here at the time, but the patient seemed sure of what he wanted.” “He said a couple of times that he’s been feeling so bad, he just couldn’t see himself getting better, and he didn’t want to be ‘hooked up’ to a lot of machines; he didn’t want a lot of tubes and stuff. I should have had him sign the advanced directives yesterday while we were talking about it.”

Other members of the team and the resident will discuss this new information and the resident should make a decision as to what should be done next.
