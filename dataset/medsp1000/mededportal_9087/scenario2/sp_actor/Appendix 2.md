# The End-of-Life Care Family Meeting Case Training Notes

# Wife of the Patient, Age 87

Setting: Conference room in the hospital where you have been invited to attend a meeting with the people who are caring for your husband to discuss plans for his care.

Your husband, who is 89 years old, is in the ICU of the hospital. He is very sick. He is on a machine that is helping him to breathe and has many lines and tubes connected to his body. He does not open his eyes even when you call his name.

Each day the doctors have told you that he isn’t getting any better, but you keep hoping. You saw a little twitch in one hand when the nurses turned him and think maybe that means that he is about to wake up.

# Social History:

. You and your husband have been happily married for 65 years. You were a homemaker and never worked outside the home. You have an eighth-grade education.   
Your husband has always been the decision-maker in your home and that has been very acceptable to you. You took care of the cooking and the cleaning and he was the breadwinner. He worked as a car mechanic most of his life.   
 You never were blessed with any of your own children, but when you were 47 years old and your husband was 49, you adopted a child. This was the most wonderful experience of your life and your daughter has been your absolute “pride and joy.”   
 You love her dearly. You are so proud of her because she went to college and has a successful career as a corporate accountant. She lives is Los Angeles, has three children, and works very hard.

# Medical Background:

About seven years ago, your husband started becoming forgetful. The doctor tried several medications, but he became progressively worse. You took care of him at home for five years, but about two years ago, he began to wander, developed incontinence, and you just couldn’t manage the care any longer.

Your daughter came home and helped you find the very best dementia-care nursing home that was available. Your husband did pretty well there for awhile. The staff loved him. He was pleasantly confused, was very agreeable, and seemed happy there. You visited every day.   
 About a year ago he began declining more. He couldn’t do anything for himself anymore – he couldn’t even feed himself. He doesn’t speak words anymore and just makes some sounds.   
 Because your daughter is so very busy with her work and children, you have tried not to burden her with her dad’s decline in condition. You do understand that dementia is progressive.   
 Your husband had not signed any advanced directives. You and he have always attended church regularly. You both believe that there is a God, that there is life after death, and that someday you will be reunited. You and your husband regularly read the Bible and prayed together. He told you that he hoped no one would ever have to take care of him; he hoped he would never have to “linger on.”   
. Your life-long pattern has been to depend on others to make decisions. You feel that your daughter could probably make a better decision as to what should happen to the care of you husband. You know your husband is not doing well, but you really don’t understand too much about his condition, what it means to be on a ventilator, or what the future might hold.

# Presenting Situation:

You and your daughter have been asked to come to a conference room in the hospital to speak with the doctor and hospital staff to discuss your husband’s condition and decide what should be done regarding his continued care.   
. You and your daughter sit down at a conference table. You’re not certain what will happen next. You are fidgeting and nervous. You are looking to your daughter to help guide you through this, trusting that she will know more about what the doctor has to say and make the right decision.   
. During the conversation, you frequently look to her, even if a question is addressed directly to you.

You are not sure what the right thing to do is. As various options are discussed, you find it difficult to put all the facts into perspective to make an informed decision. You often say, “I just don’t know what to do; daughter, what do you think?” . You know your husband would not want to “linger” if there was no hope, but you don’t know if there really is no hope, or if there are other things that can be done for him.  You bring up the fact that his faith has always been important to him. You pray with him. It’s hard to know if this is meaningful to him. Is there anything else we should do to help him feel closer to God?  You are not sure what “DNR” means.
