# Patient 1 Chronic Disease Management (Substance Abuse) Training Notes

You are a 62-year-old-Caucasion woman who lives between towns A and B on a rural state road. Your husband is a truck driver who is gone three to four days a week and provides a good income. He turns his check over to you every week to deposit and take care of the house. Your two children (son and daughter) are both grown, live in Chicago and St. Louis, and neither is married, nor do they have stable lives. Both children have drug issues, but no legal problems or financial needs. Your husband is a quiet man who likes to watch TV when home and demands little of you. You have few close friends anymore and you spend a good deal of time alone in the rural home longing for years past when the house was busy with children and school. Your husband has never been violent and does not drink to excess. The anti-depressants really do help you cope and you realize it.

When you were in your mid $\leavevmode { 3 0 ^ { \prime } s }$ you had breast cancer and had a radical mastectomy. At that time you were treated for the cancer, which has long since gone into total remission. At that time you were treated with narcotics for pain and became addicted to Hydrocodone. As the doctor tried to wean you off the drugs, you began doctor shopping. When the internet came to your home in the 1990s, you found you could get both Hydrocodone and Oxicodone from internet doctors for the right price. In addition, your children were both narcotic users with the access to larger markets in the city. Both children occasionally supply your habit and you sometimes use street drugs, such as morphine and heroin. Although you have never injected yourself, you have learned to snort (inhale) the drugs as well as take them orally. At this time, you have been seriously addicted for at least 20 years and have hid it from your husband. You see a psychiatrist in city S who has treated your depression. He knows nothing of the addiction. You know you get dangerously depressed when you stop taking Lexapro.

Not long ago you ran out of internet narcotics when your on-line pharmacy was shut down by the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). Your children could not arrange any shipments from Chicago or St Louis. You went to your psychiatrist and asked for 40 pills of Percocet for a migraine. He reluctantly prescribed the drugs, not knowing you are an addict. That night the doctor got a call from the druggist at Walgreens telling him you had filled similar prescriptions from other doctors during the same week.

The doctor told the pharmacy to fill the prescription with no refills, and called you to go to his office the next day. You showed up at the doctor’s office and confessed the whole story. The doctor said he needed to talk with the husband to treat the problem and you refused. The doctor told you he would not prescribe any drugs until he spoke with your husband. Unless you agree to the doctor’s request to make your husband part of your treatment-support network, he would “fire” you as a patient and not treat you any longer. This is the reason for your visit to the clinic. You now have another supply of narcotics from your daughter, but need Lexapro. You did not know a patient could be “fired,” and are very concerned no doctor will ever see you again if anyone finds out. This is a big concern to you. You do not know how to address this problem.

Your symptoms for depression are as follows. You sleep a lot and have little energy (except when using drugs). You have problems keeping the house clean. When your husband came home, you rally to hide things from him. You lost weight over the years. You lost interest in friends. You do not enjoy any hobbies. The anti-depressants worked and relieved the depression, but you never were able to stop the narcotics.

You use no prescription drugs other than the anti-depressants and narcotics. You do not drink or smoke. You do not take any over-the-counter drugs. You currently use six to eight $1 0 ~ \mathsf { m g }$ tablets of Hydrocodone each day (10/500 Manufacturer--Watson), but will use street narcotics, or other narcotics, if your usual supply runs out.

When the resident asks about the depression, describe it honestly. As the resident asks questions, answer honestly, but do not volunteer any information. Answer only the questions asked.
