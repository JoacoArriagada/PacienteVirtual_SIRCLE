# Appendix 13

# Training Notes for the Medical Error Case

# Standardized Patients/Professionals Needed:

Patient is not very nervous, anxious, or fearful, but he wants to know what happened to him. If the resident does not reveal the error, the patient does not question it. If the resident does reveal the error, the patient could say something like "How did something like that happen?" He should not ask what will be done to prevent the error in the future, because we want to see if the resident comes up with this unprompted.

Nurse, J. Mosley, RN, is flustered because she made an error.

Intern, Dr. S. Carter is accepting of feedback and not "upset," but she does give the excuse of having good handwriting.

# Note: This case takes place in two different rooms:

1) One room will be set up to simulate a hospital room where the patient will be in bed.

2) The second room will be set up as a residents’ lounge where two conversations will take place:

a. A nurse will discuss the situation with the resident b. The resident will discuss the error with the Intern

# Training Notes

# Patient:

Background. You are a 68-year-old man who was admitted to the hospital for a knee-replacement surgery tomorrow morning. You are diabetic, but have been able to maintain appropriate blood-sugar levels with proper diet. Your doctor told you that during your hospital stay, you may need to be given insulin, so the injection you received after your lunch was not unexpected. However, the reaction you had following that injection was terrifying and something that you had never experienced before. Shortly after lunch you began feeling weak and shaky. You broke out into a sweat and knew something was wrong, so you called for the nurse immediately.

She checked your blood sugar and quickly got you a glass of orange juice and some IV medicine to get your blood-sugar levels under control. After a while your symptoms subsided and you felt better, but since you had such a bad reaction to the insulin, you’re apprehensive about taking any more. You’re nervous about the possibility of another such reaction—it was an awful and frightening feeing that you do not want a repeat. The nurse told you that s/he’d talk with your doctor and s/he would be in to see you shortly.

Presenting Situation. The experience you had following the insulin injection has made you extremely uneasy. You didn’t know what was happening, but the thought of another such incident is unnerving. You are fearful that under the circumstances, if you are not awake and alert another bad reaction to the insulin could be disastrous. When the doctor comes into your room, you report how you felt and express your dire concerns with any future administrations of insulin.

# Training Notes

# Nurse: J. Mosley, RN

Background. You have been caring for the patient since he was admitted earlier in preparation for kneereplacement surgery tomorrow morning. You saw on his chart that he was diabetic and Dr. Carter ordered 40 units of Lispro insulin to be administered with the patient’s lunch. After he finished his lunch, you gave him the prescribed dosage. Shortly thereafter, the patient called for assistance. When you arrived, he was sweating, shaking, and in visible distress. He said he felt weak and strange—he didn’t know what was happening to him. When you did an Accucheck, his count was at 39—way too low. You followed the hospital’s protocol for hypoglycemia and gave the patient a small glass of orange juice with half an amp of D50.

The patient is feeling fine now and his Accucheck is 150 (within normal range). Your patient is stable, but now you go back to the file to see what might have happened. You notice that Dr. Carter, the intern, used an unapproved “u” instead of writing out “units” for the insulin; the $" \boldsymbol { \mathbf { u } } ^ { \prime \prime }$ was mistaken for a zero, and the patient got a dose of 40 units of Lispro insulin instead of 4 units for an Accucheck of 202.

Presenting Situation. You go to the residents’ lounge to speak with the doctor about the patient, taking the paperwork to show the resident Dr. Carter’s unacceptable abbreviation and how it had been misinterpreted. You explain what you have done to stabilize the patient, but tell the resident that now the patient is extremely anxious and fearful of another “bad reaction” to insulin. You report that the patient is in the next room and does not know about the error. You ask the resident if s/he can write a list of actions that should be taken at this point. The resident needs to go speak with the patient, but there also are probably some other things most residents would like to do at this point. Instead of simulating a chart, or having the nurse take orders, they can just write them down.

# Training Notes

# Intern: S. Carter, MD

Background. You have been on duty at the hospital for over 16 hours and the patient load is extremely heavy. You haven’t taken time to eat and want to get to the cafeteria before it closes shortly, so you try to hurry through the backlog of case notes that need to be entered. You know you’re not supposed to use abbreviations; however, you’re in a hurry and know that your handwriting is very legible, so using abbreviations as a way to save time should not be a problem. You finish all of your paperwork and tell the nurse you’re leaving for 30 minutes to grab a bite to eat.

Presenting Situation. You’re caught up on patient notes, you’ve gotten something to eat, and you’re relaxing in the residents’ lounge for a few minutes before you go back onto the floor. The resident who has been supervising your work enters the lounge and wants to speak with you about a problem that has occurred with one of the patients. The resident shows you an order you wrote earlier for the patient in which you used an unacceptable abbreviation. The resident explains that the abbreviation was misinterpreted and the patient was given an inappropriate dosage.

The resident should give you, Dr. Carter, feedback on the error. Attributes of good feedback should include:

Statement of issue in a concrete manner (didn’t “hedge”) Use of non-judgmental language Help you, as an intern, to make plans to avoid this mistake in the future
