* **Thank you for participating in H&P Scoring!**
* The written H&P is a rich source of meaningful student assessment data. It provides insight into student’s competency with information gathering, interpretation, and clinical reasoning
* **Patient history and physical examination is the single most important diagnostic tool available to physicians.** Obtaining a comprehensive history, performing a complete physical exam, and documenting a clinical encounter are critical physician skills. The written history and physical (H&P) reflects an author’s competency in Accreditation Council on Graduate Medical Education domains of: patient care, medical knowledge, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, and at times, systems-based practice and practice-based improvement. Core Entrustable Professional Activities reflected in the written H&P include: gathering a history and performing a physical exam, prioritizing a differential, recommending and interpreting common diagnostic and screening tests, and documenting a clinical encounter.

* **How more and less experienced clinicians gather information**
  + Less experienced clinicians: gather information first and formulate the assessment LATER
  + Experienced clinicians: formulate an assessment EARLY and use information gathering for hypothesis testing refining the assessment in “real time.” The “hypothesis driven” process shows pretty clearly in the history of present illness (HPI) and physical exam (PE) sections of the written H&P.
  + Motivated less experienced clinicians: can achieve a similar “hypothesis driven” effect by
    - * intentionally thinking about the assessment while gathering information
      * going back (often more than once)to ask additional questions and perform additional physical exam maneuversand then revising the H&P

**H&P Scoring**

* H&P scoring is meant to be a **formative learning exercise** with shared goal of improving student clinical reasoning and documentation.
* **We recommend you start out by reading the assessment.** This puts the rest of the H&P into focus and allows for a clearer idea as to whether the writer’s history and physical exam are hypothesis (assessment) driven.
* The rubric uses a 5-point scale with anchors to facilitate scoring and provide students with specific ideas for documentation improvement. Examples listed are meant to be illustrative but not exclusive
* The rubric is criterion referenced. A “5” is a theoretical ideal H&P written for this particular patient. An experienced attending with time on his/her hands would write a “5” H&P.
  + A “3” describes someone who obtains a complete H&P, but who is gathering information first and formulating an assessment and differential later. A medical student is expected to achieve a score of 3 by the end of his/her third year.
  + A “5” describes someone whose H&P is both hypothesis (assessment) driven AND patient specific. A “5” clearly has a differential diagnosis in mind and who uses the H&P for hypothesis testing. A strong and motivated student who goes back to ask additional questions and/or perform additional physical exam maneuvers after obtaining the initial H&P might achieve a a “4” or “5” in some categories.
* Use an “intermediate” score of 2 or 4 for students who are approaching the higher category but just not quite there
* In order to achieve a higher score, ALL the characteristics of the lower scores need to be met. So to get a “5” on a given question a student would need to meet both the “3” AND the “5” criteria.
* **Accuracy.** In cases where you as the rater are not directly involved in a given patient’s care, look for the H&P being age appropriate and internally consistent
* **Disclaimer.** There is variability in written documentation expectations. The P-HAPEE rubric and “best practice” recommendations are based on literature, review of written documentation expectations from a number of medical schools, stablished educational frameworks, discussion with medical educators, students, and clinicians as well as a multi-center validation study.

1. **History of Present Illness:** begins with the first change in health status related to the chief complaint and concludes at the time writer assumed patient care including reason for admission; identifies history source

|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 1. Inaccurate , incomplete, and/or includes excessive irrelevant data |  | Accurate, mostly organized sequence of relevant events with well-characterized symptoms (quality, severity, etc.) and most of the irrelevant data omitted |  | Hypothesis (assessment) driven, concise, comprehensive, organized. | **Expectations**   * **Accurate.** Internally consistent * **Organized**   + The story should flow in a logical sequence usually beginning with the first change in health status and proceeding chronologically until the time the writer assumed patient care. * **Complete with well-characterized symptoms**   At minimum 4 or more of the HPI elements: location, severity, timing, quality, duration, context, modifying factors, associated signs/sx  Students often make the error of not including the medical management prior to admission, for instance the ED course   * **Irrelevant data omitted**    + History elements that are either completely irrelevant or belong in the “additional history” section should not be included in the HPI.This is often a writer who has not “processed” the information obtained from the family who is simply transcribing their words directly. * **Identified history source.** The writer should identify the history source early on, usually right after your patient introduction. * **Reason for admission.** After reading the H&P you should understand why the child is being admitted.   **Superstar Points**  **Hypothesis driven:** Whenreading a “5” HPI you have a clear impression that the writer has an assessment and differential in mind and is using the HPI for hypothesis testing. **(**It is helpful to read the assessment before reading this section)  Eg:12 yr old previously healthy boy with acute onset fever, crampy abdominal pain, and initially nonbloody, then bloody, mucousy diarrhea. The child has colitis and is moderately dehydrated and the top differential diagnosis considerations are bacterial colitis vs. inflammatory bowel disease.  Inclusion of family history (+/-) of autoimmune disorders (inflammatory bowel disease), travel and animal exposure (infectious colitis) in the HPI vs. family history, and social history.  Inclusion of oral intake, urine/stool output, dry lips (hydration), arthritis, aphthous stomatitis, uveitis, and skin manifestations (inflammatory bowel disease) in the HPI vs. review of systems   * **Patient’s/caregiver’s perspective.** A “5” writer seeks to understand and incorporate the patient and family perspective. What do they think may be the cause? What impact has this illness had on patient’s and family’s lives? How has the hospital experience been so far? Any fears they have, any expectations they have of the medical staff? Anything we can do to make the hospital experience better? * **Collateral history.** Obtaining history from sources other than pt/caregiver: review of prior records, calling outside sources when indicated | | | | |

In pediatrics this often includes vision, hearing, developmental, nutrition, growth, development screening | | | | |

1. **Assessment:** “big picture” synthesis of collected information leading to the formulation of a prioritized differential and identification of the most likely diagnosis. Author (1) **Identifies** critical defining history AND physical exam/diagnostic study findings. (2) **Synthesizes** findings into medical terms and concepts (“started today”→acute; RR 60, retractions, pH 7.2→respiratory distress). (3) **Characterizes** findings using semantic qualifiers (no relevant PMH/PHM significant for; acute/chronic; mild/severe; acidotic/alkalotic).
