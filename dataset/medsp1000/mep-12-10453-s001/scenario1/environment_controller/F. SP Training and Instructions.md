Title: Rash in Breast-Feeding Mother: A Standardized Patient Case for Clerkship Students

Issue: Rash and Itching

Patient name: Rosemary Rizzo

Presenting Situation: Rosemary Rizzo is a 28-year-old Italian female who has come to the Emergency Department today because of an itchy rash.

**CASE SYNPOSIS**

You are Rosemary Rizzo, a 28-year-old Italian female.

**WHY YOU ARE SEEING THE DOCTOR TODAY**

Your chief complaint is a rash and intense itching. You noticed yesterday that you were covered with a red rash all over your body. Today, you are incredibly itchy. For the past 2 days, you have been achy, tired, and a feverish – and had assumed that you had the flu.

The itching and rash are everywhere. Scratching the rash doesn’t even eliminate the itching.

**OPENING**

**Note: Bolded information includes key points that should be shared with students and may not be ad-libbed.**

Your initial statement should relate to your itchy rash. Its hard to predict what the student will say when s/he comes into the room, but you should appropriately respond to the student’s greeting or question. For example:

The student may ask: “What brings you to the emergency department today?”

* You could respond: “I’m having an itchy rash all over my body”

The student may say something like: “Hello. How are you feeling?”

* You could respond: “I itch like crazy.”

**HOW YOU APPEAR DURING THE ENCOUNTER**

Physical description

* You should be reasonably neat and groomed
* As you are portraying a patient in the Emergency Department who recently had a baby, you will be in comfortable athletic wear with a baggy shirt/sweatshirt. You may wish to wear a sports bra since the students will likely be listening to your chest and lungs.
* You should appear a bit tired and a tiny bit disheveled since you haven’t been feeling well (and since you have a new baby at home – the latter of which won’t come up initially until the student asks additional questions.)

Description of affect and behavior

* You are **tired** since you haven’t been feeling well and you haven’t been sleeping well
* You are **concerned** and want to know **what has caused the itching and fever**
* You are **concerned about the health of your baby**
* You are able to sit up, stand, and walk
* You don’t have difficulty breathing
* **You hope they can give you something for the itching**
* You are cooperative and answer questions readily
* You are intelligent

**REVIEW OF SYSTEMS**

**Vital Signs: You have had a fever between 100 and 102 degrees Fahrenheit orally for the past couple days.**

**Skin: You have a rash everywhere except the palms of your hands and the soles of your feet. You haven’t changed laundry detergent, and you’ve been using the same soap for years.**

**HEENT: You have not had any mouth pain or itching**

Pulmonary: You have not had any difficulty breathing.

Gastrointestinal: You have not noticed any pain in your belly.

Genitourinary/Rectal: No changes

**Musculoskeletal: Your joints and muscles have been weak and achy**

Neurological: You have not noticed any weakness of arms or legs. Nor have you experienced any tingling in your fingers or toes.

**MEDICATIONS**

* You were prescribed **Bactrim** twice a day since last week; since you are so tired, you may have missed some doses
* You are also taking a **Prenatal vitamin** (you have continued taking this since you just recently gave birth)

**PAST MEDICAL HISTORY**

Your overall health has been good and you **just gave birth to your first child** – he’s 11 days old (his name is Kristopher Michael).

They had to put a catheter in you for 36 hours after giving birth because you were unable to urinate after having the baby, but fortunately they were able to remove the catheter before you were discharged from the hospital. The day after coming home from the hospital, you developed pain and burning when urinating and you had pain in your back. You went to an **Urgent Care Center and were diagnosed with a urinary tract infection (UTI)**. The healthcare provider at the Urgent Care Center prescribed the antibiotic, Bactrim to be taken twice daily for 14 days.

You think that the fever and muscle aches started 3 days ago (6 days after you started the antibiotic). Yesterday you noticed the red rash (7 days after starting the antibiotic). Today, the rash became absurdly itchy. You have not changed laundry detergent. You haven’t traveled outside the country to any place unusual.

You still have at least a week’s worth of antibiotic left.

**FAMILY HISTORY**

**Parents:**  both alive and well; in their late 50s
**Grandparents:** paternal grandfather died of a heart attack at age 65; maternal grandmother is alive but recently had a stroke. Both are back in Italy.
**Brothers/sisters:** ages 26 and 32, both in good health although your brother has some food/medicine sensitivities. **He gets really sick after eating fava beans.**
**Children:** gave birth to first child, a little boy, 11 days old.

**PRESENT LIFE**

**Age:** 28 years
**Occupation:** school teacher
**Marital status:** married for 3 years; spouse is 30 years old and in good health
**Life details:** You are planning to take a full academic year off from teaching
**Health insurance:** Blue Shield

**PERSONAL HABITS:
Alcohol:** none since you got pregnant; 1-2 drinks per month socially prior to pregnancy **Tobacco:** never smoked **Caffeine:** 1 cup of coffee per day **Drugs (illicit):** none **Vitamins:** prenatal
**Exercise:** you were walking on the treadmill during your pregnancy. You are waiting until your 6 week check-up with your obstetrician and plan to return to your regular workout routine at the gym as soon as you get clearance from the doctor.

**HOW YOU WILL RESPOND TO DIFFERENT INTERVIEWING QUESTIONS/STYLES**

1. You are poised and self-assured. Do not try to dominate the interview. Do not volunteer information; wait for the student to ask you questions. Bold-faced information below represents key points that you should try to make during the student encounter.
2. If the student asks you whether you have any drug allergies, you should respond, “None that I know of, but my brother **has to avoid some medications and some foods; he got really sick after eating fava beans once**, but I don’t remember the exact details.”
3. If the student asks you whether you have ever taken sulfa medications or antibiotics before, you should say, **“Not that I know of, but I really haven’t ever used a lot of medications. I’ve always been pretty healthy.”**
4. The student may ask if you have taken anything for the fever or rash. You should say, “**No, I didn’t know what was safe to take**. **What can I take? Will you prescribe something for me?”**
5. If the student asks you if you are breast-feeding your son, you should say, **“Yes.”** (Please do NOT volunteer that you are breast-feeding; see if the students ASK. Once the students ask, you can make it clear that you **want any medications to be safe for your baby and not affect your milk supply**.)
6. My baby is going to be okay, right? I’m not contagious am I? **I don’t want my baby to get sick.** (The SP should have some anxiety about the health of her baby.)
7. If the student asks if the symptoms of your urinary tract infection have improved. You should say, “**They seem to be improving, but I don’t think its completely gone. I still have a little burning when I have to use the toilet.”**
8. If the student mentions that Bactrim may not be the best choice while you are breast-feeding, you might ask, “**What kind of harm?” or “Why did the provider at the Urgent Care center prescribe it for me then**?”
9. If the student asks how important breast-feeding is to you, you should emphatically respond, “**I am really enjoying breast-feeding. I love cuddling with the baby. I know how good breast-feeding is supposed to be for baby’s immune systems and overall health. I PLAN to continue breast-feeding my baby**.”
10. You should ask, “**Is there something that you can give me for this rash that is safe for my baby and won’t affect my milk supply**?”
11. If the student uses medical jargon (e.g. “kernicterus”, “cerebral palsy”, “jaundice”, “bilirubin”, “G6PD”) you should request clarification, “ e.g. What does that mean?”
12. If the student asks you if the baby has any rash or has yellow skin/eyes, you should respond, “**I don’t really know. He is very fussy. I don’t get much sleep**.”
13. If asked if your vaccinations are up to date, you should respond, “**Yes, my vaccinations are up to date**.”

Although you began the encounter as poised and self-assured, now you are concerned about your rash, your infection, maintaining a good milk supply, and your baby’s health.

**ENDING THE ENCOUNTER**

Ordinarily, the student will end the encounter with a statement such as: “Do you have any questions?” If the student has not addressed how bothersome the itchy rash is for you, you should ask, “**Can you give me something to treat this itching? And what about my fever and pain**? **Is my baby going to be okay**?”

However, if the student has already addressed these concerns, you should respond with a statement such as: “**No, I have no further questions at this time**.”

**PHYSICAL EXAM**

* No evidence of allergy in nose/eyes.
* No ulcers in mouth.
* No hair loss has been noticed.
* No angioedema (swelling of lips or tongue).
* No difficulty breathing
* No pain to palpation on chest wall when the chest is auscultated to look for evidence of pulmonary involvement.
* No cardiac abnormalities (no murmurs).
* Blood pressure is normal.
* No pulmonary findings (no rubs, no wheezing, no rales).
* No edema.
* No abdominal distension or pain. No hepatomegaly or splenomegaly.
* No peripheral neuropathy; reflexes intact; sensation normal.
* If student examines your joints, s/he will usually procede in the following order: fingers, hands, wrists, elbows, shoulders, neck, jaw, etc. S/he will observe each joint for symmetry, redness, range of motion. Next, s/he will palpate each joint for effusion, nodules, inflammation, thickening. Finally, the range of motion may be evaluated, where the patient is instructed to relax while the joint is moved by the examiner. If the student examines your joints, you can indicate some level of pain/tenderness.
* If the student investigates your muscle aches, s/he may begin by palpating a muscle group such as the quadriceps
* No “costovertebral tenderness” (e.g. when student palpates your back)

**PHYSICAL EXAM CARDS**

1. When the student attempts to look at the rash (arms, legs, back, belly), the 3 x5 card for the RASH should be shown.
   Maculopapular rash; no desquamation.
2. When students attempt to feel lymph nodes in your neck, you should show them this 3 x 5 card:
    Lymphadenopathy in cervical and axial lymph nodes
