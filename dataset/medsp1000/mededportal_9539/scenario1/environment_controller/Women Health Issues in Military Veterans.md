# URINARY INCONTINANCE PATIENT: CARRIE EDWARDS

LEARNER INFORMATION (to be shared with learner prior to role–play)

Patient’s Name: Carrie Edwards

Chief complaint: Unable to keep from leaking urine

Location: VA Primary Care Physician’s Office

Learner Skill Set: Eliciting data in an objective and non-judgmental manner; accurate recording of symptoms; sensitivity; motivational interviewing (MI) skills; self-awareness

# FACILITATOR’S GUIDE (NOT to be shared with learner prior to role-play)

Carrie’s husband wanted her to come and see the doctor because of some “trouble” they are having during intimacy. He thinks it is a hormone “thing” and wants her to try hormone replacement, but she isn’t sure she wants to do that.

She is sexually active and is not having pain with intercourse, although she has been noticing involuntary urine loss with sexual activity. She is embarrassed about having to talk about her chronic urine loss and the ammonia-like odor.

# CARRIE EDWARDS URINARY INCONTINENCE STANDARDIZED PATIENT TRAINING MATERIALS

# CASE SUMMARY

Carrie Edwards is a married 51-year-old female. She presents to the office at this time because of involuntary urine loss, which has become worse over the past 1-2 years. This has significantly influenced her social life and her ability to maintain normal activities. She thought that her symptoms were normal for a woman her age who had kids. Her girlfriends convinced her that her symptoms were not the norm and that she should be evaluated by her personal physician. Her husband thinks it’s a menopause “thing” and wants her to ask about hormone therapy.

She has not been to the doctor for years as she has been very healthy and has seen no need. She is very independent and “tough,” thinking that it is weak to get sick. She would express the opinion that preventative care is unnecessary. She is horrified at this loss of control over her body.

During the day her urine loss is mostly related to involuntary urine loss on the way to the bathroom, but is occasionally also associated with coughing, sneezing, or laughing. The amount that leaks out when laughing has increased.

Recently she and her husband are now noticing involuntary urine loss with sexual activity. She has noticed an ammonia-like odor. This is what tipped the scale and caused her to make the appointment.

# PAST MEDICAL HISTORY

General health: Good.

Have there been any recent changes in the patient’s health? Just the urinary incontinence.

Any previous Hx of diabetes or hypertension? None. (This is important as worsening disease states can worsen symptoms, especially if patient were on a water pill, which she is not on.)

Any new medications that could potentially worsen her urinary symptoms? None.

Ask open ended or specific questions:

High blood pressure: Not that I know of.

High cholesterol: I don’t think so.

She has not been to the doctor since the birth of her youngest child. She has had no pap smears, no mammograms, and no checkups. She has not seen the need for it since she has been so healthy.

Since the delivery of her last pregnancy over 18 years ago she has had increasing difficulty with involuntary urine loss. Her symptoms began right around then, but have significantly increased over the past 1 to 2 years. Initially it was only associated with rare loss on the way to the bathroom and occasionally having to get out of bed to urinate in the middle of the night.

Over the past 2 years she has noted increasing need to void at night. Initially she was getting up about once a night but that has increased to 3-4 times nightly. She is having increasing difficulty not losing urine on the way to the bathroom to void and is now also experiencing urine loss with coughing, sneezing, or laughing. She generally needs to wear either a maxi pad or an adult diaper to not soil her clothing.

She does not lose gas during sexual activity.

# MILITARY INTERVEIW THIS INTERVIEW IS MEANT TO SUPPORT THE INFORMATION ALREADY IN THE CASE. IT IS NOT MEANT TO BE MEMORIZED

1. What branch of the military did you serve in? US Air Force.

2. How long? 10 years. What rank? E-6 (pay rate) Technical Sgt. (rank). What job? Military Police. Who would you report to? Shift supervisor. Who reported to you? Team members, usually 3 to 5 officers. Where were you stationed? Basic training at Lackland Air Force Base; tech school was also there; then returned; went to Ramstein Air Base in Ramstein, Germany, 1978- 1983; Wright Patterson Air Force Base in Dayton, Ohio, 1984-1988.

3. Describe the physical work environment. Sometimes would check ID cards at the gate. Other times would did patrols in the housing area or the flight line. Would investigate car wrecks, thefts. Sometimes deployed for short amount of time to other bases for back-up when bases were short, security police officers.

4. Please describe a typical day (travel, hours worked, tasks given, etc.). Every shift would check in at headquarters, then be assigned to do gate security, traffic control, or investigate various crimes.

5. How did this profession affect your ability to live a healthy lifestyle (exercise, sleep, nutrition)? The long hours (mostly 12- hour shifts) made it hard to make time to exercise and always being on the go, did not always eat right.

6. Are there over the counter medications, alternative medications that were typically used in your profession (weight loss, energy drinks, tobacco, steroids, etc.)? Smoked 1 pack of cigarettes per day, multivitamins, calcium $5 0 0 \mathrm { m g }$ twice daily.

7. What are things that your friends find interesting about military service? (Like how often did you shoot your gun?)? What types of questions do you get asked by the public? Everyone wants to know where I traveled and if I ever shot anyone (no).

8. How did you get interested in Military Service? It seemed like a great way to travel and meet different people.

9. What did you like most about your profession? Working with lots of different people.

10. What did you dislike most about your job? Very stressful, worrying about getting shot when you have a family and friends at home.

11. Educational background: High school, Graduated high school. Vocational school or College, Education Benefits? None. Special training, Boot camp? Boot camp was 6 weeks long. They just yell a lot and try to break you down and reform you in their image of a team player.

12. What are the physical and psychological qualifications/testing required?

To get into the military you have to pass a written test. Each job has its own cutoff score to be eligible. The higher your score, the better job you can get. You have to pass a basic background check. Being in the security police, you were expected to be in top shape and that became harder after having kids.

13. What positions have you had since you started this profession? After separating from the Air Force in 1988, I had been a stay-at-home mom before getting the job at the Post Office.

14. Can you get promoted? If so, to what position and how? When are you done with your service? You can get promoted in the military. It is based on how long you have been in your present rank. For enlisted folks they are listed as E1 through E9. They are also based on test scores you achieve showing job proficiency.

15. What are the "perks" of military service? You can travel. They make schools available in your career field, 30 days of vacation time, unlimited sick time.

16. How did you get paid (contract, per mile, per hour, etc.) How can you make more money? You get paid a monthly salary, money for food—basic allowance for subsistence (BAS), a housing allowance if living off base or use of a house on base if you have a family or a dorm room if you live by yourself. In the reserves your pay is prorated for the few days you work a month.

17. What was the most stressful part of your job? Dealing with criminals and worrying about getting shot at

18. How was it taxing psychologically? You have to be alert all the time; your life and your team’s lives depend on it.

19. How free were you to discuss your political views and opinions? You have the right to speak your view and opinion. You cannot give speeches or campaign for candidates while in your uniform.

20. What emotional problems were common in people in your profession?

Similar to the civilian counterparts, PTSD, anxiety, depression. You had to be on the lookout for addictions.

21. What type of physical activity was required in your position? You wear a heavy bulletproof vest. You had to qualify with shooting multiple weapons. You also had to pass the PT test and stay in shape by running, doing sit ups, and pushups.

22. What things happened that really make you angry? People breaking the law and getting mad at me for catching them.

23. What other professions did you work with, and how did they affect your job (make your job easier or harder)? I worked with just about everyone in the military.

24. What was it like to re-enter civilian life? It was difficult going from being a boss, someone with authority carrying a weapon, to being a stay-at-home mom. I loved taking care of the kids, but it was an adjustment.

25. What "health risk" behaviors were common in your profession (tobacco, alcohol, specific drugs, sex, stress, marital or family discord, etc.)? A lot of the folks smoked and drank. Partying was a way to deal with the stress.

26. How did your vocation affect the personal life of you or people you know? Well in the military you have to be ready to leave everyone and everything at a moment’s notice. Sometimes you don't know how long you will be gone and sometimes you cannot tell people where you are going or what you are going to be doing.

27. What activities and/or hobbies do people in your profession like to do? Most of the people in the military are younger and involved in everything from sports, to camping, to school, to taking care of families for the married folks.

28. Are there any items of clothing, or props that someone portraying a person in your profession would use consistently (Hats, keys, stuff you’d keep in your pockets or bags, day planners, work boots, particular “costume,” or types of clothing…)? No specific props needed.

29. If a person in your position were to get discharged, what would they most likely have done to justify involuntary termination and what would the steps involve? To get separated involuntarily is fairly hard to do in most cases. Sometimes though there are cutbacks like in any job and anyone can be let go. If you break rules, there are rules in place—kind of like when you have a union job—that must be followed to let you go. If you break laws, then you can be dealt with just like in the civilian world with fines, probation, and jail time.

30. What type of jargon would be common for someone in this line of service …words they use that are “lingo” specific to the profession? Every branch of the military has its use of acronyms. Basic training is boot camp. Then there is your job training (tech school for Navy and Air Force, AIT for army). DOS is date of separation. Chow hall is the dining facility.
