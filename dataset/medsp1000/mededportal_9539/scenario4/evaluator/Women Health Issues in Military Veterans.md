# HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS

Your challenge, as the standardized patient (SP) is threefold

□ To appropriately and accurately reveal facts and emotional concerns about Ms. Day’s health, medical, sexual, and social history
□ To observe any changes in approach made by the interviewer and adjust your portrayal and responses accordingly
□ To accurately report in verbal feedback the impact that the interviewer had on Ms. Day and to reflect on how the observable skills were used by the learner

Ms. Day was sexually assaulted by her commanding officer two years ago during her military deployment in Afghanistan. Ms. Day was a Captain in the Marines. She worked closely with her commanding officer (CO) for several months, developing strategies to support the needs of Afghan women while assessing risk to soldiers involved with this work.

The attack occurred after two weeks of increasingly flirtatious comments and nonverbal indicators from her C.O. Ms. Day ignored the comments because they made her uncomfortable and she did not want to encourage what was an obviously inappropriate advance. The other staff was starting to notice, which increased her discomfort.

Charlotte got up in the middle of the night to use the rest room and when she returned her CO was in her room waiting for her. She initially resisted the attack but soon realized that resistance could lead to serious physical injury. He covered her mouth and used force to hold her down, while growling verbal threats against her career if she were to report what happened. The episode was utterly humiliating. He was there for only 20-25 minutes, but when he left she felt he had been there for hours.

□ The incident involved physical overpowering; there was no than additional brutality beyond the rape. Intimidation and the subtle power of a superior officer over a subordinate allowed the incident to happen. Her pride kept her from calling out or screaming. She is proud to be tough, stoic, and independent. She did not want him to see her fear.
□ Charlotte blames herself for this. She feels that she failed in some way and that she is somehow unworthy of respect. It is a male-dominated culture: The unspoken rule in the military is $\mathrm { C O } = \mathrm { G O D }$ . A commanding officer makes the rules. Marines are “trained to obey.” This CO was known to be a volatile, aggressive person and she didn’t want to do anything to provoke him.
□ The incident shattered her trust in the service. As a Marine you are trained to trust your CO as if they were a father figure who had your best interests at heart. The CO became the enemy, which is counter to all of her training. Her pride in being a Marine was shaken. Most service men/women never say they are ex-Marines when they return to civilian life. Charlotte identifies herself as an ex-Marine.

During the attack, Charlotte’s commanding officer threatened to ruin her military and even civilian career with bad evaluations and future references, if she were ever to come forward about the rape. Not only was she afraid of the CO’s reprisal; she was worried about being singled out from her work group, if she made the incident known. She knows of three peers (female) who reported similar situations and were further mistreated or left the service because of fear. Reporting an incident such as this is frowned upon by peers and other officers though most would not admit that. It’s commonly known that “perpetrators get promoted, victims get discharged.”

Ms. Day went back to work the day after the attack and told no one. He was in the office the next day as well and acted as though nothing ever happened but did not engage or flirt with Ms. Day again. Ms. Day was scared and depressed following the assault but did not report it. Her symptoms of trouble sleeping, trouble concentrating, and feeling blue resolved on their own about 3 months after the attack. (See Depression Screening for detailed information on symptoms.)

About two months after the rape, Charlotte was discharged from active duty. She came back to Dayton and served in the reserves (military police) for 6 months.

During this time, she began dating her future husband – her first serious relationship since the rape. Early on in their courtship, Charlotte had a brief recurrence of her symptoms, and her boyfriend (now husband) thought that he was not adequately pleasing her sexually or that perhaps she wasn’t interested in having a serious relationship.

He eventually expressed his concerns, and Ms. Day reluctantly shared her trauma experience with him and also informed him that he was her first sexual partner since the attack. This conversation led to increased understanding and opened up communication between them, which contributed to her healing process and improved their sexual relationship. Ms. Day and her husband were able to work through their relationship challenges together and have maintained a strong sexual and emotional bond, until the fertility issues started.

They became engaged after dating for only six months and have been married for about a year. Ms. Day had a brief recurrence of symptoms when she first became serious with her husband but has felt normal until now.

The transition from active duty to civilian life was hard for her. The freedom and lack of routine left her feeling adrift. It has been a challenge to take care of herself the way she knows she should. After all, why would a person put a lot of energy into self-care when you feel you are a bad or unworthy person? In the military there was no choice about schedule…you HAD to do things when you were told.

# MILITARY INTERVIEW THE FOLLOWING INTERVIEW WITH A MARINE IS TO BE USED AS BACKGROUND TO SUPPORT THE MILITARY INFORMATION IN THE CASE. IT IS NOT MEANT TO BE MEMORIZED.

1. What branch of the military did you serve in? Marines.

2. How long? 6 years. What rank? Captain. Who would you report to? Section Chief, Lieutenant Colone. Who reports to you? Team members, usually 3 to 5 officers/enlisted members. Where were you stationed? Basic officer training, Quantico, Virgina; Specialty school was also Camp Johnson, North Carolina; Stationed Okinawa, Japan, 2 years; then deployed to Afghanistan for 1 year; 2 years at Camp Pendleton, then got out of active duty and was assigned to the Military Police unit, Wright, back home, Dayton, in Ohio. Now separated from the reserves.

3. Describe the physical work environment. When not deployed it was mainly an office environment. During the deployment, worked with helping local women in Afghanistan, trying to also get information on possible terrorist activities. Hours were long, had to wear heavy battle armor, and carry a weapon.

4. Please describe a typical day (is there travel, hours worked, tasks given etc.). A typical day in the office was spent organizing paperwork, updating files. During the deployment we would meet, discuss the village area we were going into, what security we would use, and what information we were looking for.

5. How did this profession affect your ability to live a healthy lifestyle (exercise, sleep, nutrition)? When I wasn't deployed and now that I have separated from the military, I have a normal office job, but sometimes have to work late or weekends, since I am the part-time fill-in person.

6. Are there over-the-counter medications, alternative medications that were typically used in your profession (weight loss, energy drinks, tobacco, steroids, etc.)? Did smoke for 5 years, $\%$ pack per day. Quit 1 year ago when trying to get pregnant. Takes over-the-counter Advil for Headaches and Tums for stomach upset.

7. What are things that your friends find interesting about military service (like how often do you shoot your gun?)? What types of questions do you get asked by the public? I really don't talk about it much after the incident happened in Afghanistan.

8. How did you get interested in military service? It seemed like a great way to travel and meet different people.

9. What did you like most about your profession? Working with lots of different people.

10. What did you dislike most about your job? Trying to get all the paperwork correct that people need for their careers and lives.

11. Educational background: High school, Graduated high school. Vocational school or College, Education Benefits? Bachelors of Arts in Business with an emphasis in Human Resources Special training, Boot camp? Officer Candidate School was 12 weeks long. They just yell a lot and try to break you down and reform you in their image of a team player.

12. What were the physical and psychological qualifications/testing required? To get into the military you have to pass a written test. Each job has its own cutoff score. The physical requirements are ongoing and make sure you stay in shape.

13. What positions have you had since you started this profession? None.

14. Can you get promoted? If so, to what position and how? When are you done with your service? You can get promoted in the military. It is based on how long you have been in your present rank; for officers you spend 2 years as a $2 ^ { \mathrm { n d } }$ Lieutenant, 2 years as a $1 ^ { \mathrm { s t } }$ Lieutenant, then 4 to 6 years as a Captain.

These promotions are automatic as long as you do your job and stay out of major trouble. After 4-6 years as a Captain you can compete for your chance at Major by submitting a packet against those in your career field. Promotions in the military are not like those in normal jobs. In order for you to be promoted, there has to be “an opening” for you to be moved into. In order to be promoted, you have to have a certain amount of time “in grade,” meaning you have been at your current rank for a specific amount of time. If you are passed over, this can actually force you to retire from the military. This is also a possible reason that Ms. Day left the Marines at the time she did.

15. What are the "perks" of military service? You can travel, they make schools available in your career field, 30 days of vacation time, unlimited sick time.

16. How did you get paid (contract, per mile, per hour, etc.)? How could you make more money? You get paid a monthly salary, money for food—basic allowance for subsistence (BAS), a housing allowance if living off base or use of a house on base if you have a family or a dorm room if you live by yourself. In the reserves your pay is prorated for the few days you work a month until you get activated for deployment, then you get the same pay as the active duty folks plus any special pays for your deployment such as hazardous duty pay, separation pay, hostile fire pay.

17. What was the most stressful part of your job? Deadlines. That is what HR is all about.

18. How was it taxing psychologically? Knowing you have to get everyone’s paperwork in on time for their family to have insurance, get paid, save for retirement.

19. How free were you to discuss your political views and opinions? In the military you have the right to speak your view and opinion. You cannot give speeches or campaign for candidates while in your uniform.

20. What emotional problems were common in people in your profession? Similar to the civilian counterparts—PTSD, anxiety, depression. You have to be on the lookout for addictions.

21. What type of physical activity was required in your position? You wear a heavy bulletproof vest. You have to qualify with shooting weapons. You also had to pass the PT test and stay in shape by running, doing sit ups, pushups, and pull-ups.

22. What things happened that really make you angry? People in authority that abuse their positions for their own gain.

23. What other professions did you work with, and how did they affect your job (make your job easier or harder)? I worked with just about everyone in the military.

24. What was it like to re-enter civilian life? It was difficult going from such a structured environment to working part-time. But we wanted to have a family, so it was time for me to get out of the military. When one cousin, a career Army man, retired from the service (before age 40), he got a job in industry that dealt with military contracts. He said two of the hardest things to adjust to were listening and knowing “who out ranked me.” For example, in the military, he didn’t have to listen closely at the meeting to instructions, because he would later be given a written briefing that had the same details. While in the service, he could simply look at someone’s uniform and tell if they were a higherranking officer than him or below him. When he began working at the new office as a civilian, he felt he was on his “best behavior” all of the time because you could bump into the president of the company at the vending machines and not know it because they don’t wear their rank on their sleeve.

25. What "health risk" behaviors were common in your profession (tobacco, alcohol, specific drugs, sex, stress, marital or family discord, etc.)? A lot of the folks smoked and drank. Partying was a way to deal with the stress.

26. How did your vocation affect the personal life of you or people you knew? Well, in the military you have to be ready to leave everyone and everything at a moment’s notice. Sometimes you don't know how long you will be gone and sometimes you cannot tell people where you are going or what you are going to be doing.

27. What activities and/or hobbies did people in your profession like to do? Most of the people on the military are younger and involved in everything from sports, to camping, to school, to taking care of families for the married folks. I personally like more low key activities like reading, watching TV, and gardening.

28. Are there any items of clothing, or props that someone portraying a person in your profession would use consistently (hats, keys, stuff you’d keep in your pockets or bags, day planners, work boots, particular “costume” or types of clothing…)? No specific props needed.

29. If a person in your position were to get discharged, what would they most likely have done to justify involuntary termination and what would the steps involve? To get separated involuntarily is fairly hard to do in most cases. Sometimes though there are cutbacks like in any job and anyone can be let go. If you break rules there are rules in place—kind of like when you have a union job—that must be followed to let you go. If you break laws then you can be dealt with just like in the civilian world with fines, probation, and jail time.

30. What type of jargon would be common for someone in this line of service …words they use that are “lingo” specific to the profession? Every branch of the military has its use of acronyms, basic training is boot camp, then there is your job training (tech school for Navy, Air Force, AIT for Army). DOS is date of separation. Chow hall (dining facility). In the Marines there is no such thing as an ex-marine. Once a Marine always a Marine. Even though Marines work closely with the Navy they are not in the Navy and no do not like to be referred to as Navy. The numbers in front of a title, such as “E-4 Petty Officer” refer to pay grade. There is a narrow window of time, when you first enlist, in which you can say “I want out” and leave without penalty.
