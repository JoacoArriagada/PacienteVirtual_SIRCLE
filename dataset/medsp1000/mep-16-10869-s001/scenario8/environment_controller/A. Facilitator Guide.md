**Case B. Duchenne muscular dystropy**

**Case 4B. A very active, 3-year-old boy with mild hypotonia comes for genetics evaluation due to his diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder.  Due to what appears to be isolated autism spectrum disorder (ASD), he undergoes standard testing for genetic causes of ASD including fragile X syndrome and microarray analysis.  He is found to have a deletion of the DMD gene to the surprise of all.**

* + *Note the hypotonia, autism spectrum disorder, and young age at which more severe muscle signs (calf hypertrophy) may not yet be apparent.*
* + *DMD has an X-linked inheritance pattern as well as a high chance of a sporadic mutation leading to this condition.*
  + *Gather family history focused not only on males with severe muscular dystrophy, but women with fatigue/decreased exercise endurance, cardiomyopathy, and unexplained use of a wheelchair in distant relatives.*
* *The family may be in shock and not ready to talk in detail about the implications of DMD for other members of the family. There is no urgency for testing at this visit, unless the mother is pregnant or planning a pregnancy in the very near future.*
* + *50-65% of DMD is due to deletions/duplications detectable on microarray. Once found to be due to a deletion, more specific testing can be done, as microarray is often more expensive, but also may be more readily available in some centers.*
  + *A creatine kinase (CK) level, if significantly elevated, can help get a quicker sense of risk for the mother. If she is already pregnant, an echocardiogram may be indicated, as dilated cardiomyopathy is the most serious finding in carrier women and can worsen in pregnancy.*
* *If carried by the mother, future pregnancies have a 25% chance of being an unaffected male, 25% affected male, 25% carrier female, and 25% non-carrier female.*
