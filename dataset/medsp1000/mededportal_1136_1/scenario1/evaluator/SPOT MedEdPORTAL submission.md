<u>**Standardized Patient Oriented Teaching (SPOT)**</u>

<u>**BACKGROUND**</u>

Communication skills are a critical component of medical education and have been stressed by the American Association of Medical Colleges (AAMC) and the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME). The AAMC Project on the Clinical Education of Medical Students lists both basic and advanced competencies. The AAMC lists end-of-life communication, interviewing culturally diverse patients, and assessing risk of violence.  The LCME in the Functions and Structure of a Medical School clearly states that students must be taught communications skills (ED-19) and that the curriculum includes common societal problems (ED-20). This education must be done with the recognition that gender and cultural biases will affect health care delivery (ED-22).

Annually in the United States, almost one million women are victims of IPV, and 1,800 victims are murdered by their partner (USDJ). At Tulane University, formal lectures on intimate partner violence were instituted during the first year medical school curriculum.  The instruction included a mock patient being evaluated for intimate partner violence related injuries.  The co-investigator on this proposal, Dr. Houry, was involved with this curriculum and conducted follow-up studies on the long-term effectiveness and retention of this instruction. (Ernst, Ernst)  Overall, 71% of students who participated in the curriculum were more likely to diagnose domestic violence in a patient during their clinical rotations than students who had not participated in the course (52%).

Research has found that lap/shoulder safety belts, when used, reduce the risk of fatal injury to front-seat passenger car occupants by 45 percent and the risk of moderate-to-critical injury by 50 percent. (NTSA 2004)  During 2003, 8,089 passenger vehicle occupants under 15 years old were involved in fatal crashes. For those children, where restraint use was known, 30 percent were unrestrained; among those who were fatally injured, 53 percent were unrestrained. ([<u>NHTSA, 2004</u>](http://www-nrd.nhtsa.dot.gov/pdf/nrd-30/NCSA/TSF2003/809762.pdf)) It is estimated that 446 children under age 5 were saved as a result of child restraint use in 2003.  An estimated 7,020 lives were saved by child restraints from 1975-2003.  ([<u>NHTSA, 2004</u>](http://www-nrd.nhtsa.dot.gov/pdf/nrd-30/NCSA/TSF2003/809765.pdf))

Delivering bad news is a difficult task for medical practitioners. Whether this is a diagnosis of cancer, miscarriage, losing ones eyesight or death disclosure it occurs on a daily basis for almost all practicing clinicians.  Concerning is the fact that the majority of practicing physicians have received little or no formal training in communicating bad news. (Baile)(Vetto) In work done at our institution using a standardized patient (SP) model to teach death disclosure, only 17 or 37 students reported prior teaching on how to deliver a death disclosure. (Quest)  Our current model of learning through experience is ineffective and studies indicate that the way bad news is delivered will affect a patient’s short and long term adjustment to illness. (Maguire). Several studies have assessed the role of SP’s in teaching delivery of bad news and (Rosenbaum) (Garg) but little evidence exists that training using SP’s improves a student’s ability to deliver bad news effectively.

There currently exist societal and organizational mandates to provide medical students with an education on communication skills, deliverance of bad news, counseling skills, training in recognizing and treating societal problems all in a context of multi-cultural world. Use of SP’s provides a unique opportunity to teach communication skills in these difficult public health encounters. To our knowledge, this would be the first novel and innovative SP based curriculum which integrates communication skill education with critical medical topics not routinely taught in medical school courses. This curriculum can be adapted by other institutions to train medical students, nursing students, residents, and other health care providers. Cases can be changed to highlight a specific disease process keeping the general format that highlights effective communication skills.

<u>**IDENTIFYING CASE FACTORS**</u>

**Presenting complaint**

Mr. Smith is a 45 year old man who presents to the Emergency Department for a bicycle collision.

**Length of patient encounter**

History, physical, and management – 5-10 minutes

Communication of bad news – 5-10 minutes

Discussion 15-20 minutes

**Learning objectives/case purpose**

By the end of the lecture and standardized patient session students will be able to:

1. Intimate Partner Violence
    1. Describe the importance of understanding IPV
    2. Cite the statistical estimates for IPV
    3. Describe how to assess, manage, and treat cases of IPV
    4. List IPV resources
2. Injury Control and Prevention
    1. Define the public health approach
    2. Differentiate between primary, secondary and tertiary prevention
    3. Define the three “E’s” of injury prevention
3. Communication skills
    1. Know why communication of “bad” news is important
    2. Understand the 6-step protocol for delivering bad news

**Target group**

This material was specifically designed for fourth year medical students during their emergency medicine clerkship.

Other potential audiences include:

Medical students

Residents

Hospital chaplains

<u>**CASE SUMMARY/SP TRAINING NOTES**</u>

**SP demographics**

Age: 45 <u>+</u> 10 years

Gender: Male

Ethnicity: Any

BMI: Normal to Slightly Overweight

**Setting**

Emergency Department

**History of present complaint**

You are a 45 year old man who was hit by a car today.  You have been taken by ambulance to the Emergency Department for evaluation.  Your major injury is a mangled left leg but you don’t know it as you haven’t really seen what your leg looks like.  You know it’s injured but do not know the extent.  You are on a backboard and in a neck collar.

The student will talk to you, examine you, and tell you what further exams and tests he/she would like to order.  He/She will inform you of results, talk to you about bicycle safety and explain to you that you need surgery and that you might have to have a leg amputation.

You were riding your bicycle at around dusk without a helmet and without any safety lights.  You were to the right of a car in the right lane.  The car suddenly began to make a right turn and hit you.  You fell off the bike and recall that your left leg was run over by a tire.  You did not lose consciousness.  You have neck pain and left leg pain (10/10) as well as feeling sore all over.  No other pain in any extremities.  No abdominal pain, chest pain, or shortness of breath.  You last meal was lunch, about 4 hours ago.

**Physical examination findings**

Tenderness over the back of your neck over the spine.

Leg Exam - Mangled extremity with extensive soft tissue damage.  Ligaments, muscle, bone, nerves, and vessels all present on inspection.  Significantly diminished distal pulses. (Appendix A)

No chest wall tenderness.

No abdominal tenderness.

No other back tenderness.

Rectal Exam **-** Normal tone. Prostate in normal position.  No blood visualized. (Not to be performed)

**Diagnosis**

Comminuted open tibia/fibula fracture that cannot be salvaged.

**Management plan** 

After evaluation by orthopedic surgery they have recommended amputation and booked the OR.

**Challenges/Responses**

First time you are told about the amputation:

“No, you can not take off my leg”

If they do not mention that the orthopedic surgeon will review the reasons for the amputation:

“I need to speak to the doctors that are going to do this. Is there anything else that can be done to save the leg?”

Positive response if they tell you why amputation is necessary and important. They may mention lack of blood supply and in particular the risk of infection and inability to walk.

“I guess that makes sense. I still want to speak to the orthopedic surgeon before they put me to sleep.”

If they do not give you pain medications after the initial history and physical.

“Doctor, the leg is hurting me a lot. Can I get something for pain?”

**Powerpoint lecture** (Appendix C)

The lecture can be provided to the student in advance for independent study or presented by faculty.

<u>Stage 2</u>

**Standardized Patient Session**	

During the specialized SP day specially trained faculty will serve as preceptors.  Their role will involve:

1. Observing the encounter,
2. Providing immediate feedback to the students. Feedback is based upon faculty observation of communication skills, and discussion of diagnosis and plan with the patient, and
3. Facilitating a discussion and feedback from the SP

**SP Education**

SP’s will be hired and trained to properly portray the patient in each encounter. They will receive additional training in evaluating communication skills and receive case based education.
