SPECIAL NOTES: Medical Student Year 1

This exercise is designed for demonstration of the standardized patient encounter for a group of approximately 15 students in a classroom setting. The standardized patient and one facilitator are seated in the front of the classroom. The class does not participate in the actual standardized patient encounter. However, students observe and take notes of the interaction, which lasts 20 minutes. Afterword, they practice typing a SOAP note in a timed manner to simulate a testing experience similar to the USMLE $\textcircled{8}$ Step 2 Clinical Skills Exam. Students are instructed to focus on completing the History of Present Illness (HPI) and Physcial Examination (PE) portions of the note. They receive 15 minutes to do this. Students submit their notes via e-mail to the instructor, preferably in real time via a wireless Internet. They later receive written feedback. The facilitator then presents an example of a SOAP note from the encounter and allows the students to compare it with their notes.

# Medical Student Year 2

This exercise allows for the participation of small groups consisting of 6-7 students in the interview and physical examination portions of the standardized patient encounter, which comprise 20 minutes combined. The students are assigned roles in the activity, i.e.: each student receives an index card indicating a different assignment, such as HPI, PMH, etc. They take notes throughout the encounter. Afterword, they practice typing a SOAP note in a timed manner to simulate a testing experience similar to the USMLE $\textcircled{8}$ Step 2 Clinical Skills Exam. Students are instructed to focus on completing the HPI, PE and assessment portions of the note, providing two to three most likely diagnoses. They receive 15 minutes to do this. Students submit their notes via e-mail to the instructor, preferably in real time via a wireless Internet. They later receive written feedback. The facilitator then presents an example of a SOAP note from the encounter and allows the students to compare it with their notes.

# Medical Student Year 3

In this exercise, students interview and and perform the physical examination portions of a standardized patient encounter independently, with a time limit of 15 minutes. They take notes throughout the encounter. Afterword, they practice typing a SOAP note in a timed manner to simulate a testing experience similar to the USMLE $\textcircled{8}$ Step 2 Clinical Skills Exam. Students are instructed to focus on completing all portions of the note, including two to three most likely diagnoses and a prioritized plan. They receive 10 minutes to do this. Students submit their notes via e-mail to the instructor, preferably in real time via a wireless Internet. They later receive written feedback.

# Medical Student Year 4

This exercise transforms a fourth-year medical student from the role of a learner to that of an educator/role model. The student demonstrates a standardized patient encounter for a group of approximately 15 students in a classroom setting. The standardized patient and facilitator (fourth-year student) are seated in the front of the classroom. The class does not participate in the actual standardized patient encounter. However, students observe and take notes of the interaction, which lasts 20 minutes. Afterword, they practice typing a SOAP note in a timed manner to simulate a testing experience similar to the USMLE $\textcircled{8}$ Step 2 Clinical Skills Exam. Students are instructed to focus on completing the History of Present Illness (HPI) and Physcial Examination (PE) portions of the note. They receive 15 minutes to do this. Students submit their notes via e-mail to the instructor, preferably in real time via a wireless Internet. They later receive written feedback. The facilitator then presents an example of a SOAP note from the encounter and allows the students to compare it with their notes.

# BILL THOMPSON

# PROFILE

Bill Thompson is a 67 year-old man who comes to the clinic this morning complaining of hearing loss. You have noticed decreased hearing in both ears for the past six months. It seems to be getting worse slowly over time. You feel like you have to strain to hear in crowded or noisy environments, like a busy restaurant.

You are happily married to your wife of 45 years. You are a former smoker and started smoking one pack of cigarettes per day at age 17 but quit 10 years ago, when your father was diagnosed with lung cancer. You drink a few beers per week and do not use illicit drugs.

You are a retired airplane mechanic, a profession you worked in for nearly 40 years. There was a lot of exposure to loud noise at work. In the early part of your employment, ear protection was not required and you did not use any. However, you started using earplugs over the last 15-20 years of your career. # PHYSICAL EXAM

Cranial nerve exam – penlight is shined in front of each eye with equal pupillary response, which is communicated to you by the doctor, sensation on face is assessed (you indicate it is the same on both sides), you are able to close eyes and follow the examiner’s fingers without difficulty, smile, turn your head side-to-side, shrug shoulders, hear light sound equally in both ears

Ear Exam – Your ears are examined with an otoscope and normal findings are communicated to you

# Weber Test

A vibrating tuning fork is placed in the middle of the forehead or on top of the head, equidistant from the ears, and patient is asked if he can hear sound equally in both ears. You indicate both ears perceive the sound equally.

# Rinne Test

A vibrating tuning fork is placed behind the ear (along mastoid process) until the patient indicates that the sound has stopped. Then, the tuning fork is immediately placed in front of the ear. Patient is asked which location was louder. You indicate the sound is louder in front of the ear on the right. When the left ear is being tested, you indicate that the sound is louder behind the left ear.

# NEXT STEPS

The following assessment and plan is communicated to you by the doctor:

“Considering the significant amount of time you were exposed to loud noises through your occupation, sometimes using ear protection but other times not, I am favoring a diagnosis called sensorineural hearing loss. This is hearing loss that occurs as the result of damage to the specialized hearing cells in the ear. Smoking can also damage hearing cells, so I am glad that you quit. Sensorineural hearing loss may worsen but usually at a slow rate. Although it cannot be reversed, avoidance of certain things like loud noise can help prevent further loss. It is often treated with hearing aides. However, there is another type of hearing loss, called conductive hearing loss, which may explain your symptoms. This can be the result of hardening or rupture of the eardrum. As I mentioned, your ear exam was normal in appearance but I was not able to evaluate the function of your eardrum. Finally, in rare instances, growths around the ear canal can also cause this problem. Because your hearing tests were inconclusive, I would recommend a consultation with an ear, nose and throat specialist who can help decide if further testing should be performed. You will likely need a formal hearing test and this can be arranged as well. What questions do you have?”

You have no further questions and indicate that you are looking forward to finding out the cause of your problem.
