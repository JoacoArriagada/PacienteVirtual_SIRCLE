Notes and Definitions:

* 1. WHICH CUES: “Which cues do I mark?” Please mark “significant changes” in your cueing for each context. For example, you might mark your first subtle cue, and then mark the next different subtle cue that you give. Similarly, you mark your first obvious cue and different obvious cues that follow. Subtle and obvious cues might be differentiated by progressing from non-verbal to paraverbal to verbal, for example, or you might choose an alternate non-verbal behavior or verbal comment. **Try to capture when you make noticeably different advancements in the cuing, either because you are demonstrating more obvious cues or because you are using completely different behaviors.** Do not mark repeated cues or slight increases in obviousness. For each cue you mark in each context, you should choose a number that indicates whether the student followed up or not.
  2. FOLLOWING UP: “How will I know if the student followed up?” **The student’s behavior in response to your cue should have encouraged you to provide more information, given you the impression that the student wanted to know more, or that the student was considering the implications of what you were revealing.** Students might follow-up verbally, such as asking a question or making a supportive statement, or they might use non-verbal behaviors to follow up, such as shifting their body language or pausing and/or using paraverbal language (uh-uh, grunts, etc.) to encourage you to continue speaking. Note that is not the behavior itself that matters, but how it made you feel; for example, consider the difference in a “pause” that creates space to talk vs. an “awkward silence,” or the difference in an open-ended question or a closed-ended question with inflection to suggest that you keep talking vs. a closed-ended question that stops the line of conversation. If you think a student recognized the cue, but didn’t know what to do, please still code that as “not followed up.”
  3. HOW MANY CUES: “How many cues do I mark?” We do not want you to mark every cue you give. Remember, if the student doesn’t get the first few subtle cues, you should have been moving on to more obvious ones, so you may have only a few subtle cues. And if the student didn’t get the obvious ones, you should have moved on to other context, so you may have only a few obvious cues in each context as well. Further, if a student follows up a cue well, you may not have to give many more. The goal is to capture how you think you advanced the cuing within each context.
