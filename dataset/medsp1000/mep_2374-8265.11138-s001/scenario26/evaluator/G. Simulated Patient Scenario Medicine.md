Just prior to the events of this case, the patient develops worsening nausea/vomiting and abdominal pain, which should prompt a face-to-face evaluation by the learner. The learner should recognize a surgical abdomen, obtain emergent surgical consultation, and perform initial stabilizing interventions for sepsis.  Learner goals are to recognize concerning symptoms based on a nurse report prompting a face-to-face evaluation, diagnose acute perforated diverticulitis, call an appropriate consultation for a surgical emergency, and perform appropriate initial management of sepsis. |

Describe the management for perforated viscus, including appropriate surgical consultation. 4. Perform appropriate initial stabilizing interventions for a patient in septic shock, such as IV fluids and antibiotic administration. |

Ideal Scenario Flow

*Provide a detailed narrative description of the way this case should flow if participants perform in the ideal fashion.*

The learners arrive at the patient’s bedside and discover that he is acutely ill, with fever, tachycardia, hypotension, and findings of an acute surgical abdomen. On history, they elicit that the patient had acutely worsening pain, beginning an hour ago. They verbalize their thought process to the nurse confidant or the facilitator operating the scenario throughout the case, and provide reassurance and updates to the patient.

Learners should take immediate steps to stabilize the patient, who is hemodynamically unstable. Good initial interventions would include IV crystalloid bolus, ensuring continuation of antibiotics, and drawing repeat labwork, including pre-operative labs. They should recognize that the patient’s unstable vital signs limit their ability to administer narcotic pain medications or transport the patient for advanced imaging. A portable upright chest X-ray will demonstrate free air underneath the diaphragm, consistent with a perforated viscus.

Based on imaging and physical examination findings, learners should call for a surgery consultation, and advocate for the patient to go to the operating room. They should update their senior resident or attending as well.

Anticipated Management Mistakes

*Provide a list of management errors or difficulties that are commonly encountered when using this simulation case.*

1. Learners may forget initial resuscitation techniques including establishing IV access, administering IV fluids, placing the patient on a monitor, or even examining the patient
2. Learners may not recognize a deterioration in patient condition
3. Learners may fail to differentiate the initial diagnosis of diverticulitis with the current/new concern for perforation
4. Learners may downplay the patient’s condition and simply focus on pain management
5. Occasionally learners will consult the surgical service simply because of worsening abdominal pain without a specific question or concern. The surgical service will give the learner a difficult time about the consult unless the learner specifically addresses the concern for perforation. The suggested prompts are above.

Debriefing Guide

1. Reactions and reflections
   1. Start with open ended questions: How did you feel about the case? What went well?
2. Learner summary of the scenario to clarify facts of the case
3. Learning points
   1. Approach to initial resuscitation techniques1
      1. Airway, Breathing, Circulation assessment
         1. Airway - assess if the patient can speak or for airway obstructions
         2. Breathing- breath sounds, secondary indicators such as cyanosis
         3. Circulation - pulse assessment, skin color, capillary refill
      2. IV, O2, Monitor
         1. IV placement - is the IV access sufficient at this time?
         2. O2- does the patient require oxygen therapy at this time?
         3. Monitor - obtain the vital signs and monitoring required
      3. Identify abnormal vital signs
      4. Differential diagnosis2,3
         1. Acute abdomen ddx: perforation vs. ischemia vs. aortic pathology
         2. Diffuse abdominal pain ddx: small bowel obstruction vs. large bowel obstruction vs. colitis vs. inflammatory bowel disease vs. peritonitis
         3. Non-abdominal etiologies: sickle cell crisis, porphyria, diabetic ketoacidosis
         4. Diagnosis of exclusion: anxiety vs. continued pain vs. gastroenteritis
      5. Recognize sepsis due to perforated viscus4
         1. SIRS criteria
         2. QSOFA as a predictor of mortality
   2. Approach to suspected perforated viscus2,3
      1. Imaging
         1. CT vs X-ray imaging
            1. Upright CXR has greater sensitivity than upright KUB
            2. Consider stability of the patient for transport
         2. POCUS (point of care ultrasound)
            1. Not available in this case
            2. Briefly discuss how it may change management
