**Ideal Scenario Flow**

*Provide a detailed narrative description of the way this case should flow if participants perform in the ideal fashion.*

The learners approach the parent, who is holding the infant, attempting to get past the nurse or security guard to leave the unit. The parent is insistent about leaving the unit with her/his infant. The patient’s nurse is escalating the situation by invalidating the parent’s concerns and threatening to call Child Protective Services.

The learners should approach calmly and ask the parent to describe her/his concerns, employing open body language, minimal interruptions, and de-escalation techniques. The parent should state that nothing is happening other than the nurse is bothering the infant, and they do not understand the point of being admitted when they have so much going on at home.

The learners should explain the medical reasons for the admission (continued observation for changes in clinical exam, ongoing empiric antibiotic administration, follow-up on culture results) and the potential consequences of not treating (worsening infection, morbidity, or mortality). The learners should continue to calmly redirect the parent back to the room and invite the parent to sit down. The learners should elicit other parental concerns, which include frequent vital sign checks, concern about remaining children at home, and lack of support items, which the learners will agree to working with the nurse to collect. The parent agrees to stay.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

*Provide a list of management errors or difficulties that are commonly encountered when using this simulation case.*

1. Learner may not recognize escalating behaviors
2. Learner may not recognize nonverbal cues
3. Learner may lack knowledge around de-escalation techniques
4. Learner may fail to listen to the parent’s needs
5. Learner may lack medical knowledge around the care of a neonate with fever
6. Some learners may feel upset or be triggered by the scenario. In these situations, the facilitator should interrupt the case, break the scene, and discuss de-escalation techniques

**Debriefing Guide**

1. Reactions and reflections

Start with an open ended question: How did you feel about this case? What went well?

1. Learner summary of the scenario to clarify facts of the case
2. Approach to the difficult parent/patient1
   1. Partner with team approach, including social work, nurses, ancillary staff, consultants (if needed)
   2. Improve communication
      1. Active listening, including interrupting less, summarizing parents’ concerns/points, reconcile conflicts about severity of problem
      2. Partner with parent/patient
      3. Employ empathy
      4. Negotiate the process of care, including responsibilities of each party involved
3. Discharge against medical advice in pediatric patients2
   1. Reasons
      1. Perceived improvement of child illness
      2. Unsatisfactory treatment and care, most likely due to poor communication between physician and parent
      3. Inconvenience for child hospitalization
      4. Financial constraints
   2. Better communication between physician and parent/patient can improve overall impression of hospitalization and limit discharges against medical advice
4. Evaluation and management of a neonate with fever3
   1. Clinical background
      1. Increased risk of serious bacterial infections (up to 15%)
      2. Infants are more difficult to assess clinically than older children
   2. Recommended complete laboratory evaluation
      1. Blood: complete blood count, blood culture, Herpes simplex virus (HSV) PCR, HSV culture, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein
      2. Urine: urinalysis, urine culture
      3. Cerebrospinal fluid: cell count, glucose, protein, Gram stain, CSF culture
      4. HSV surface cultures of eyes, nose, mouth and rectum
   3. Management
      1. Empiric antibiotics: ampicillin and ceftazidime
      2. Empiric antivirals: acyclovir
