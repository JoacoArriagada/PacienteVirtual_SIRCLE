Appendix E: *Simulation Case 2 and Facilitator Guide*

Case Title: *Routine Diabetes Management with a Deaf Patient, Certified Deaf Interpreter, and American Sign Language Interpreter*

Case Authors: Shannon Zhou, BS; Marcus Milani, BS, MA; Morgan McBride, BS; Hope Pogemiller, MD, MPH

Standardized Patient Educator: Morgan McBride, BS

Date: 03/06/2025

Name of Educational Activity: Clinical Skills Simulation: Communication

Chief Complaint: “I’ve had diabetes for years, but I want to make sure I’m on the right track.”

Main Question: What are some key communication considerations when working with a Certified Deaf Interpreter (CDI) and an American Sign Language (ASL) interpreter in a medical encounter, and how might these impact your approach to discussing complex topics like diabetes management?

Case Objectives:

1. Work effectively with a CDI-ASL interpreter team, using appropriate pacing, turn-taking, and patient eye contact
2. Learn the indications for using a Certified Deaf Interpreter in addition to an ASL interpreter
3. Recognize the optimal set up with the CDI-ASL team (ASL interpreter on the same side as patient, CDI and student on the same side facing the ASL interpreter and patient)
4. Use plain language and visual aids to explain diabetes management and confirm understanding (e.g. teach-back method)
5. Hone non-verbal communication skills to improve interactions with Deaf/Hard-of-Hearing patients
6. Collaborate with patients to address concerns and adapt the plan to their lifestyle.

Ideal Scenario Flow

The learners enter the outpatient clinic and recognize that the encounter involves both an ASL interpreter and a CDI working with a Deaf patient (information provided on door instructions). The learner introduces themselves directly to the patient, confirms the interpreters’ roles, and maintains appropriate eye contact and pacing throughout the visit. They communicate in short, clear sentences, pausing for interpretation, and use plain language, drawings, or visual aids to review diabetes management, medication adherence, and neuropathy symptoms. Learners employ teach-back methods through the interpreters to confirm understanding and respond empathetically to the patient’s concerns about new medications, risks, and lifestyle challenges. They demonstrate cultural humility by collaborating with the CDI-ASL team, validating the patient’s experiences, and ensuring comprehension before closing the visit with a clear, jointly developed management plan and appropriate follow-up.

Anticipated Management Mistakes

1. Management of pacing: Learners may initially need time to adjust pacing with the ASL-CDI team. CDI and ASL Interpretation often occurs simultaneously, with CDI lagging slightly behind ASL interpretation. Learners should take appropriate pauses between sentences, consciously regulating the pace of conversation to allow adequate time for interpretation and patient processing. Students should monitor the patient’s and interpreters’ nonverbal cues to determine whether the pace is comfortable and, if uncertain, may ask the interpreters directly if the pacing is appropriate. Feedback on pacing and timing will also be provided during the post-simulation debrief to reinforce best practices for communication in interpreted encounters.
