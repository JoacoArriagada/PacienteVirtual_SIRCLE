| MANAGEMENT OR DIAGNOSTIC PLAN | The patient is seeking long-acting reversible contraception and does not plan to have children in the near future. She prefers a method that does not require daily or somewhat frequent attention, ruling out options like the birth control pill, ring, and patch. She is open to hormonal methods but is concerned about potential weight gain, which makes the Depo-Provera injection less appealing.  Given her history of heavy menstrual bleeding, the copper IUD is not an appropriate option due to the risk of worsening flow and cramping. While hormonal IUDs are an acceptable option in theory, the patient expresses significant anxiety about insertion pain and would prefer to avoid intrauterine devices.  After a shared decision-making conversation, the etonogestrel implant (Nexplanon) emerges as the preferred option. It aligns with her desire for a low-maintenance, long-acting method, has the potential to reduce menstrual bleeding, and is not commonly associated with weight gain. Additionally, Nexplanon offers a quick and simple in-office insertion without requiring instrumentation of the uterus, addressing her concerns about pain and invasiveness. |

Ideal Scenario Flow

The learners enter the outpatient clinic and recognize that the encounter involves a DeafBlind patient and ClearSpeech Interpreter(s). The learner introduces themselves directly to the patient, confirms the interpreters’ roles, and maintains appropriate pacing throughout the visit. As the discussion shifts to birth control options, the learner uses tactile models from the demonstration bin to explain each method (feeling the size and texture of a pill pack, ring, or implant), while narrating what the patient is touching to support comprehension. The existence of the tactile models are listed on the door instructions. Learners highlight reliability, side effects, and benefits relevant to the patient’s stated concerns (e.g., lighter periods, minimal weight change, low maintenance). Learners check for understanding frequently, invite the patient to share their preferences, and address fears. Using shared decision-making, the learner guides the patient to a contraceptive plan that aligns with their needs and comfort level, arriving at a mutual decision.

Anticipated Management Mistakes

This simulation ran relatively smoothly without management issues. We list some common learner mistakes below.

1. Management of pacing: Learners may initially need time to adjust pacing with the ClearSpeech Interpreter, who speaks into an FM system that links to the patient’s hearing aids. DeafBlind patients may alternatively use Protactile signing, which would employ the use of Protactile Interpreters (pacing similar to ASL interpreter). Learners should take appropriate pauses between sentences, consciously regulating the pace of conversation to allow adequate time for interpretation and patient processing. Students should monitor the patient’s and interpreters’ nonverbal cues to determine whether the pace is comfortable and, if uncertain, may ask the interpreters directly if the pacing is appropriate. Feedback on pacing and timing will also be provided during the post-simulation debrief to reinforce best practices for communication in interpreted encounters.
2. Announcing Speakers’ Name: When there are multiple learners, it can be helpful to announce your name prior to speaking (e.g. “This is Jane; how long have you been having pain?”). This practice helps orient the DeafBlind patient by clarifying who is communicating and supports smoother interpretation.
3. Tactile Teaching: Students may make the mistake of verbally explaining contraceptive options without providing the patient an opportunity to tactically explore the models. For instance, simply stating, “These are some contraceptive options,” is not meaningful for a patient who cannot see them. Learners should instead ask if the patient would like to feel the models and with consent, place each model in the patient’s hands one at a time, describe what they are touching (e.g., “This is the implant; it’s about the size of a matchstick”), and allow time for the patient to feel the shape and texture while explaining its features.
