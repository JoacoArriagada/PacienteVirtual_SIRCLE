| CASE INFORMATION | |
| Chief Concern | The patient does not communicate with spoken English. When the students enter the room, the patient points to their right lower quadrant (RLQ) with a pained expression. Upon prompting, the patient writes on a piece of paper or their phone’s notes app, “Pain here [points to RLQ]. Started yesterday”. |
| Additional Concerns | Fear of hospitals and surgery. Requests medication to treat appendicitis. |
| THE PATIENT’S STORY: | “At first, it was a dull ache around my belly button, I thought maybe it was bad food. But a few hours ago, the pain suddenly moved down to my lower right side–so sharp I couldn’t stand up straight. It’s bad–like a 9/10. I can’t eat, can’t sleep, and even coughing makes me want to cry. I’ve never felt like this before. I’m scared, and it’s so hard to communicate without an interpreter, I can’t explain properly.”  “I’m terrified of hospitals, what if I need surgery? I don’t know how anesthesia works for Deaf people like me. Will I wake up? Can they explain things clearly after? I feel alone and frustrated.”  “I can’t work, can’t focus. I had to cancel my shift today. What if this ruins everything. I just want to know if there is a way to fix this without surgery? Waiting could make it worse, but I don’t know what to do.” |
| HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS: | |
| Onset | 6 hours; acute |
| Setting | The patient woke up with stomach pain that quickly worsened. |
| Duration | 6 hours |
| Time relationships | Constant pain |
| Location / Radiation | Pain started in the periumbilical region, and by the time the patient presents to the ED it has migrated to the RLQ. |
| Quality | Sharp, stabbing |
| Severity | 9/10 pain |
| Aggravated by what | Pressing (rebound tenderness), movement, hitting bumps in the road while riding in a car, coughing |
| Relieved by what | Nothing |
| Associated with what | Fever and nausea |
| Attitude (what does the patient think is the problem, and how do they feel about it) | The patient is unsure what is happening but suspects infection and wants the problem to be fixed with medication. |
| Overall course | This patient’s appendicitis began with vague periumbilical pain that migrated to the RLQ over a few hours, progressing to severe (9/10), constant sharp pain with nausea and a low grade fever. By presentation, they demonstrate classic RLQ tenderness with guarding and rebound pain, suggesting acute appendicitis. Without prompt surgery, the appendix may rupture. The lack of interpreter creates communication barriers requiring visual aids until an interpreter arrives in the final minutes (entering 5 minutes into the 11 minute encounter), while their surgery fears necessitate clear explanations about the urgency of operative management versus the dangers of delay. |

| PHYSICAL EXAM FINDINGS | |
| Exam in medical terms | Mild abdominal distension, involuntary guarding. Pain upon palpation with maximal tenderness at McBurney’s point. Rebound tenderness when palpation is lifted. Pain in the right lower quadrant when the left lower quadrant is palpated is demonstrated by Rovsing’s sign. Pain on passive extension of the right thigh shown by the psoas sign. Pain on internal rotation of the flexed hip shown by the obturator sign. |
| 1. Written in layperson’s terms | Upon inspection of the belly the medical student will look for swelling or tightness. They will gently press on different areas, starting in areas of minimal pain, and pay close attention to the lower right abdomen–the most common spot for appendicitis pain. If pressing on the left side makes the right side hurt, or if you feel sharp pain when they quickly release pressure, it could mean your appendix is inflamed. They might also move your right leg in certain ways to see if that causes pain. |
| 1. General appearance | Grimacing, holding abdomen, laying on the exam bed |
| 1. Vital signs | Temp 38°C, HR 90, BP 120/80 |
| 1. Specific findings and affect | Tenderness/rebound in RLQ, guarding |
| 1. Response to certain physical movements | Winces when palpated |
| DIAGNOSIS AND DIFFERENTIAL | |
| Diagnosis with support from positive and negative history and PE findings | The classic presentation includes periumbilical pain migrating to RLQ, tenderness at McBurney’s point with rebound and guarding. Positive Rovsing’s, psoas, or obturator sign further support the diagnosis. Fever and nausea may also support the suspicion. |
| Differential with support from positive and negative history and PE findings | The differential includes gastroenteritis, ovarian torsion, ovarian cysts, diverticulitis, urinary tract infection, and mesenteric adenitis. |
| MANAGEMENT OR DIAGNOSTIC PLAN | Urgent referral for abdominal/pelvic CT scan, labs (CBC, BMP, CRP), NPO, IV fluids, surgical consult. |
