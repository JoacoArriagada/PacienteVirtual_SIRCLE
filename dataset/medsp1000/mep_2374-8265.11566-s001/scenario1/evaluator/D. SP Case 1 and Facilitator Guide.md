Appendix D: *Simulation Case 1 and Facilitator Guide*

Case Title: *Appendicitis in a Deaf Patient*

Case Authors: Marcus Milani, BS, MS; Shannon Zhou, BS; Hope Pogemiller, MD, MPH

Standardized Patient Educator: Marcus Milani, BS, MS

Date: 03/06/2025

Name of Educational Activity: Clinical Skills Simulation: Communication and Diagnosis

Chief Complaint: “Severe stomach pain”

Main Question: How do you diagnose and explain appendicitis to a Deaf patient without (and later, with) an ASL interpreter?

Case Objectives:

1. Use non-verbal strategies (writing, gestures, images) to obtain history and explain diagnosis.
2. Hone non-verbal communication skills to improve interactions with Deaf/Hard-of-Hearing patients
3. Address fears about surgery using simplified language and visual aids.
4. Prioritize appendicitis in differential based on HPI and physical exam.

Most Likely Diagnosis and Differential With Rationale From History and/or Physical Exam:

The diagnosis is acute appendicitis due to pain migration from periumbilical region to the right lower quadrant (RLQ), RLQ tenderness, rebound pain, and guarding. The differential includes gastroenteritis, UTI, pyelonephritis, and ovarian pathology; however, the lack of diarrhea, dysuria, or vaginal pain suggests a different etiology. Please note that the focus of this case is on communication skills rather than differential or diagnosis.

Ideal Scenario Flow

The learners enter the simulated emergency department exam room and find a patient laying on the exam table, visibly uncomfortable and holding their abdomen. The patient does not communicate verbally at baseline and points to their ears and shakes their head, signifying that they are deaf. An ASL interpreter is on their way but not yet present (as indicated on the door instructions). Learners immediately recognize that there is a communication barrier and the urgency of the situation based on the patient’s anxious disposition and expressions of severe pain. Learners are already wearing a clear mask to facilitate nonverbal communication (provided during the classroom pre-brief). Students actively pursue alternative communication methods (e.g. using paper and pen to write/draw, using phone to type/dictate, pointing to areas of the body, using nonverbal facial expressions/gestures, etc.). After 5 minutes, the interpreter arrives. Learners continue to gather a history and perform a physical exam with the help of the interpreter. They look at the patient when speaking rather than the interpreter. As the clinical picture becomes clear, students should explain the need for surgical intervention and consider sketching a simple diagram or searching for an image online for a visual aid. Learners address any fears or concerns the patient may have.

Anticipated Management Mistakes

1. Difficulty communicating without an interpreter: While students had received a lecture on Deaf culture and communication methods, some required a brief adjustment period to recall and apply these techniques in practice. This challenge was anticipated and represents a central learning objective, encouraging learners to adapt and employ alternative communication methods when an interpreter is not immediately available.

Feedback from the SP emphasized that writing back and forth while in pain can be difficult and using a cellular device (e.g. typing/dictating into the Notes app or Google Translate app) may be easier to read and more comfortable for a patient lying down. Short, simple phrases were preferred over lengthy sentences. The SP also noted the importance of gesturing and making eye contact, and that knowing even a small amount of ASL (e.g. sign for pain) is incredibly helpful, though not required for the simulation. While several ASL phrases were introduced during the interactive lecture, facilitators may wish to include a brief refresher during the classroom pre-brief.

1. Timing of interpreter entry: Interpreters were instructed to set a timer for 4 minutes on their cellular device from the start of the simulation before entering the room. This timing allowed students to experience the challenge of initiating care without immediate interpreter access, while still ensuring practice with the help of an ASL interpreter. This went smoothly in our implementation. Facilitators who plan to replicate this scenario should ensure that clear instructions are provided to interpreters during the classroom pre-brief.
