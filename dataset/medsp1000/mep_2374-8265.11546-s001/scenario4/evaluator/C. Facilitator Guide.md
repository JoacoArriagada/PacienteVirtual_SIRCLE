**Code Status Discussions**

*for those with advanced illness*

1. **ESTABLISH THE SETTING.**
   1. Sit at eye level.
   2. Ask if patient wishes for others to be present.
   3. Introduce the subject and ask for permission to proceed:
      1. “I’d like to discuss something with you that I discuss with all patients I admit to the hospital. Is that okay?”
      2. “I’d like to talk with you about possible healthcare decisions in the future. Is that okay?”
2. **WHAT DOES THE PATIENT OR CAREGIVER UNDERSTAND?**
   1. “First, I like to be sure we’re on the same page.”
   2. Ask open-ended questions:
      1. “Can you tell me about your health?” or “What do you understand from what the teams have shared with you about your health?”
   3. If the patient is open to it, fill in any details that may be missing:
      1. “Is it okay if I share with you what I know?”
3. **WHAT IS IMPORTANT TO THEM IN THE CONTEXT OF THEIR ILLNESS?**
   1. “Have you ever thought of what you may want if you were to become sicker?”
   2. “What is important to you?”
4. **DISCUSS CODE STATUS.**
   1. Introduce the topic and provide a basic definition.
      1. “I want to talk to you about something called code status. This provides directions to your medical team about what we should or shouldn’t do if you were to unexpectedly become very ill and your heart stopped beating.”
   2. Ask what they know or may like to know about code status and explain in simple terms.
      1. “If a patient’s heart stops beating, we sometimes press firmly on their chest to pump blood through their body, use electricity to shock their heart back into beating, and place a breathing tube in their windpipe.”
      2. “Unfortunately, this does not always lead to recovery or survival. In fact, only about 1 in 7 people survive CPR and are discharged from the hospital. Some of these may experience complications related to CPR, including permanent neurological and functional impairment.”
      3. Don’t be afraid to describe resuscitative efforts in the context of their illness! Survival is decreased with advanced illness.
      4. “Sometimes my patients will say they wouldn’t want these things, which is why I ask.”
5. **ESTABLISH A PLAN THAT ALIGNS WITH THE PATIENT’S GOALS.**
   1. Feel free to make a recommendation with their permission.
6. **SUMMARIZE YOUR DISCUSSION.**
   1. “We will continue to devote our energies towards your recovery here in the hospital. If your heart were to unexpectedly stop or your breathing worsen, instead of performing chest compressions or placing you on a breathing machine, we would make you comfortable at the end of your life. Does that sound right?”

**Don’t forget to respond to any emotion you encounter along the way!**

**This can be a difficult topic for patients and families!**

**Code Status Discussions**

*for those without advanced illness*

1. **NORMALIZE AND CONTEXTUALIZE THE CONVERSATION.**
   1. “There’s something I’d like to talk with you about – it’s something I ask all of my patients when they are first admitted to the hospital, no matter how healthy they are.”
2. **INTRODUCE CODE STATUS AND ASK WHAT THEY KNOW.**
   1. “It’s about something called code status, which provides direction to the medical teams about what patients may or may not want done should their heart stop beating or should they stop breathing.”
   2. “What have you heard about this term?”

*YOU MAY ENCOUNTER EMOTION, OR PATIENTS MAY BE CONFUSED AS TO WHY YOU’VE ASKED THIS QUESTION!*

1. **EMPATHIZE AND REASSURE.**
   1. “I can understand how this would be confusing and I don’t think it’s likely to happen. Some of my patients have strong opinions on this topic and I ask because it’s important I honor their wishes.”
   2. “I can imagine this is hard to talk about.”
2. **PROVIDE EDUCATION IF NEEDED AND USE SIMPLE TERMS.**
   1. “If a patient’s heart stops beating, we sometimes press firmly on their chest to pump blood through their body, use electricity to shock their heart back into beating, and place a breathing tube in their windpipe.”
   2. “Unfortunately, this does not always lead to recovery or survival like we see on TV. In fact, only about 1 in 7 people survives CPR and is discharged from the hospital.”
   3. “Sometimes my patients will say they wouldn’t want these things, which is why I ask.”
3. **LISTEN FOR THEIR RESPONSE.**
   1. “What are your thoughts?”
4. **SUMMARIZE THE PLAN.**
   1. “In the unlikely event that you were to stop breathing or your heart stop beating, you would like for our teams to perform CPR and place a breathing tube. I’ll be sure these preferences are reflected in the chart. In the future, should things change, we can always discuss again.”

**Peds Scenario #1: Discussing Code Status in the NICU**

Intern:

You are caring for Jade, a 10-day-old full term baby girl with a skeletal dysplasia. As part of her condition, she has a small thorax with abnormal ribs. Her family was prepared that she would very likely require respiratory support and were accepting of intubation if needed. She is now on the ventilator and clinically stable, though you know her hospitalization is likely to be prolonged and perhaps complicated. You have come to know her family over the past few days and after providing medical updates, wish to discuss code status.

Standardized Patient:

* You are the parent of Jade.
* You’re not sure what the term “code status” means and ask the intern to explain.
* As they do this, you become concerned saying you thought Jade’s condition had stabilized and are wondering if this means the medical team expects her to get sicker.
* After the intern responds to this emotion and concern, you calm down.
* You are fine with Jade being on a ventilator, but you worry that if her heart stops, doing chest compressions would cause her a lot of pain given how small her chest is.
* You decide you wouldn’t want Jade to have chest compressions, but would rather let her die peacefully if her heart stops.

Debrief:

1. Don’t forget to ask permission to discuss a very difficult topic.
2. How can you respond to emotion if the patient becomes scared by the discussion?
   1. *Example response:* It is important to acknowledge the emotion and ask permission to continue the discussion. For example, “I can tell this discussion is causing you some anxiety/stress/fear. Discussing code status is difficult. Is it ok if we continue with the discussion, or would you like to have this discussion at a different time?”
3. In pediatrics, based on the child’s underlying condition it may be possible to select some resuscitative interventions (like intubation or cardioversion) and not others (like chest compressions). It is very rare, however, to elect for chest compressions without intubation—this would not be recommended as it is very unlikely to be successful.
