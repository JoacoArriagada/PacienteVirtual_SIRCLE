*Three Adult Acute Abdominal Pain Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) Cases for Medical Student Assessment in the Surgery Clerkship: The Instructor’s Guide*

**Goal/Objectives:**

*Goal:* For medical students in the surgery clerkship to be able to approach adult patients with acute abdominal pain.

*Objectives:* By the end of the surgical clerkship, each medical student should be able to:

1. Perform a focused history for an adult patient with acute abdominal pain.

2. Perform a physical examination of the abdomen by looking, listening, and palpating.

3. Generate a list of three diagnoses for an adult patient with acute abdominal pain.

4. Write a list of three laboratory tests or diagnostic studies to be ordered for an adult patient with acute abdominal pain.

5. Interact professionally with an adult patient with acute abdominal pain.

**Conceptual Background:**

The United States Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE) Step 2 Clinical Skills (CS) Examination consists of patient encounters where students have 15 minutes to evaluate a patient and 10 minutes to write a note. This note includes a list of the case-specific differential diagnoses, and a list of further tests for evaluation. Testing material includes content from the USMLE Clinical Knowledge (CK) Examination such as nutritional and digestive disorders.

The Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) has proven to be a reliable and valid modality to assess the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) competencies of

patient care, medical knowledge, interpersonal and communication skills, and professionalism. The use of the OSCE for student assessment in the clinical clerkship is well-established.

This three-station acute abdominal pain OSCE is used for formative purposes in the Surgery and Perioperative Care Clerkship at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine. It is significant in that it serves as a tool to evaluate goals and objectives of the clerkship. Moreover, it parallels the USMLE Step 2 CS Examination with similar length of the patient encounter, the differential diagnosis list, and the initial patient work-up, while directly assessing multiple ACGME competencies.

These cases were written and revised by faculty members Board-Certified in general surgery. Standardized Patients were selected and trained by the Standardized Patient Program, a program coordinated through the Advanced Clinical Education Center and managed by the Office of Medical Education for the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine. The Standardized Patient Program is responsible for quality assurance and feedback-based improvement in OSCE delivery.

**Practical Implementation Advice:**

For an effective implementation of this formative three-station acute abdominal pain OSCE to medical students in the surgical clerkship, a few things are needed. First, available space is needed for delivery of this OSCE. Each student will need to be able to perform a focused history and physical examination in a patient-friendly room. A room for introduction of the exercise before the OSCE and a separate room for debriefing the medical students after the OSCE are also needed. (Having ancillary space for surgery residents to watch student-patient interactions is recommended.) Second, time away from clinical rotations is needed for the medical students to take this OSCE. Each patient encounter, including the clinical primer, history, and physical examination is allotted 15 minutes. The students then have five minutes to generate a differential diagnosis list of three items. Students should be provided with paper and clipboards for this exercise. Finally, a group of trained standardized patients is needed. Standardized patients need to be trained in the delivery of the history, the delivery of the physical examination, and the ability to evaluate medical student history behaviors, physical examination behaviors, and interpersonal behaviors.

In our institution, we have the following practice: We orient nine students at a time to the exercise. We have nine patient rooms available in our testing facility. (All rooms are equipped with cameras and microphones so surgery residents can give feedback on the patient encounter.) Three rooms are “Room A,” three rooms are “Room B,” and three rooms are “Room C.” Each of the nine standardized patients we use is assigned to one case presentation in one room. Each student goes into a Room A, a Room B, and a Room C so each student sees each case.

After orientation, students go to their assigned first case in the case sets. After completing the patient interaction portion (15 minutes) and written portion (5 minutes), they move to the next case. During the 5 minute break, standardized patients electronically fill out feedback forms. When all three cases are complete, these nine students meet in the debriefing room to discuss the cases. This exercise is currently used for formative feedback only. Each surgical clerkship typically contains enough students for three groups of nine. A typical schedule is as follows:

8:00 AM Group One Students Arrive

8:05 AM Orientation for Group One

8:10 AM Case 1

8:30 AM Case 2

8:50 AM Case 3

9:10 AM Group One Debriefing

9:10 AM Group Two Students Arrive

9:15 AM Orientation for Group Two

9:20 AM Case 1

9:40 AM Case 2

10:00 AM Case 3

10:20 AM Group Two Debriefing

10:20 AM Group Three Students Arrive

10:25 AM Orientation for Group Three

10:30 AM Case 1

10:50 AM Case 2

11:10 AM Case 3

11:30 AM Group Three Debriefing

*Personnel/Assignments:*

* Clerkship Coordinator: Gives orientation to the students
* Clerkship Director: Debriefs the students, and observes them
* 2-3 Surgery Residents: Give written feedback on interaction performance
* 9 Standardized Patients: Deliver the case script, physical examination, and fill out feedback forms
* 2 Standardized Patient Team Members: Orient the Standardized Patients, coordinate student movement, deal with room issues

**File Use:**

Standardized patients are to be professionally trained in delivering the case, and providing feedback based on the checklist. At no point do the medical students see the case document or the checklist. The content of the cases is discussed during the debriefing. The natures of the feedback is given to students in the form of a “learning prescription” after completion of the OSCE. The order of the case delivery is not important, as students are staggered at the start of these cases in our institutional practice.

The case primer should be attached to the door for the medical students to read and study before entering the room for the patient interaction.

**Successful Deployment:**

It should be noted that not all institutions have as many resources available for implementation of this three-station OSCE. While our institutional practice involves nine simultaneous standardized patients, other institutions may be able to successfully run medical students through a smaller scale of this three-station acute abdominal pain OSCE. For example, only three standardized patients could be used for all medical students. Additionally, not all of the cases need to be seen by each medical student. Also, medical students could see two patients with acute abdominal pain.

*Tips for Success:*

* Having reliable standardized patients
* Starting on time
* Keeping the students to the schedule
* Use of walkie-talkies to communicate to standardized patients and to announce time limits

**Resource Limitations:**

This OSCE exercise is very resource-intensive. It requires a lot of coordinated effort and the talent of a large number of standardized patients. Scaling down this exercise is certainly doable, and is much less resource-intensive.

Currently, this resource is used only for formative feedback and not summative evaluations. Given its similarity to the USMLE Step 2 CS Examination, the cases could be expanded to test more in the surgery domain. Understanding acute abdominal pain is a goal of the clerkship. However, it is the only surgical element tested. Expanding it to include other subject matter in the surgical realm would be useful.

We are currently performing research to see if standardized evaluations from this resource could be used to replace the unreliable evaluations in certain performance domains by faculty and service residents.
