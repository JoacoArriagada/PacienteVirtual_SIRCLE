# Debriefing Materials for Septic Abortion Case

# Evaluation of Low Abdominal Pain in a Female Patient

The emergency clinician must maintain a very systematic approach to the reproductiveaged female patient presenting with abdominal pain. The causes are many, although disorders of the gastrointestinal, urinary and gynecologic systems are key considerations.

Evaluation of an adolescent in the emergency department is different than that of an adult. Adolescence is a unique developmental stage and teens vary in their level of cognitive development. It is important to know your state’s laws regarding confidential care for minors. Ensuring confidentiality will increase willingness of patient to disclose information and seek health care. Parents or guardians must leave room. Screen patient’s social history using the HEADDSSS mnemonic: Home, Education, Activities, Diet/exercise, Depression, Sex/drugs, Safety, Spirituality/self.

Obtaining an initial urine qualitative pregnancy test (followed by, in some cases, a blood quantitative pregnancy test) is of utmost importance. The result of this initial diagnostic study is critical as it has both diagnostic (eg, choice of imaging modalities) and therapeutic (medication considerations during pregnancy) implications.

The finding of a positive pregnancy test in the setting of low abdominal pain (first trimester) is an ectopic pregnancy until proved otherwise. The routine approach to “ruling-out” ectopic pregnancy is to “rule-in” a normal intrauterine pregnancy (IUP). The finding of a normal IUP makes the diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy very unlikely, except for in patients using assisted reproductive technologies (ART) such as medical ovulation stimulation or in-vitro fertilization. Abdominal or pelvic sonography is a critical diagnostic study in assessing for the presence of an IUP.

Fever in first trimester pregnancy should similarly be evaluated in a systematic fashion. Causes of fever in pregnancy are many, and mirror the causes routinely encountered in non-pregnant females of similar age. Key considerations in the setting of fever and abdominal pain in the first trimester pregnant patient include urinary tract infection (UTI) and acute appendicitis, among many others.

Of important note, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is infrequently encountered during pregnancy. This is thought secondary to physiologic mucous-plugging of the cervical os, which provides some degree of protection against ascending infection. However, cervical/uterine instrumentation may precipitate an acute infection. The presence of cervical motion tenderness and puss-like discharge from the cervical os, although non-specific, are suspicious for uterine or tubal infection. In addition, an enlarged/diffusely tender uterus raises the suspicion for the possibility of endometrial infection, termed endometritis.

Although the frequency of self-induced (non-medical) cervical/uterine instrumentation is low, it may still be encountered in the ED setting (see references). The diagnosis of septic abortion is made primarily on clinical grounds. Gynecologic consultation is imperative, as is initiation of broad-spectrum IV antimicrobials. Systemic infection/sepsis may occur in rapid fashion. Maternal blood typing to determine maternal Rh status is essential, as treatment with RHO immune globulin (RhoGAM) is needed for Rh negative mothers.
