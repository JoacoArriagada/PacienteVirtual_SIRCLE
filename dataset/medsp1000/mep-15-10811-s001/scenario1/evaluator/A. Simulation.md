**Ideal Scenario Flow**

Prior to entering the room, the learners are told only that the patient transferred from an outside hospital with chief complaint of weakness. The learners should obtain pertinent history from the patient including the time course of progressive ascending weakness and paresthesias for the past week and more recent progression of bulbar and respiratory symptoms. The learners should perform a focused neurological exam including checking strength, sensation and deep tendon reflexes (the confederate registered nurse (RN) may verbally convey the finding of areflexia since this may be difficult to simulate). Learners should also perform exam maneuvers that are high yield for assessing neuromuscular respiratory strength (e.g. neck strength, breath counting). Pulmonary function tests including FVC and NIF should be given by confederate RN up request by the learners. Over the course of the simulation, the patient will develop progressive respiratory decline including worsening tachypnea and tachycardia, hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis on blood gas, and eventually hypoxia and obtundation. The learners are expected to recognize these signs and symptoms of acute neuromuscular respiratory failure due to AIDP and initiate urgent treatment with ventilator support. Bi-level non-invasive ventilation may be tried initially, but eventually they should escalate to endotracheal intubation and invasive mechanical ventilation. The case ends when the learners give “sign-out” to the intensive care unit (ICU) fellow prior to transferring patient to the ICU.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

Specific prompts and responses for anticipated management mistakes are outlined in the “Instructor notes- changes and branch points” section above. To summarize, they include:

* Failure to obtain pertinent history and physical. If the learner does not obtain the salient points of the history that suggest AIDP (i.e. subacute progressive weakness & numbness, associated with bulbar and respiratory symptoms, preceded by diarrheal illness ~4 weeks prior), then the simulated patient can volunteer this information to the learners. Likewise, if the learners do not elicit the pertinent exam findings, then the confederate RN can prompt or make suggestions (e.g. “did you test strength?”; “it looks like they couldn’t get reflexes at the outside hospital”).
* Failure to perform high-yield diagnostic testing for neuromuscular respiratory weakness. These include physical exam maneuvers (e.g. breath counting, neck strength), bedside spirometry (i.e. FVC, MIP, MEP), blood gas, and other diagnostics to rule out secondary causes of respiratory failure (e.g. complete blood count, chest x-ray). If the learners fail to pursue these important diagnostic studies, then the confederate RN may prompt them accordingly (e.g. “should I request RT to check PFTs and draw blood gas?”; “did you want to check any labs?”). Any elements that the learners fail to address during the simulation should be discussed in the debriefing session.
* Failure to recognize acute respiratory decompensation. If learners fail to recognize the patient’s progressive respiratory decline, then the simulation jockey should change the vitals displayed on the monitor to reflect the patient’s progressive hemodynamic instability (e.g. HR & RR increases, triggering the monitor to alarm). The confederate RN can verbally bring attention to the patient’s declining status. In addition, the standardized patient is instructed to develop progressive encephalopathy/obtundation (reflecting hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis), which should catch the learners’ attention. If the learners fail to recognize these signs in a timely manner, then this should be discussed in the debriefing session.
* Failure to initiate ventilator support. This is a pivotal action in the case. The learners may mistakenly treat with supplementary oxygen, which will not help the patient because neuromuscular respiratory failure is due to ventilation failure (as opposed to oxygenation failure). If the learner does not initiate ventilator support (non-invasive or invasive), then the standardized patient’s hemodynamics and respiratory status will decline. The confederate RN may suggest appropriate intervention if the learners do not generate on their own. This issue should also be discussed in the debriefing session after the simulation.

Many of these management mistakes by learners were a result of lack of knowledge and experience with the complexities of neuromuscular respiratory failure. Namely, senior residents likely had more experience with this topic compared the juniors. To address this, the topic of acute neuromuscular respiratory failure was reviewed by all the resident in a didactic session a couple weeks prior to the simulation, in an effort to equalize the learners’ baseline knowledge of this topic.
