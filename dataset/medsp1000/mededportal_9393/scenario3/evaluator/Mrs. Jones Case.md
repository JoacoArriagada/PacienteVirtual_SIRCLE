**Mrs. Jones**

**TRAINING MATERIALS for SP/CI**

**PRESENTING COMPLAINT:** Weakness

**DIAGNOSIS: Delirium** in the setting of infection and metastatic cancer

**PATIENT NAME: Lucille Jones**

**PATIENT DEMOGRAPHICS:**

**Age** 89

**Sex:** Female

**Race:** Caucasian or African American

**PROFILE:**

Mrs. Jones is an 89 year-old woman with metastatic lung cancer (has brain tumors in addition to lung tumors) who presents to the Emergency Room with weakness. She is brought to the Emergency Department by her **paid caregiver, Angie,** due to her profound weakness and concerns that she is seeing things/having hallucinations. This caregiver has since left and now Mrs. Jones’s adult child (son or daughter, biological or adopted) is at the bedside. This child lives out of town and drove several hours to be with Mrs. Jones in the Emergency Room. Mrs. Jones’s son/daughter has previously deferred to her to make decisions regarding her health, but right now is the first time that Mrs. Jones’s son/daughter is having to speak on her behalf. The child believes that Mrs. Jones does not see her oncologist very often and may have missed her last couple of appointments. She does not believe any treatment is being pursued for the cancer but she has not been involved in her care plan.

Mrs. Jones is divorced. She has one adult child (biological or adopted) who is her **health-care power of attorney.** The adult child has funds to support Mrs. Jones as needed. Although the child lives three hours away, she/he can take time off from work to help.

Mrs. Jones was very vigorous up into her mid-80s, working in her garden on a daily basis. She was diagnosed with metastatic cancer 1 year ago. Mrs. Jones currently has an aide, Angie, who helps bathe and dress her and spends the night with her. During most of the day, she is alone watching television and reading the paper, which to her is a **poor quality of life**. Lately, she has been **very lethargic and ill,** not eating or drinking very much and spending much of the day in bed, not watching TV or reading the paper. However, she has been fairly clear in her thinking until now.

**OBJECTIVES FOR LEARNERS:**

 To use the confusion assessment method to screen for delirium

 To effectively interview a surrogate decision maker

 To recognize that this presentation of delirium portends a poor prognosis for the patient

 To consider patient’s goals of care in decision-making

 To offer palliative care and/or Hospice should this be consistent with the patient’s wishes

 To recognize that pain may contribute to delirium

**BOTTOM LINE: There is not a “right” or “wrong” approach to this case. The goal is for the medical team to effectively communicate a treatment plan that meets the patients’ goals as outlined by the surrogate decision-maker.**

**PRESENTATION:**

**Patient:** Mrs. Jones should appear very weak and uncomfortable. She should alternate between appearing listless and appearing somewhat agitated, grabbing at things in the air as if she sees things, trying to get up or move from side to side in the bed. She should not be able to follow commands and conversation as she is very distracted by internal stimuli and unsettled. She should offer very few verbal comments (she may state, “I am ready to go home”); almost all verbal comments should be directed toward her family member. She should not engage with the physician despite the physician’s attempts to talk with her. She is in mild-to-moderate pain but is unable to communicate that effectively to her family or physician.

**Family:**

The adult child should appear concerned about Mrs. Jones comfort level. She/he knows that the cancer is a terminal illness but may need to be told that Mrs. Jones is currently end-of-life/very sick. The adult child should discuss with the medical team the plan of care, stating Mrs. Jones’s preference to be at home and not be hospitalized **if it won’t make her more comfortable or really change her prognosis**. He/she should state **desire to prioritize comfort** and palliation. **He/she should not have any desire for aggressive, life-prolonging measures**. The family thankfully has the resources to support the patient at home, such as hiring someone, taking time off from work, etc (it’s fine to be creative!). **The team may offer Hospice or Palliative Care as an option to keep her comfortable as an alternative to admission to the hospital.**

***Opening line:***

In response to the typical opening question from the learner, “What brings you to the hospital?”

The family member appears concerned, states, “Well, I got a call from Angie, Mom’s aide, that Mom is just really not doing well. Angie called EMS this morning when she couldn’t get Mom out of bed. Evidently, she hasn’t eaten anything for a couple of days and is really not herself.”

***Questions to anticipate:***

How do you think your mom is doing?

o I am really worried about her. I think she has really taken a turn.

How long has your mom been like this?

o I think this started a couple of days ago. I am not sure exactly as I live several hours from here but when I talked with Angie, Mom’s caregiver, day before yesterday she mentioned that Mom was having a bad day, wanting to just rest. She wasn’t really eating or drinking anything even though Angie was really pushing her to try to eat.

What other symptoms has she had?

o Again, I am not really sure, but I know that she seems more uncomfortable, like she is in pain from something. Angie didn’t tell me anything specifically was wrong, just that mom was weaker and more tired.

What is the status of your mom’s cancer treatment/cancer diagnosis?

o Well, I know that she has kind of stopped going to the oncologist. She doesn’t really want to go back because she knows that it’s terminal and she’s never really liked going to the doctor or being at the hospital.

Does your Mom have an advanced directive/HCPOA/living will/etc?

o I am Mom’s HCPOA. When Mom was diagnosed a year ago, we talked a lot about her care, and she really does not want any aggressive care. Her goals are to be kept comfortable and be at home when her time comes.

***Example questions to ask team:***

What are our options?

What would help Mom be most comfortable?

What can I do to help?

**SPECIFIC SUGGESTIONS FOR FACILITATOR FEEDBACK SESSION FOR MRS. JONES**

*What is your diagnosis?* Delirium

*Predisposing factors for delirium*
terminal illness, functional impairment, elderly, bed rest

*Precipitating factors for delirium?*
Infection/sepsis, dehydration, pain?, metastatic disease, electrolyte abnormalities, renal insufficiency

*Suggestions for management plan*
discuss goals of care with family
identify surrogate decision maker or HCPOA
refer to patient’s PCP or primary oncologist, case management, hospice, palliative care

The Mrs. Jones case is the most challenging case by far as it is designed to be somewhat vague. The team will hopefully recognize that Mrs. Jones has a poor prognosis and broach goals of care with the family. The main goal is for **the team to discuss with the patient’s surrogate decision-maker goals of care and decide on a plan that the decision-maker feels is appropriate**. This plan of care may or may not include Hospice, an admission to the hospital, etc. Depending on the learners’ level, specialty, and/or interview, the case may take a different direction than expected, which is fine as long as the team is debriefed about the **potential benefit of palliative care and Hospice for a patient like Mrs. Jones.**
