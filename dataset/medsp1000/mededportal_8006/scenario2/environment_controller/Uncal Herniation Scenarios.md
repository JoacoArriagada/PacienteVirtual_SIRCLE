**Two human-patient simulations to illustrate acute uncal herniation**

**IV. Background and scenario overview**

This scenario occurs in the post anesthesia care unit (the "PACU scenario"), with all necessary devices and medications available for the participants, but not in their plain sight. The simulated patient is 70 kg and 170 cm and was previously healthy. In the PACU scenario the patient is two-hours status-post a craniotomy for tumor, and experiences increased ICP in the PACU.

In the PACU scenario the patient is two-hours status-post a craniotomy for tumor and experiencing an acute increase in ICP that is causing uncal herniation. The patient has the identical size, past medical history, and starting vital signs as in the OR scenario. A simulation confederate (a PACU nurse) tells the participant the patient's history, and that although the patient has never been fully responsive in the unit, they are now completely unresponsive. If queried about narcotics or sedatives, the participants are told the patient has received only a small (2 mg dose) of morphine. Once the participant approaches the simulator the uncal herniation is triggered (time T=0 for the scenario). In the PACU version of the UH scenario, the patient has a large, bulky bandage around their head that precludes the adequate atlanto-occipital extension needed to perform conventional direct laryngoscopy. The only way the patient's trachea can be intubated is with a fast track (or "intubating") laryngeal mask airway (FTLMA).

**UNCAL HERNIATION IN THE PACU**

**VII. Environment**

1. **Lab Set Up**-the Post Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU). All airway devices and medications are drawn up into syringes and ready for use, but are not in the participants' sight. Mannitol is in a vial (not drawn up into a syringe).
2. **Mannequin Set Up**-mannequin starts with eyes half open, pupils that are normal and reactive, but unresponsive to questions. It then loses consciousness (closes its eyes), and dilates the left pupil. No moulage is used. The simulator has adequate large bore IV access and an arterial line in place. The scenario starts with the patient breathing oxygen via a simple face-mask. Electrocardiogram, pulse oximeter and an arterial blood pressure are displayed on the vital sign monitor.
3. **Props**-Standard PACU set up.
4. **Distracters**-none.

**VIII. Actors**

1. **Roles**:
2. PACU nurse
3. Neurosurgeon
4. More skilled anesthesia-provider help
5. **Who plays them**-A simulation technician or simulation instructor play the PACU nurse and surgeon. Either other simulation participants or the simulation instructor play the skilled anesthesia-provider help.
6. **Action role**:
7. PACU nurse-orients the participant(s) to the location and the scenario's context ("I'm a PACU nurse, can you see this patient in the Post Anesthesia Care Unit? He was admitted to the unit 2-hours ago, and is now more somnolent..."). They answer questions about the patient's medical history, retrieve requested devices and medications, call for help and can help moderate the scenario with cues from the simulation instructor. They can also administer medications or assist with patient positioning.
8. Neurosurgeon-this actor is very anxious and concerned for their patient. They try to hurry the participants along ("Let's get him intubated, we have to get into his head, get his pressure down now!").
9. Skilled anesthesia-provider help-often this role is played by another participant, and therefore their performance is not scripted. If other participants are not available, then this person is helpful and competent, but does not offer suggestions about the patient's management to the participant.

**IX. Case Narrative:**

1. **Scenario Background**:
2. **Chief complaint**-"Can you see this patient in the PACU, please? This is 42 year-old, previously healthy man who had a craniotomy for tumor. He came out of the operating room about 2-hours ago. He has always been a bit groggy and difficult to arouse, but he seems much sleepier to me in the last 20 minutes." *If queried:* He has a 4-week history of headache, nausea, vomiting, and photophobia. His work up revealed a right temporal glioma that was producing a 2 mm midline shift on CT. The scan revealed mild cerebral edema and sulci effacement. His only preoperative medications are dexamethasone and GI prophylaxis. Immediately prior to surgery the patient was conscious, alert and oriented, and neurologically intact.
3. **Past Medical History**: benign and non-contributory to the scenario.
4. **Meds and allergies**: The patient does not have medication allergies. Medications have been described.
5. **Family/social history**: Non-contributory.
6. **Scenario conditions initially**
7. **History patient gives**-none. As above, the patient is somnolent.
8. **Patients' physiology**-somnolent, bradypneic (RR of 6), SpO2 of 96% on 6 lpm of oxygen, BP 145/60, HR of 70 in sinus rhythm
9. **Scenario branch points**
10. **Changes in the patient's condition**-the patient experiences an uncal herniation when the participant begins to talk to the patient.

**X. Instructor's notes**

1. **Tips to keep scenario flowing in lab and via computer/Tips for actors**-If the participant is completely flailing in the scenario a neurosurgeon can arrive. For these participants they are calm and helpful (as needed). They can make the diagnosis for a participant that is not "getting it" and offer prodding or suggestions as needed ("it looks like he's herniating," and/or "I need to operate now, please get his pressure down and intubate him as soon as you can...").
2. **Scenario programming**
3. **Potential complications paths**-Some participants can wait a long time to ventilate the patient, and allow long periods of apnea. Some have asked for inappropriate medicines during the herniation (epinephrine, for example) and some have treated the hemodynamics with vasodilators (like hydralizine). Occasionally a participant freezes while face-mask ventilating, in which case the "surgeon" appears to tell the participant that the patient has to be intubated and taken back to the operating room. Some have asked for mannitol to be given very early, in which case the PACU nurse leaves for 3 minutes.
