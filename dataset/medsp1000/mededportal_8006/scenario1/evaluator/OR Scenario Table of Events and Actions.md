| Initial state | Eyes open, but not conversant. If spoken to, the simulated patient does not answer. BP 140/60, HR 70, SpO2 96% (on room air), RR 12 |  |  |
| Uncal herniation. Start of "Cushing's triad," heralded by anesthesia technician stating that the patient is sleepier than when he came into the room | Eyes close, loses consciousness. The left pupil is widely dilated and does not react to light. BP increases to 240/120, HR decreases to 40, RR to zero, SpO2 to 80% over 30 seconds. | * What change do you notice in the vital signs? * What is the differential diagnosis at this time? * If participant identifies uncal herniation: why did you make the diagnosis of UH/increased ICP? | * Highlight the physiologic changes associated with acute increases in ICP * Discuss the effects of increased ICP on CBF and the potential for cerebral ischemia |
| Hyperventilation with face-mask | Correct and critical step. Over 2 minutes BP decreases to 170/90, HR increases to 80. SpO2 increases, depending on the use of supplemental oxygen. | * Why did you hyperventilate the patient? * What are benefits of hyperventilation in someone with increased ICP? * Is there a minimum amount of PaCO2 that you should not surpass (i.e. can you overdue this therapy)? | * Decreasing PaCO2 to 26-30 mmHg can decrease ICP; however, further decreases in PaCO2 may exacerbate hypotension (through decreased venous return to the heart) and extreme hypocarbia has been theorized to contribute to cerebral ischemia |
| Raise the head of the bed | Correct step. Over 2 minutes the BP decreases by 5 mmHg. | * Why did you take the time to raise the head of the bed? * Note who raised the head. If it was the participant: was this a task someone else could have performed while you ventilated the patient? | * This is a quick and easy means of improving venous drainage, and using gravity to help decrease ICP. * Workload distribution in crisis can be discussed. |
| Propofol administered | Correct step.   * ≤50 mg decreases BP by 10%, HR increases 5% (per dose) * 51-100 mg decreases BP by 15% and increases HR by 10% (per dose) * 101-150 mg decreases BP by 20% and increases HR by 15% (per dose) * ≥151 mg decreases BP by 25% and increases HR by 20% (per dose) | If propofol administered:   * Why did you give propofol to an unconscious patient? * What effect does propofol have on CMRO2?   If propofol not administered:   * Did you consider giving an IV induction agent? | * Propofol produces some decrease in CMRO2. This effect, along with blunting this patient's ability to respond to direct laryngoscopy (DL), makes it an attractive drug in this scenario. However, the point should be made that too much propofol may decrease mean arterial pressure, and therefore threaten cerebral perfusion in this patient. * A critical element of this scenario is that this patient can be effectively ventilated with a face-mask; therefore endotracheal intubation is important in the patient's care, but not immediately critical. Thus the neuro-anesthesiologists who supervise this scenario recommend that some intravenous (IV) induction agent be administered to this patient before DL. |
| Administer muscle relaxant (either succinylcholine or rocuronium) | Correct step. No effect on the mannequin. | * Defend your choice to use or not use muscle relaxant in this patient. * What is the controversy about using succinylcholine in this patient? How do you respond to that controversy? | * Again, because this patient can be effectively ventilated with a face-mask, the simulation authors envisioned a scenario where there was time for muscle relaxant to be given and allowed to have an effect on the patient. During debriefing, this point, and the deleterious effect on the patient's ICP from coughing during direct laryngoscopy, is made. * During debriefing, we acknowledge that succinylcholine is classically considered to increase ICP, though its use is still accepted by many neuro-anesthesiologists. |
| Administer mannitol | Correct step. BP decreases by 10 mmHg, HR increases 5 beats-per-minute over 2 minutes | * What effect does mannitol have on ICP? * What is the onset of action of this drug? * How should mannitol be delivered? * What is the correct dose to administer? | * Mannitol decreases cerebral volume and decreases ICP * Mannitol's peak effect will occur after this 10-minute scenario is completed, thus the drug does not have a large hemodynamic effect in the scenario. * Because of its high osmotic pressure, mannitol should be administered reasonably slowly and carefully, into a central vein if possible (if delivered into a peripheral vein, its intravascular delivery must be assured by observing the vein). This high maintenance administration probably requires one person to complete, thus administering mannitol is a good example of distributing workload in crisis situations. * Up to 1g/kg of mannitol can be administered. The anesthesiologist may wish to consult with the surgeon prior to giving the drug (communication is highlighted during debriefing). |
| Administer fentanyl | Correct step. For doses of 1-3 cc's: BP decreases by 5 mmHg, no effect on HR; doses of 3-5 cc's BP decreases 8 mmHg, and HR decreases by 3 bpm | * Why did you give fentanyl? * What effect does it have on ICP, CMRO2, and CBF | * There may be some benefit from administering fentanyl to blunt the hemodynamic surge that can be associated with DL * Fentanyl does not significantly alter CBF, CMRO2, or ICP |
| Administer hydralizine, sodium nitroprusside, nitroglycerin | Incorrect step. Reduces BP by 20 mmHg, increases HR by 10 bpm | * Why did you choose this drug? * What are the deleterious effects of vasodilators on patients with increased ICP? | * Vasodilators can cause an increase in CBF and ICP |
| Administer labetalol | Incorrect step. Reduces BP by 20 mmHg, reduces HR by 5 bpm | * Why did you choose this drug? * What happened to the patient's HR (and, likely, to the cardiac output) after the labetalol | * In the setting of a hypertensive patient with a normal to high heart rate, labetalol is an acceptable drug. In the setting of the Cushing's response, the beta antagonism mitigates against this drug's use. |
| Administer atropine, epinephrine or ephedrine | Incorrect step. Increases HR (depending on dose), no change in BP | * Why did you choose atropine? * What is the pathophysiology we are simulating? | * The authors have seen a few participants pick these drugs in this scenario. During debriefing, we direct the participant's attention to the underlying pathophysiology, and review its treatment. We talk about not throwing medicines at vital signs, but rather considering the underlying cause for the vital signs. |
| Administer nicardipine | Acceptable step. Decreases BP by 20 mmHg, slight increase in HR | * Why did you choose this drug? * In this setting, are there other drugs that might be more beneficial for the patient? | * The calcium-channel blocker nicardipine is an acceptable choice in a hypertensive patient with increased ICP, though the authors make the point that more propofol, with its effect of decreasing CMRO2, might be a better drug to decrease BP. |
| Direct laryngoscopy (DL) | Correct step. The authors have the patient's vital signs change depending upon the blood pressure at the time DL is occurring-if the BP is ≤140 mmHg we presumed that that indicated a well anesthetized patient who would not have a significant response to DL, thus the BP increases by 5 mmHg, HR by only a few beats/minute. BP's of 141-170 mmHg have a greater stress response to DL, and those with a BP ≥171 have a large (30 mmHg rise in BP and 40 bpm increase in HR) response to this event. | * Did you notice the blood pressure when you performed DL? * Evaluate that blood pressure (and the treatment to that point) with respect to performing DL. * Would you change anything about your treatment the next time you face this scenario? | * By this time in the debriefing, we have emphasized that this patient is in extremis, but that there is a finite period of time to prepare him for the physiologic stress of DL. While outcome studies have not been done that support hemodynamic control during DL, the neuro-anesthesiologists whom the authors consulted feel that the hypertension that direct laryngoscopy can induce is probably detrimental for this patient. Therefore, having the patient well anesthetized at this point is seen as an important performance objective in this scenario. |
| Call for help | Correct step. | * How did you utilize the help that arrived? * Assess your workload distribution and communication | * Principles of teamwork, crew resource management and acute care are reviewed |
| Call neurosurgeons | Correct step. | * Who gave report to the neurosurgeon? * Assess your report for conciseness and accuracy * How do you manage an agitated and excited surgeon? | * When the neurosurgeon arrives the authors have them act agitated and excited ("What's going on! Come on, we've got to hurry and get into his head! Get his pressure down, now!") We make the point that one member of the anesthesia team (probably not the team leader) may need to manage the surgeon ("Dr. XX is giving propofol, that will decrease his BP and help blunt his cerebral ischemia...). This is an example of distributing workload, maintaining vigilance, providing communication and other elements of scene control in crisis resource management. |
