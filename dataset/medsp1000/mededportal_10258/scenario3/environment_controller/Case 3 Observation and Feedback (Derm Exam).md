# OSTE Observation and Feedback (Derm Exam) Case: Standardized Learner Instructions

Your name is Grace/Greg Schwartz You are a $4 ^ { \mathrm { t h } } .$ -year medical student doing your subinternship (sub-I) on the Bellevue wards

You admitted Mr. Anderson, a 55-year-old man with COPD, four days ago for pneumonia. He has responded well to treatment and will likely be discharged tomorrow.

You noticed Mr. Anderson has a skin condition when he was admitted. Your resident referred to it as venous stasis. The patient stated it was chronic so you didn’t focus on it. However today your patient is concerned it is getting worse.

Patient with Venous stasis   
Mr. Anderson has a history of skin changes in his lower legs; he describes his legs becoming darker over the years. He has had a recurrent ulcer on his lateral ankle. He is worried that his other leg is getting worse and he needs to know if he should report his concerns to dermatology.

You feel like you are doing a good job as a sub-I. You are confident in your ability to present and feel you know your patients well; however, you are less confident in your physical examination skills. You recall hearing a murmur on one of your other patients but not saying anything because the resident didn’t hear it and you assumed you were mistaken.

You recall learning the dermatology examination in medical school and are familiar with terms such as macule and papule. But you don’t feel confident examining rashes or skin lesions; in the past you were told that you did not adequately describe skin lesions and you would miss looking at areas that were important to examine. You have been focused on learning dermatology terms so you can use them properly to describe lesions.

You believe the patient has venous stasis but you know that many rashes can look similar, so you are not confident in your diagnosis.

At this point in your career you feel you have so much to learn. You do fine with learning concepts in class but get nervous being observed or being asked to think on the spot. You know how important the physical exam is, and you feel very responsible to learn it well. You feel you have been taught the dermatologic exam, but you wish you were more confident.

# Timing

# Beginning

You arrange to meet your attending in an empty room. You told the patient you would bring him in when you were ready to examine him. The patient is in a 4 bed room and you thought it would be more private. You already told the patient that you would return to examine him with your attending.

Let your attending take the lead. See if the attending wants to speak to you about setting expectations before being observed before you start the exam.

When the attending says to get started, open the door and invite the patient in. You begin by re-introducing the patient to your attending: “Mr. Anderson, you remember my attending. I’m going to talk to him/her about your rash.”

Wash your hands, put on gloves. Begin to perform the focused dermatologic exam Talk your way through the exam – what do you see, how would you describe it.

# During your exam:

DO NOT RE-INTERVIEW THE PATIENT. Just ask how long he has had the skin problem. And why he thinks it is getting worse   
Do not remove his socks unless told to - just push them down   
Begin by describing the skin findings. Use terms like patch, plaque, scale, shiny, mottled, hypopigmented, hyperpigmented. No drainage. Notice swelling.   
Describe where you see the skin findings.   
Compare legs.

If the patient asks you to explain a term: Do so as best you can, but look to the attending for back-up.

# Middle

After finishing the exam, say you are done and then wait for the preceptor to take the lead.

If asked to self-assess: Say you think you did a good exam: I thought I was able to use the dermatologic terms correctly. I thought your exam was thorough.

If asked if you have any questions: Ask your attending— “How did I do? Did I describe his skin condition well? Did I miss anything? How would you describe it"

Be accepting of all feedback.

# End

If the preceptor does not do so, thank the patient for letting you examine him. You tell the patient to go back to their room.

# OSTE Observation and Feedback (Derm Exam) Case: Standardized Patient Instructions

# STANDARDIZED PATIENT INSTRUCTIONS

Your name is Dennis Anderson. You are a 55-year-old man with a history of chronic lung disease from smoking (you quit 10 years ago). You work at the post office.

You noticed your legs were getting darker over 10 years ago. They would itch from time to time and swell. You developed a clot in one of your legs and you started taking Coumadin. Soon after you had the clot you developed an ulcer on the outside of your ankle. Over the years the ulcer has come and gone. When you have an ulcer you are followed closely by dermatology and treated with Unna boots.

You are worried your other leg is getting worse and is getting darker.

# Beginning

The intern asked told you that he/she wanted to examine you again with their attending. S/he told you they would bring you to a different room for privacy.

[Wait outside the room until you are invited in. Once inside the room go sit on the exam table]

You recall seeing the attending when s/he checked in on you yesterday. You know that s/he is here to look at your rash.

If asked about your skin condition – answer questions. Give brief answers.

# If asked why you think you are getting worse

Point out that you worry your other leg is getting worse because the skin is darker.

# Middle

Cooperate with the exam.

If the doctors use jargon, ask them to clarify. (Only ask once or twice.)

After the exam, the intern will tell you to return to your room [Leave the room through the same door you entered]

# End

If you have a good rapport with the doctors, accept what they say.

If you do not have a good rapport, be less trusting and ask to see a specialist.

# OSTE Observation and Feedback (Derm Exam) Case Faculty Instructions

# Your Role

You are attending on the Bellevue wards and your sub-I wants you to go see a patient who has a rash. You plan to use this opportunity to observe your sub-I perform an examination and then give her/him feedback.

# Learner Information

Name: Grace/Greg Schwartz Training Level: MS-IV

The Scenario

You are the ward attending on a busy Bellevue service. Your service was at cap until a few days ago when you managed to discharge several long-term patients. You feel badly that you haven’t spent much time with your sub-I. She came to you this morning asking you to look at her patient’s skin. Your resident is in clinic. She thinks the patient has venous stasis and the patient is concerned their skin is getting worse.

Your sub I confides in you that s/he has been working on improving their dermatologic exam skills.

The patient is a 55-year-old man admitted for pneumonia three days ago. He is doing well and is getting ready for discharge tomorrow.

Your sub-I is doing fairly well overall. Her presentations are a bit long but s/he covers the key medical issues. She has good rapport with patients and seems quite interested in learning; however, you have had concerns about her physical examination skills and her attention to detail. She lacks confidence when presenting her physical findings and often seeks others’ opinions.

You arrange to meet with your sub-I in and empty patient room. The sub-I will bring in the patient when you are ready to examine him.

# Your Task

• Observe your sub-I perform a focused dermatologic exam and then provide feedback on her dermatologic exam.   
• Focus only on the patient’s venous stasis disease.   
• Do not remove patient’s pants. If they are wearing a t-shirt don’t remove. Do not remove Unna boot.   
• Be sure to time yourself so you have time to observe and then provide verbal feedback.
