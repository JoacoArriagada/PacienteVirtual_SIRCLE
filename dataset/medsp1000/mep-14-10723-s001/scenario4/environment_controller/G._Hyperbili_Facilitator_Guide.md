**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Hyperbilirubinemia**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Vignette**

A 3-day-old female first-born infant born at 38 6/7 weeks via uncomplicated normal spontaneous vaginal delivery following an uncomplicated pregnancy. Mom was Group B Streptococcus negative and there was no peripartum maternal fever. Her primary care physician referred the patient to the Emergency Department when she was found to have an elevated transcutaneous bilirubin of 16 at 75 hours of age (the 2004 AAP guideline's light up level for age and risk group is 18). Infant and maternal blood types are unknown. The patient has been exclusively breastfed and is nursing well, 10-20 minutes total every 2-3 hours. She has had 3 wet diapers in the past 24 hours. Stools are dark black/brown. Vitals are within normal limits. She has lost 8% of her birth weight. On exam, she is sleeping but easily arousable. Fontanelle is soft and flat. She has dry mucous membranes. Skin exam is notable for jaundice extending from the head to the mid-thigh but no tenting. Maternal labs are unknown. Mom has researched “jaundice” on the Internet and is especially worried about sepsis and G6PD deficiency.

Obtain an accurate serum bilirubin level and determine the patient’s appropriate risk curve (low v. medium v. high). To determine which light-up-level curve applies to the patient, one must be able to screen for and/or identify the following risk factors:

* Isoimmune hemolytic disease
* G6PD deficiency
* Asphyxia
* Significant lethargy
* Temperature instability
* Sepsis
* Acidosis
* Albumin <3

In our patient’s case, the mom and baby’s blood types are unknown, so a direct coombs could be appropriate to rule out isoimmune hemolytic disease. Alternatively, a more high value option would be to ask the mom or call the hospital where the patient was born to determine maternal and infant blood type (which clarifies likelihood of isoimmune hemolytic disease) and Coombs testing.

A CBC with differential and reticulocyte count would screen for a hemolytic process such as G6PD deficiency, though such hemolytic processes classically are associated with an elevated bilirubin within the first 24 hours of life. In general, CBC and reticulocyte counts are not indicated unless the TSB is high.

You determine that the patient’s total serum bilirubin is 19 and light–up level is 18, so she is admitted to the acute care floor for phototherapy treatment. The patient’s mother expresses concern that they are going to receive a large bill from the hospital and wonders if there is anyway to estimate the cost of the hospitalization.

There are a variety of cost predictors available. Many insurance companies have begun providing cost estimators, and many hospitals employ financial counselors and care managers which act as liaisons with insurance companies. These resources are more useful in elective or non-emergent situations and often do not generate an estimate for a night in the hospital.

Discussion:

As the bilirubin level is now more than three points below the curve, it is reasonable to discontinue phototherapy. Furthermore, there is low suspicion for ongoing hemolysis given maternal blood type that would make ABO incompatibility unlikely. Studies have demonstrated that checking a rebound bilirubin does not improve outcomes.7,8,9 Repeating a level six hours later would result in in an unnecessarily prolonged hospital stay and an unwarranted test that provides little value since it won’t change management.

All advocate for not checking a rebound bilirubin level once phototherapy has been discontinued in the hospital though do emphasize the importance of follow-up with Pediatrician. It is also reasonable to engage the services of a lactation consultant, especially when breastfeeding jaundice is suspected, as they can advise the mother in overcoming barriers to breastfeeding and provide support.
