**Vignette**

A 3-day-old female first-born infant born at 38 6/7 weeks via uncomplicated normal spontaneous vaginal delivery following an uncomplicated pregnancy. Mom was Group B Streptococcus negative and there was no peripartum maternal fever. Her primary care physician referred the patient to the Emergency Department when she was found to have an elevated transcutaneous bilirubin of 16 at 75 hours of age (the 2004 AAP guideline's light up level for age and risk group is 18). Infant and maternal blood types are unknown. The patient has been exclusively breastfed and is nursing well, 10-20 minutes total every 2-3 hours. She has had 3 wet diapers in the past 24 hours. Stools are dark black/brown. Vitals are within normal limits. She has lost 8% of her birth weight. On exam, she is sleeping but easily arousable. Fontanelle is soft and flat. She has dry mucous membranes. Skin exam is notable for jaundice extending from the head to the mid-thigh but no tenting. Maternal labs are unknown. Mom has researched “jaundice” on the Internet and is especially worried about sepsis and G6PD deficiency.

Question #1:

The next best step is to:

1. Admit to the acute care floor for phototherapy. No laboratory evaluations are needed given the extent of jaundice on physical exam and given that a transcutaneous bilirubin is close to the light up level.
2. Repeat a transcutaneous bilirubin level (TSB).
3. Check total serum bilirubin and look up mother's blood type
4. Check total serum bilirubin, CBC with differential and reticulocyte count, and coombs test
5. Check total serum bilirubin, CBC with differential and reticulocyte count, Direct coombs test and perform a full sepsis evaluation.

Correct Answer: C

Discussion:

The best answer is that which provides the most value to the patient, meaning the test(s) that provide the most useful information with the fewest costs and risks to the patient. The correct answer in this clinical scenario is C.

The next best step would be to obtain an accurate serum bilirubin level and to determine the patient’s appropriate risk curve (low v. medium v. high). To determine which light-up-level curve applies to the patient, one must be able to screen for and/or identify the following risk factors:

* Isoimmune hemolytic disease
* G6PD deficiency
* Asphyxia
* Significant lethargy
* Temperature instability
* Sepsis
* Acidosis
* Albumin <3

In our patient’s case, the mom and baby’s blood types are unknown, so a direct coombs could be appropriate to rule out isoimmune hemolytic disease. Alternatively, a more high value option would be to ask the mom or call the hospital where the patient was born to determine maternal and infant blood type (which clarifies likelihood of isoimmune hemolytic disease) and Coombs testing.

A CBC with differential and reticulocyte count would screen for a hemolytic process such as G6PD deficiency, though such hemolytic processes classically are associated with an elevated bilirubin within the first 24 hours of life. In general, CBC and reticulocyte counts are not indicated unless the TSB is high.

Admitting the patient for phototherapy without establishing the patient’s serum bilirubin level is of low value due to the high risk to the patient. The patient may not need phototherapy, thereby exposing the patient to the risks associated with an unnecessary hospitalization such as infection exposure. On the other hand, the patient’s serum bilirubin may be dangerously elevated such that the patient warrants an exchange transfusion.

A serum bilirubin level is the most accurate measurement of bilirubin in the body. Studies have shown that the extent of jaundice on physical exam does not reliably predict serum bilirubin levels3, therefore answer A is incorrect. Transcutaneous bilirubin devices are reliable screening tools in infants >30 weeks gestation. However, their accuracy diminishes at levels >15 mg/dL.4 While one could repeat a transcutaneous bilirubin, it is likely of low value due to the likely need for a serum bilirubin level anyway. Therefore answer “B” is incorrect.

A full sepsis evaluation is unwarranted at this time given the overall well appearance of the infant, the lack of fever. The patient also lacks risk factors for sepsis as mom was GBS negative and afebrile at the time of delivery. Thus answer “E” is incorrect.

The most likely cause of hyperbilirubinemia is breastfeeding jaundice.

The American College of Physicians has developed a High Value Care Curriculum that proposes these five steps when deciding whether or not to order proceed with diagnostic tests:

* **Step one:** Understand the benefits, harms, and relative costs of the proposed diagnostic and therapeutic interventions
* **Step two:** Minimize or eliminate the use of interventions are not beneficial or may be harmful
* **Step three:** Choose interventions and care settings that maximize benefits, minimize harms, and reduce costs
* **Step four:** Customize a care plan along with the patient, incorporating their values and addressing their concerns
* **Step five:** Identify system level opportunities to improve outcomes, minimize harms, and reduce healthcare waste

The full curriculum is available here:5

[American College of Physicians' High Value Care Curriculum](http://www.im.org/p/cm/ld/fid%3D372)

Key Point:

* Practicing High Value Care means pursing, along with the input of the patient, diagnostic and therapeutic tests and procedures which maximize benefits and minimize harms and costs to the patient\*

Question #2:

You determine that the patient’s total serum bilirubin is 19 and light–up level is 18, so she is admitted to the acute care floor for phototherapy treatment. The patient’s mother expresses concern that they are going to receive a large bill from the hospital and wonders if there is anyway to estimate the cost of the hospitalization.

You respond by:

1. Referring her to the financial counselor of the institution
2. Referring her to the care manager for the acute care floor
3. Direct her to the internet
4. Suggest she contact her insurance company
5. All of the above

The correct answer is E.

Discussion:

There are a variety of cost predictors available. In this context, we use the term “cost” to refer to the expected charge OR to the amount eventually billed to the patient. Please see the cost versus charges document for further explanation.

The Affordable Care Act has led to the rise of high-deductible plans, which means increased out-of-pocket costs for many consumers. As a result, consumers are demanding more cost transparency in the health care industry.6 This demand has led to the creation of several websites that allow consumers to enter the name of a procedure and the zip code of the procedure. This will generate a cost estimate. These websites, while not perfect, provide a ballpark estimate. Several of these websites are listed here:

* For Hospitals in California (<http://www.oshpd.ca.gov/chargemaster/>) - it is state law that the charge master be available to the public! Remember charge does NOT equal cost
* Heath Care Blue Book (<https://healthcarebluebook.com>)
* Guroo ([http://www.guroo.com/#](http://www.guroo.com/)!) - cost of care organized by care bundle
* Costs of Care ([www.Costsofcare.org](http://www.costsofcare.org))
* Fair Health (www.fairhealth.org)

Many insurance companies have begun providing cost estimators as well. Many hospitals employ financial counselors and care managers which act as liaisons with the insurance companies. However, all of these resources, especially the cost estimators, are more useful in elective or non-emergent situations. It should also be noted that the estimators often predict costs for diagnostic labs and procedures but do not generate an estimate for a night in the hospital.

In this particular case, it should be noted that there were several strategies that may have prevented the need for this admission, such as home phototherapy or formula supplementation.

\**Optional activity*: With computer hooked up to projector, navigate to one of the above websites and explore the costs of a variety of tests or procedures, such as an MRI of the brain.

Key Points:

* A variety of tools exist to try and predict health care costs\*

Question #3:

The total serum bilirubin is rechecked the following morning, 6 hours after initiation of phototherapy. It is now 14, which is more than three points below the light-up level. You learn that mom’s blood type is A+. Your next step is to:

* 1. Continue phototherapy, consult lactation services, and recheck total serum bilirubin again in 6 hours
  2. Discontinue phototherapy, consult lactation services, and recheck total serum bilirubin again in 6 hours
  3. Discontinue phototherapy, consult lactation services, and discharge the patient home with close PCP follow-up
  4. Discontinue phototherapy and discharge the patient home with close PCP follow-up.

Correct Answer: C

Discussion:

As the bilirubin level is now more than three points below the curve, it is reasonable to discontinue phototherapy. Furthermore, there is low suspicion for ongoing hemolysis given maternal blood type that would make ABO incompatibility unlikely. Studies have demonstrated that checking a rebound bilirubin does not improve outcomes.7,8,9 Repeating a level six hours later would result in in an unnecessarily prolonged hospital stay and an unwarranted test that provides little value since it won’t change management.

Despite this evidence, the practice of checking a rebound bilirubin is common. This is not surprising, as tremendous healthcare variation exists. Evidence-based guidelines have been developed to promote best practices and minimize overuse and are an essential tool to minimize variability in disease management.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has published guidelines and many local institutions do as well. Two guidelines are available here:

[American Academy of Pediatrics Hyperbilirubinemia Guidelines](http://pediatrics.aappublications.org/content/114/1/297) 1

(http://pediatrics.aappublications.org/content/114/1/297)

[Seattle Children’s Hospital](http://www.seattlechildrens.org/healthcare-professionals/gateway/pathways/) 2

(http://www.seattlechildrens.org/healthcare-professionals/gateway/pathways/)

All advocate for not checking a rebound bilirubin level once phototherapy has been discontinued in the hospital though do emphasize the importance of follow-up with Pediatrician. It is also reasonable to engage the services of a lactation consultant, especially when breastfeeding jaundice is suspected, as they can advise the mother in overcoming barriers to breastfeeding and provide support.

Key Points:

* Evidence-Based Guidelines exist to minimize unwarranted use and variation in healthcare\*
* A rebound bilirubin level following cessation of phototherapy cessation is not routinely recommended
