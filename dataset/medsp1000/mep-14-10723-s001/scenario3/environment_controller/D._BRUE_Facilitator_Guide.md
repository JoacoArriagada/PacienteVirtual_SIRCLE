**What’s *BRUE*ing?**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Vignette 2:**

Liam Smith is a 10-week-old baby who presents to the emergency department after an episode where parents felt that he was not responsive. Liam was asleep in his bassinet, having eaten 2 hours earlier. He ate approximately 2 ounces and burped well. Mom went to go pick him up to feed him. Initially he appeared alert but suddenly he began to have some irregular breathing, tensed up and turned pale. He did not have any vomiting, choking or gagging. He has not been ill nor has he had any cough, congestion or rhinorrhea. The entire episode lasted approximately 30 seconds. No CPR or rescue breaths were provided. He quickly returned to baseline. This is the first time this has ever happened. Concerned, Liam’s parents brought him into the emergency department.

Liam was born at 36 weeks via spontaneous vaginal delivery and went home with parents without any complications. He normally feeds about 2oz of formula every 2-3 hours. He usually does tend to spit up frequently. There is no family history of sudden death, cardiac arrhythmias or seizures. He lives at home with mother and father. He is the first child. No pets. No smoke exposure.

On physical examination, he is well appearing, non-toxic and interacting appropriately with mom. His temperature is 37.2C (rectal), pulse is 126 beats per minute, respiration rate is 40 breaths per minute and blood pressure is 86/58. His anterior fontanelle is soft and flat. He is tracking appropriately. His heart, lung and abdominal exams are normal. There are no bruises and he moves all his extremities equally. Neurologically, he is alert and his tone and strength are appropriate for age.

Liam fits the diagnostic criteria along the algorithm for being a “low risk BRUE”:

1. He is 70 days old, born at 36 weeks, no CPR was performed, the event lasted less than 1 minute, and this was the first event.

1. During the encounter the mother receives a phone call from Liam’s grandmother reminding her that Liam’s aunt had Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (WPW) as a child. Does this change your thoughts on whether or not to obtain the EKG?

Although Liam did not present with tachycardia, it is possible although unlikely that he could have an arrhythmia. However, getting an electrocardiogram when he is back to baseline may provide false reassurance. EKG may rule out WPW but will not rule out other potential arrhythmias. The risks and benefits described above should be discussed with the family. In this case, learning about parental preferences would be important.

**Vignette continued:**

Liam’s parents are very nervous about going home from the emergency department and would prefer to be admitted to the hospital. They are worried that the event will happen again and would feel more comfortable staying in the hospital overnight. They also want to take him home with a home apnea monitor, which is something they read about on the internet.

The following represent different options to present to the family:

1. Discharge home from the emergency department with close pediatrician follow up.
2. Briefly monitor patient with continuous pulse oximetry and serial observations.
3. Admit the patient to the hospital solely for cardiorespiratory monitoring.

Parents should be offered education on BRUE and CPR resources.

A key element of providing high value care is taking into account patient and family wishes and incorporating patient or caregiver goals into decision-making.

* Factors that may affect a patient or family’s comfort level with a care plan include a lack of understanding of the benefits or harms of the options and the evidence behind them, and social or logistical barriers to care. Part of providing high value care is reviewing all of these elements with the patient or family and incorporating them into the final care plan.
