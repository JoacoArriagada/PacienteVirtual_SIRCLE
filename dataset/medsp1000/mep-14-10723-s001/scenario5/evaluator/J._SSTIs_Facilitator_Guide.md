**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (SSTI)**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Learning Objectives**

At the end of these vignettes, participants will be able to:

1. Practice an evidence-based approach to the work-up and management of common pediatric skin and soft tissues infections (SSTI);
2. Describe the indications for, and risks/benefits of, common laboratory and imaging tests used to diagnose SSTI;
3. Develop an evidence-based rationale for the use of antibiotics in SSTI, including factors affecting Methicillin-Resistant *Staphylococcus Aureus* (MRSA) coverage;
4. Understand indications for the use of incision and drainage in SSTI.
5. Utilize tools presented to analyze and assess the likelihood of a diagnosis and the value of a diagnostic test/intervention\*

**\*Objective is one that relates to general concepts of HVC rather than being disease-specific**

**Vignette 1:**

Cory is a 9-year-old otherwise healthy male who presents with a three-day history of worsening redness and pain to his left outer shin. He reports noticing it after falling off his skateboard and scraping the side of his leg earlier this week. The area has slowly been getting a little larger, but he has not noted any red marks going up his leg. He reports pain mostly when he touches it but otherwise has been able to run around with his friends like usual. He has not had fever and there has not been any drainage. There is no personal or family history of recurrent skin infections. There are no close contacts that work in health care or correctional facilities. On physical exam, he is afebrile with normal vital signs. He is well appearing and his exam is only notable for an area of erythema overlying healing superficial abrasions along the lateral side of his left lower leg. It is not circumferential and measures roughly 9 x 4 cm. It is brightly erythematous but not violaceous, and is mildly indurated, warm, and tender to touch. There are no areas of fluctuance or crepitus, and no evidence of streaking up the leg. There is no involvement of the skin overlying the surrounding joints, nor any evidence of effusion. He is distally neurovascularly intact and full weight-bearing on both legs.

Question #1:

Does this case describe a patient with simple or complicated soft tissue infection?

What historical and exam features classify it as such? What other information would you obtain?

Discussion:

This case describes a simple cellulitis, defined as a diffuse, superficial, slowly spreading infection of the skin and subcutaneous tissue in the absence of purulence. It can also be considered mild in the absence of severe systemic signs (fever, toxic appearance, hypotension). Findings that may indicate special circumstances include: clinical factors suggestive of deeper infection (e.g. myositis, osteomyelitis, necrotizing fasciitis), orbital cellulitis, cellulitis associated with a burn, surgical wound or instrumentation, animal bite, or dental infection, and underlying patient co-morbidities such as primary or acquired immune deficiency.2

Question #2:

Of the following, which are the most appropriate next steps in evaluation and management of this patient? You may choose more than one answer.

1. Blood culture
2. Empiric treatment with clindamycin
3. Complete Blood Count (CBC) with differential
4. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) with contrast of affected limb
5. C-Reactive Protein (CRP)
6. Empiric treatment with first generation cephalosporin
7. Admission for intravenous (IV) antibiotics
8. Discharge with close outpatient follow-up
9. Empiric treatment with trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX)

Answer:

F (consider B if high community rates of methicillin resistant staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) or risk factors present)

Question #3:

What patient and community factors might guide your antibiotic choice or adjunctive therapies?

Discussion:

Group A streptococcus (GAS) and *Staphylococcus aureus* - both methicillin sensitive (MSSA) and methicillin resistant (MRSA) - are the most common bacterial causes for skin and soft tissue infections. It is difficult to discern among them with great specificity on clinical grounds alone, and microbiologic data is lacking on non-draining non-purulent SSTIs, though some postulate that GAS (and less commonly other streptococci) is much more prevalent in non-purulent SSTI. Community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA) continues to be prominent in our communities, and in some areas clindamycin resistance is also rising. It is important to be aware of your local antibiotic resistant patterns.3 See next question for further discussion of more evidence to support different antibiotic choices.

Host factors may also play a role in recurrent SSTIs. Close contact with those who work in the healthcare field or in other residential or correctional facilities may be at increased risk for CA-MRSA colonization. Underlying conditions, such as lymphedema, obesity and eczema, may also increase a patient's risk for recurrent SSTI. In these situations, improving control of the underlying condition may help decrease risk of recurrence.1

Question #4:

Define each of the above diagnostic or therapeutic actions as either high or low value for this patient. Incorporate the pre-test probability, quality, and costs into your decision. What evidence exists to support your decision-making?

Discussion:

Healthcare value has been defined as Value = Quality/Cost

a. Quality encompasses study appropriateness, patient safety, patient outcomes and minimizing healthcare waste

b. High value care is not the equivalent of choosing the least expensive test, as cost does not equal value.

c. Cost includes not just financial cost but also non-financial factors, such as potential harms associated with diagnostic delay or diagnostic error, adverse effect from testing or treatment, or patient/family stressors associated with such.

Patients with simple cellulitis generally do not require hospitalization for IV antibiotics. Some indications for hospitalization may include: severe systemic signs (Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) or toxicity), suspected deeper or complicated infection, rapid progression, immunocompromised host, infants less than months of age, inability to tolerate oral antibiotics, prior treatment failure (generally considered after 48 hours of appropriately dosed antibiotics), or poor access to follow-up care. With the costs of hospitalization, and the probability of successful treatment on appropriate oral antibiotic, this is a very low value option for first presentation of simple SSTI.1

Inflammatory markers may be modestly elevated and should not typically alter your clinical decision-making in a well-appearing child; they are not routinely recommended. Cultures of blood or tissue samples are also not indicated in simple non-purulent SSTI. In adults, the yield of positive blood cultures is generally less than 5%. Recent pediatric studies of even hospitalized children with SSTI reveal exceeding low yield (less than 1%).4,5

While MRSA has been commonly isolated in abscesses, its prevalence in non-cultured non-draining infections is unknown. The rise of CA-MRSA has led to more empiric treatment of all SSTIs with agents such as clindamycin or TMP/SMX, instead of traditional beta-lactam antibiotics. A recent large population database study in 2016 shows slight decline in MRSA rates, but increase in clindamycin-resistant MSSA strains.3 Evidence also suggests clindamycin confers no treatment benefit over beta-lactams in cellulitis even in endemic MRSA regions, while TMP/SMX may be associated with increased treatment failure.6,7 Ultimately, the highest value choice for antibiotic will be one that considers antimicrobial stewardship in providing the most narrow coverage while decreasing risk for evolving resistance, and minimizing potential toxicity and side effects.

Key Points:

* Simple nonpurulent SSTs can most effectively be cared for on an outpatient basis, without indication for further imaging or lab work.
* Beta-lactam antibiotics are effective first line treatment for simple cellulitis, which are more typically streptococcal. The widespread of use of clindamycin for empiric MRSA coverage may be associated with increasing rates of clindamycin resistance in MSSA.
* Providing HVC involves balancing study appropriateness, patient safety, patient outcomes, and minimizing health care waste and cost, and low cost does not necessarily equate to high value\*
