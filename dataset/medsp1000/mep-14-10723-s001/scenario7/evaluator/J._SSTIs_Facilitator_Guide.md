**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (SSTI)**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Vignette 3**:

Riza is a 6-year-old previously healthy female. She presents to your Emergency Department (ED) with a chief complaint of worsening redness and pain in her right leg. Three days ago, she saw her Primary Care Physician (PCP) for new-onset redness and warmth of her right lateral leg, associated with tenderness to touch and described as approximately the size of a quarter. Her PCP prescribed cephalexin for suspected cellulitis, which she has been taking. Today, Riza developed a fever up to 102°F at home along with fatigue and worsening of her right leg pain and redness. Her PCP referred her directly to the ED.

Her exam is as follows:

Temperature 39.5°C (PO); Heart rate 150 bpm; Blood Pressure 95/60 mm Hg; Respiratory Rate 26 per minute, oxygen saturation 98% on room air.

General: Tired-appearing school-aged child who is awake and answers questions appropriately. HEENT: Normocephalic, atraumatic. Pupils equal round and reactive to light. Oropharynx clear. Respiratory: Tachypneic without retractions or distress. Cardiovascular: Tachycardic without murmurs, rubs or gallops. Capillary refill time: 3 seconds peripherally. Abdomen: Soft, non-tender, non-distended, normoactive bowel sounds. Extremities/Musculoskeletal: Marked erythema, warmth and edema of right lateral leg and calf, with small areas of violaceous discoloration. The erythema and edema have ill-defined margins. She cries “ouch” with any palpation or movement of her right leg. No crepitus or fluctuance appreciated. Pulses 2+ bilaterally. Neurologic: Cranial Nerves II-XII intact, no focal deficits.

You outline the borders of the erythema on her right leg as best as you can and mark the date and time.

You order a dose of acetaminophen for her fever. Before the medication is given, she has an unprovoked episode of non-bloody, non-bilious emesis.

Discussion:

The three diagnoses in the table above are suggestions for comparison, but other diagnostic possibilities could be used (e.g., thrombophlebitis; pyomyositis). This exercise is meant to help learners think about the distinguishing features of common, uncommon and potentially life-threatening SSTIs and identify this patient as *higher risk* based on the presence of: systemic symptoms (fever, fatigue, nausea, vomiting)/SIRS; failure of appropriate outpatient antibiotic therapy; and exam findings (severe pain, spreading edema and erythema). These important distinctions can be made from history and physical, even before any lab or radiologic testing is undertaken.

One take-home point from this exercise is that, for life-threatening and rare diagnoses such as necrotizing fasciitis about which we have a dearth of rigorous research, a careful history and physical and high index of suspicion can be of tremendous value. Early recognition and repeated assessments are key, as is early involvement of surgeons since diagnosis and treatment require surgical intervention. This is one example of a “more is more” situation, in which heightened clinical awareness, prompt surgical consultation and initiation of antibiotics can be life saving.1,14

Key Points:

* When a patient presents with systemic toxicity (e.g., fevers, malaise, nausea, vomiting, SIRS/sepsis) in the setting of worsening SSTI, and/or atypical exam features are present (e.g., pain out of proportion; spreading edema and erythema), you should have a high index of suspicion for necrotizing SSTI.
* Prompt surgical consultation is first-line for both diagnosis and management. Blood tests and imaging may be of benefit but should not delay surgical consultation.
* Comparing pre-test probability, risks and benefits of each potential diagnostic intervention can promote the practice of HVC\*

\*This key point is one that relates to general concepts of HVC rather than being disease-specific
