**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Complex Care - The Complexity of the Complex Patient**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Vignette:**

Patient SM is a 4 year old female born at 26 weeks gestation with a history of chronic lung disease, tracheostomy without ventilator, congenital hydrocephalus with ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt, diabetes insipidus, hypothyroidism, feeding intolerance, gastrostomy tube (GT)-dependence, gastroesophageal reflux and constipation. The patient has had numerous hospitalizations for pneumonia over the years, with the most recent event being approximately six months prior requiring a month-long stay. She is presenting to the Emergency Department with one episode of desaturation to 81% overnight while sleeping, picked up on her home monitor. The oxygen saturation gradually resolved over 10 minutes, increasing to >95% (baseline) with suctioning and repositioning. The desaturation was observed by home nursing staff who noted that initially, there was not a good wave form on the pulse oximeter. The patient remained stable through the episode, without any change in behavior. She had been mildly tachycardic over the last day, but otherwise has been at her baseline. The patient is not verbal, but uses gestures and facial expressions to communicate. The patient has remained afebrile. She receives 2 liters per minute of oxygen through her tracheostomy collar and has not needed increased suctioning for secretions. The mother notes no signs of any cough or choking. The patient is tolerating her GT feeds. She has had no issues with breakthrough urination and, when asked, she denies any headaches or dizziness. There have been no sick contacts.

On physical examination, she is well appearing and at her baseline per mother.

Her vitals are: temperature 36.9, pulse 117 beats per minute, respiratory rate 24 and blood pressure 92/58. Her oxygen saturations are 99% on 2 liters per minute via tracheal collar. She is awake, alert, and smiling. Her mucous membranes are moist, VP shunt is palpable, tracheostomy is in place with the surrounding tissue being clean, dry, and intact and secretions are thin and clear. She has symmetric chest wall movement with no retractions, good air movement and no wheezing, rales, or rhonchi. Her abdomen was soft, nontender, mildly distended, gastrostomy tube in place clean, dry and intact.

Discussion:

The concern is whether this patient has a respiratory illness. Due to the medical complexity of the patient, clinicians can forget that the diagnosis of a patient should follow the same systematic approach taken with all patients. However, there are more systems to consider and the patients are more fragile.2 A thorough history and physical should always be performed before other tests are required. Although the patient in this scenario had a desaturation, the patient had been at her baseline prior to this event and had been at baseline for the most part afterwards. The event was short and resolved quickly with minimal intervention. She is intermittently tachycardic, but is not tachypneic and has remained afebrile. She shows no signs of upper respiratory infection and does not have a history of a similar event occurring that led to a respiratory illness. Most importantly, her lung exam is at baseline and is within normal limits.

According to the guidelines from the Infectious Diseases Society of America and the American Thoracic Society, chest x-rays (C) should only be done in support of the diagnosis of pneumonia, along with the clinical symptoms.3,4 In this case, the likelihood of the test making a change in management is low given her presentation. When the diagnosis of pneumonia is made, cultures are used to guide the treatment of pneumonia. (One must keep in mind that some patients with tracheostomies may be colonized with certain bacteria and it is difficult to differentiate between an infection and colonization on a culture in this case.) Obtaining a chest computed tomograph (CT) scan can be considered, but the harm of radiation and cost when the diagnosis can be made by other means should be a deterrent. A blood gas can be considered if the patient is toxic appearing with labored breathing to determine effective ventilation, but is not recommended in this setting. It is unlikely to be abnormal in the absence of any clinical signs of potential respiratory failure. Thus, it would most likely not change management nor would benefit the patient. However, in cases of neuromuscular impairment, a blood gas might definitely be helpful due to these patients not always exhibiting labored breathing.

Vignette Continued:

After observation of the patient, you are set to discharge the family home with precautions to return if symptoms worsen. The mother becomes frustrated and upset that you are not doing more tests and labs. She is demanding certain labs and procedures, and wants a Pulmonology consult.

Discussion**:**

Frequently in similar situations as the above scenario, the treating physician may not know the child well if they are not at the home medical institution. Many of the complex care patients can deteriorate quickly, or have abnormal baseline exams, or have unusual presentations of illness. The mother is concerned because she knows that the patient has clinically deteriorated in the past. It is important to keep an open dialogue to hear her concerns.

Shared decision making is important to the clinician-patient relationship. It is key to providing high value care. It has previously been defined as “an approach where clinicians and patients share the best available evidence when faced with the task of making decisions, and where patients are supported to consider options, to achieve informed preferences”.10 Shared decision making has been shown in some studies to improve outcomes.10 In this case, it invites the mother into the decision-making. She will have quality information regarding your thought process and evidence based practices and will be able to understand why certain decisions are being considered.

Also consider creating a comprehensive care plan for each complex care patient and making sure it is reviewed with the family. This way their input would be included and they would be aware of what the care plan is for different clinical scenarios. They should also have some medical records with them. The care plan and medical records will help with every hospital visit or doctor’s appointment, especially in emergency situations where the patient is being seen by a physician who is not familiar with her care. It decreases the ambiguity and anxiety around trying to decide what is the patient’s baseline exam and labs and what is the best course of action based on the patient’s historical signs of illness and historical response. An additional thing to consider is calling the primary care physician if there are significant questions in the care.
