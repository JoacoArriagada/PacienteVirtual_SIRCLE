**Brief Resolved Unexplained Events -**

**What’s *BRUE*ing?**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Learning Objectives:**

At the end of this case, participants will be able to:

1. Discuss how to integrate health care value into decisions for diagnostic testing.\*
2. Use quality, cost and value to guide decisions for diagnostic testing. \*
3. Describe the difference between the classification Brief Resolved Unexplained Event (BRUE) and the old term Apparent Life Threatening Event (ALTE).
4. Identify characteristics of a lower-risk BRUE and apply them to high value-based management decisions.

**Vignette 1:**

Alison Jones is a 10-week-old healthy term baby who presented to the emergency department for evaluation after a brief episode of decreased responsiveness. She was in her normal state of health until 930AM. Approximately 20 minutes after she fed 3oz of formula, mom laid her down and noticed that it appeared that she could not breathe. She seemed to not be breathing for about 20 seconds. She was looking straight ahead, her face turned red and she seemed stiff. Mom picked her up and patted her back. She had some formula come out of her nose and mouth and then she took in a big gasp of air. The entire episode lasted approximately 90 seconds. She is now back to her baseline. She normally feeds about 3oz of formula every 2-3 hours. She does tend to spit up frequently. She usually feeds very well and does not appear to choke. There is no family history of sudden death, cardiac arrhythmias or seizures.

She lives at home with her mother and father. She is their first child. No pets. No smoke exposure.

On physical examination, she is well appearing, non-toxic and interacting appropriately with mom. Her temperature is 37.2C (rectally), pulse is 126 beats per minute, respiration rate is 40 breaths per minute and blood pressure is 86/58. Her anterior fontanelle is soft and flat. She is tracking appropriately. Her heart, lung and abdominal exams are normal. She has no bruises and she moves all her extremities equally. Neurologically, she is alert and her tone and strength are appropriate for age.

Question #1:

Which of the following is the next best step?

1. Sepsis work up including complete blood count (CBC), blood culture, urinalysis and urine culture
2. pH probe for Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease
3. Electroencephalography
4. Computed tomography scan of head
5. Obtain additional history

Correct Answer: E

Discussion (with question prompts):

1. It is important to have a strong working differential diagnosis in order to prevent unnecessary testing. What is Alison’s most likely diagnosis?

Reflux and laryngospasm of gastric contents likely associated with overfeeding

1. What factors on the history and physical point to this diagnosis as being most likely? Is there any other information you would want to obtain?

The following details lead more towards reflux: otherwise normal state of health; episode occurred shortly after feeding; her face turned red rather than cyanotic or pale; when her mom picked her up, she vomited and spit up formula; she quickly returned back to baseline; mom laid her down immediately after feeding.

I would want to know pertinent negatives in history and physical in order to rule out potential pathologies, such as sepsis, seizures or arrhythmias. In addition, you should consider observing a feeding session - this can be invaluable in order to assess for oral dysphagia.

Also, it would be important for residents to ask questions to be able to distinguish between obstructive and central apnea.

1. Discuss definition of BRUE and how it is different than an ALTE. This can be done in small groups of 2-3 individuals or as a large group brainstorming session. Create a Venn Diagram of ALTE, BRUE and where the diagnoses may overlap in the middle.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **ALTE** | **BRUE** |
| * Apnea * Color Change * Tone Change * Choking and Gagging * FRIGHTENS the observer | * 12 months or younger * Lasting less than 1 minute (usually 20-30 seconds) * Resolved (with normal exam and vitals at time of examination) * Unexplained after full history and physical obtained * One or more of the following:   + Cyanosis or pallor   + Absent, decreased or irregular breathing   + Marked change in tone   + Altered level of responsiveness |

BRUE has a strict age limit, is only used if no other explanation available and is not solely based on caregiver’s perceptions regarding whether the event was life threatening.1

Apparent Life Threatening Event (ALTE) was an overarching umbrella term that could include numerous diagnosis not limited only to reflux, upper respiratory events and seizures. Having such a broad potential for diagnosis raised significant challenges for clinicians and parents in the evaluation and care of these infants.2 The risk of a serious underlying disorder or a recurrent event is rather low especially when the infant appears well after the event.3 High risk diagnoses for BRUE are arrhythmia and child abuse.

1. Do you think she can be diagnosed as having a Brief Resolved Unexplainable Event?

No, because according to the definition, BRUE can only be used if there is no other explanation. This baby likely had an episode of reflux and laryngospasm.

1. For each diagnostic test listed below, answer the following questions as you fill out the table:
   1. Pretest probability: What is your overall gestalt of how this test will help you make your diagnosis?
   2. How will this test benefit the patient, if at all?
   3. What is the cost of this test to the patient- think about monetary costs as well as potential for harm and patient experience?
   4. What is the overall value that this test could provide for the patient? (a gestalt of high or low value)

Further discussion points:

Health care value has been defined as Value= Quality/Cost

* 1. Quality encompasses study appropriateness, patient safety, patient outcomes and minimizing healthcare waste
  2. High value care is not the equivalent of choosing the least expensive test, as cost does not equal value.
  3. Cost includes not just financial cost but also non-financial factors, such as potential harms associated with diagnostic delay or diagnostic error.

|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | **Pre-Test Probability** | **Benefits** | **Costs**  **(harms, $, risk, experience)** | **Value (High vs. Low)** |
| **Sepsis work up** | Low | Rule out infectious etiology with higher certainty | Costs of equipment, labs and analysis  Parental anxiety  Potential longer hospitalization; potential for false positive test results; Patient experience- pain | Low |
| **pH probe** | High | Potential to objectively make diagnosis for reflux | Multiple visits or prolonged stay; possibility of false positive results; procedural complications; potential need for further testing (testing cascade) or interventions such as medications (PPI) | Low |
| **EEG** | Low | Could potentially catch an early seizure disorder | False reassurance that EEG is negative; Potential for false positive; Uncomfortable and likely hospitalization;  Parental anxiety | Low |
| **CT scan** | Low | May reassure families that there isn’t a CNS abnormality  May catch the very rare unexpected abuse head trauma or other CNS cause | Radiation exposure; Possible sedation;  Expensive; Potential for false positive results | Low |

**Key Points:**

- A thorough history and physical guides the work-up needed for a patient with BRUE

- Health care value has been defined as Value = Quality/Cost. Quality encompasses study appropriateness, patient safety, patient outcomes and minimizing healthcare waste while cost includes not just financial cost but also non-financial factors, such as potential harms associated with diagnostic delay or diagnostic error\*
