**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Community Acquired Pneumonia - Know Thy Number Needed to Treat**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Vignette 1:**

Greg Fields is a previously healthy 3 yo M who was in his usual state of good health until 4 days ago, when he started to develop low-grade fevers to 101 degrees F, rhinorrhea, congestion, and cough. He had some decreased oral intake, but was otherwise active and playful. At that time, he was seen by his pediatrician who diagnosed him with a viral upper respiratory infection (URI) and sent him home with instructions for supportive care. Four days later, he was again seen in his PCP’s office with improvement of rhinorrhea and congestion, but increased cough. His mother told the pediatrician that his fevers have continued and today he appeared less active, was eating less, and she measured his temperature at 101.1 degrees F. On physical exam, the pediatrician noted bilateral crackles in the bases of the lungs. The patient was also mildly tachypneic, but had no notable retractions or increased work of breathing. His vital signs were significant for RR of 39 and a pulse oximeter reading of 93%.

Greg attends preschool and daycare. He has received all of his immunizations to date.

Discussion:

This patient can be diagnosed with pneumonia based on clinical features of disease (abnormal oxygen saturation, mild respiratory distress, focal lung physical exam findings). This clinical diagnosis can be made without further diagnostic testing. Most likely, given the patient’s initial course and age group, this is a viral pneumonia. The majority of children have viral pneumonias, especially in this age group of 2-4 years.2-7 However we cannot fully exclude a bacterial pneumonia. Even with a focal physical exam finding, a chest xray is not always necessary. Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) Guidelines recommend that routine chest radiography not be used for any patient well-enough to be treated in the outpatient setting, especially if they lack significant hypoxemia, significant respiratory distress, or have not yet failed initial outpatient therapy.1 In the situation where a patient does have any of these features, a chest radiograph may help with determining if the patient has any complications such as a parapneumonic effusion, empyema or necrotizing pneumonia.1 Similarly, in a child with only mild symptoms, a CBC, blood culture, or respiratory viral panel will not offer additional information that will change your treatment plan, i.e. they will provide little to no benefit to the patient and come with a non-incidental risk and cost profile, including pain to the child from phlebotomy and risk of false positive blood cultures.

Discussion:

This patient has presentation most consistent with viral pneumonia (signs of upper and lower respiratory tract infection, non-focal lung exam) and most likely will not benefit from treatment with antibiotics.

The potential benefit of any treatment needs to be weighed against possible adverse effects from the treatment. In the case of antibiotic therapy for CAP in the preschool-aged child, the evidence shows that most of these children will not benefit (due to the viral etiology3,1), and would be unnecessarily exposed to the risk of antibiotic side effects, including diarrhea, C. difficile colitis, rashes, allergic reactions, and antibiotic resistance.22

Current IDSA guidelines recommend that children with respiratory distress and hypoxemia (defined as pulse oximetry reading less than 90%), age less than 6 months, CAP due to resistant pathogens (i.e. MRSA), and those for whom outpatient observation and/or treatment may be suboptimal should be admitted to the hospital for management.1

Vignette continued:

Greg returns 2 days later with higher fever up to 103.5F and now has nasal flaring with crackles noted in the left middle and lower lung fields and absent breath sounds at the left lung base. Pulse oximeter reading is 89%. You place him on supplemental oxygen and decide to admit for further management. Chest X-ray obtained on admission shows lobar consolidation in the LLL.

Discussion:

This patient is demonstrating signs of bacterial disease (acute worsening, focal lung exam and alveolar infiltrates on radiography)23 and is ill enough to be hospitalized, so should be treated with an antibiotic. Moderate quality evidence led to the IDSA guideline recommendation to start ampicillin or penicillin G for fully immunized infants and school-aged children who are hospitalized for CAP. Exceptions to this recommendation include high local S. pneumoniae penicillin resistance, incompletely immunized children, life-threatening illness, and complicated infections (i.e. empyema). In these cases, third-generation cephalosporins and/or antibiotics with MRSA coverage (i.e. vancomycin, clindamycin) may be considered.
