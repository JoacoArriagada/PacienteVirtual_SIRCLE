**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Community Acquired Pneumonia - Know Thy Number Needed to Treat**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Learning Objectives:**

At the end of this vignette, learners will be able to:

1. Discuss the low benefit of blood cultures in affecting outcomes in pediatric community acquired pneumonia (CAP) and the risk of false positives
2. Analyze risks and benefits of radiography in CAP
3. Explain how the epidemiology of CAP can affect antibiotic choice to maximize benefit and minimize risk
4. Compare and contrast cost versus charges\*
5. Explain how to calculate number needed to treat (NNT) and number needed to harm (NNH)\*

**\*Objective is one that relates to general concepts of HVC rather than being disease-specific**

**Vignette 1:**

Greg Fields is a previously healthy 3 yo M who was in his usual state of good health until 4 days ago, when he started to develop low-grade fevers to 101 degrees F, rhinorrhea, congestion, and cough. He had some decreased oral intake, but was otherwise active and playful. At that time, he was seen by his pediatrician who diagnosed him with a viral upper respiratory infection (URI) and sent him home with instructions for supportive care. Four days later, he was again seen in his PCP’s office with improvement of rhinorrhea and congestion, but increased cough. His mother told the pediatrician that his fevers have continued and today he appeared less active, was eating less, and she measured his temperature at 101.1 degrees F. On physical exam, the pediatrician noted bilateral crackles in the bases of the lungs. The patient was also mildly tachypneic, but had no notable retractions or increased work of breathing. His vital signs were significant for RR of 39 and a pulse oximeter reading of 93%.

Greg attends preschool and daycare. He has received all of his immunizations to date.

What imaging and lab testing would you perform to confirm a diagnosis of community acquired pneumonia?

1. Chest XR 1-view
2. Chest XR 2-view
3. Chest XR, CBC, Blood Culture
4. Chest XR, CBC, Blood Culture, Viral Respiratory PCR from Nasal Swab
5. No Testing, Clinical Diagnosis

The correct Answer is E.

Discussion:

This patient can be diagnosed with pneumonia based on clinical features of disease (abnormal oxygen saturation, mild respiratory distress, focal lung physical exam findings). This clinical diagnosis can be made without further diagnostic testing. Most likely, given the patient’s initial course and age group, this is a viral pneumonia. The majority of children have viral pneumonias, especially in this age group of 2-4 years.2-7 However we cannot fully exclude a bacterial pneumonia. Even with a focal physical exam finding, a chest xray is not always necessary. Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) Guidelines recommend that routine chest radiography not be used for any patient well-enough to be treated in the outpatient setting, especially if they lack significant hypoxemia, significant respiratory distress, or have not yet failed initial outpatient therapy.1 In the situation where a patient does have any of these features, a chest radiograph may help with determining if the patient has any complications such as a parapneumonic effusion, empyema or necrotizing pneumonia.1 Similarly, in a child with only mild symptoms, a CBC, blood culture, or respiratory viral panel will not offer additional information that will change your treatment plan, i.e. they will provide little to no benefit to the patient and come with a non-incidental risk and cost profile, including pain to the child from phlebotomy and risk of false positive blood cultures.

**Notes on Cost vs. Charge**

Confusing terminology and obfuscated health care prices frequently convolute conversations regarding cost versus charges. In attempting to make cost-conscious decisions balanced with patient centered care, providing high value care to patients’ pivots on understanding the difference. First for a few definitions:

* Cost: The definition of cost varies based upon perspective.
  + Providers: Cost is the expense incurred to deliver health care to the patient, often represented in both direct and indirect costs. Direct costs are derived from services associated specifically to the care of a patient while indirect costs often are overhead fees and structural components required for a functioning healthcare system.
  + Patients: Cost from the patient perspective is the amount the patient pays for out-of-pocket services.
* Charges are the amount asked by providers for a health care good or service as seen on a medical bill.

The convolution, however, does not end here. Charges are submitted typically from a hospital to the payers—this includes insurance companies who then pass along the “co-pay” to the patient. A co-pay is a fixed amount based off an agreement between the patient and the insurance company. Often, the total charge exacted to the payer and patient is not known to the physician and a wide variation exists in the charge prices of tests and procedures. In attempt to bring transparency to this variation, cost-to-charge conversion ratios for Medicaid/Medicare expenses at individual institutions are public domain and can be acquired upon inquiry.

As more patients and physicians become increasingly cost conscious, physicians possess the obligation to understand the linguistics surrounding the discussion as a way to advocate for high value care. Taking individual responsibility as a health care provider requires intentional alignment of decision making with shared patient and family values in conjunction with responsible evidence based care.

Risks, costs, and benefits of chest x-ray:

With a history and physical exam that are supportive of a diagnosis of pneumonia (viral vs. bacterial), and a patient who is not severely ill (mildly low pulse oximetry, only mild respiratory distress), guidelines recommend not obtaining chest radiography.7 A chest x-ray (CXR) will sometimes provide you with more information, however in many cases, this information may not lead to any meaningful changes in your diagnosis or your decision to treat. From a value-perspective, we can examine the down-stream effects of obtaining a CXR in several cases. In a CXR that *does* have significant findings (i.e. a lobar infiltrate), we will be unlikely to further escalate our antibiotic plan in the setting of only mild disease. In the same vein, with a CXR result that is indeterminate or non-focal in a patient with clear symptoms, we are unlikely to de-escalate our initial plan (i.e. not treat with antibiotics if we were planning to) as the patient may have an early infiltrate that is not visible on radiography yet. This low benefit from radiography should also be balanced by several significant risks. The primary risk is that of exposing children to unnecessary antibiotics. In cases where a clinician is not planning to order antibiotics based on the patient’s history and physical, a CXR with incidental findings has been shown to increase prescriptions of unneeded antibiotics.8 These antibiotics lead to an increased risk of diarrhea, rash, and anaphylaxis with little to no benefit to the patients. Additionally, unneeded CXR’s expose children to ionizing radition. Though the radiation dose from a chest x-ray is low compared to CT scans, it is still preferable to avoid radiation exposure if possible. Young children appear to be 10 times more susceptible to developing cancer from the effects of exposure to ionizing radiation.9 Finally, a CXR ordered when it is not needed creates a risk of incidental findings or misinterpretations that may lead to repeat CXR’s or additional imaging such as CT scans that have a higher radiation exposure burden.10

Risks, costs, and benefits of blood cultures:

Current guidelines do not recommend sending blood cultures in healthy, fully immunized patients who are diagnosed with CAP that can be managed as an outpatient.1 With the majority of CAP in this age group being viral in origin,3,1,11 and most patients recovering well with outpatient antibiotic regimens,12 patients are unlikely to have any further benefit from knowing the specific species of bacteria. In general, patients diagnosed and treated as an outpatient will not receive benefit from sending a blood culture.

The equation becomes more complicated for patients admitted to the hospital. Although current guidelines recommend blood cultures for children requiring hospitalization for presumed bacterial CAP that is moderate to severe,7 several studies have questioned whether all patients admitted to hospitals with CAP should qualify as “moderate to severe”.2,13 The yield of blood cultures in community acquired pneumonia has been estimated to be 1-5%, though some studies have seen yields as high as 7-10%.14-18 Of those cultures that are positive, as many as 15% may be contaminants.15

A more useful metric for blood cultures may be to target patients with higher risk for bacteremia and higher risk for severe complications, where we would want to be sure we are treating the right bacteria. Several studies have identified that patients with pneumonia with effusion/empyema, need for admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), < 6 months of age, or immunosuppressed status are a higher-risk population for true bacteremia.2,13,17 Targeting only this population with blood cultures increases the yield of positive cultures, thereby decreasing the number of blood cultures that will need to be sent to produce a meaningful change in management and decreasing false positives overall.13,15 This targeted approach also decreases cost to the system by eliminating low-yield, low value tests.13

Risks, costs, and benefits of viral respiratory testing:

It has been estimated that 66-86% of CAP has a viral etiology,3,11 however viral testing is usually of limited benefit for several reasons. We can explore these reasons by looking at two groups of patients, those with a high clinical suspicion for bacterial pneumonia and those with a low clinical suspicion of bacterial pneumonia. When a patient has a history and physical exam concerning for a focal bacterial pneumonia, a positive viral test does not rule out the presence of bacterial co-infection or superinfection. Bacterial yield from blood cultures is typically low, but even with this low yield, studies on co-infection have shown that anywhere from 7-20% of cases of pneumonia have both bacterial and viral respiratory pathogens identified.3,6 So the decision whether or not to treat with antibiotics must be based on clinical suspicion for bacterial disease and severity of illness. A positive viral test does not definitively rule out bacterial illness and may not even indicate current viral infection, as viral tests may be positive in asymptomatic individuals, and remain positive for a period after clinical resolution of symptoms.19-21 On the other end of the spectrum, in a patient with no concerning features of bacterial co-infection by exam, history, lab testing, or radiography, IDSA guidelines currently recommend not treating with antibiotics in patients with a positive influenza test.1 This clinical scenario should support a provider in not treating a patient with low risk of bacterial co-infection with antibiotics, especially in preschool aged children where we see a large predominance of viral pneumonia.3,11 Ultimately, when ordering viral testing, we should ask if this test will change management in a significant way, and in most situations, viral testing results will not supersede clinical history and exam. Because of the high cost of most viral testing, and the low chance of results leading to a change in management, it is generally considered a low-value test. Influenza testing during influenza season may be an exception given the available treatment for influenza that may be of some benefit. However testing for influenza in particular, without a shot-gun approach to viral testing, is likely a wiser and more cost effective approach

Key points**:**

* Consider cost, risks, and benefits in evaluating testing such as CXR and viral respiratory panels for patients
* Even small risks and costs should be avoided if only negligible benefit is found\*
* Blood culture yield and contaminant rate can be used to consider whether a blood culture is warranted.

Question #2**:**

What would you do next in the management of this patient

1. Administer ceftriaxone IM
2. Admit for IV antibiotic
3. Anticipatory guidance and send home
4. Obtain a CXR
5. Prescribe amoxicillin

The correct answer is C.

Discussion:

This patient has presentation most consistent with viral pneumonia (signs of upper and lower respiratory tract infection, non-focal lung exam) and most likely will not benefit from treatment with antibiotics.

These questions can be first discussed in small groups, which are then shared with the large group or in large group.

*What are the most common etiologies of CAP in this age group, and how do you decide on the need for antibiotic therapy in a child this age with the diagnosis of CAP?*

Viruses are responsible for the vast majority of cases of CAP in preschool-aged children, and as such do not typically require antibiotic therapy. 3,1 A recent, large epidemiologic study of the etiologies of CAP in children found that among children hospitalized for pneumonia, 66% tested positive for viruses only, 7% bacteria only, and 8% both viruses and bacteria.3 As such, current IDSA guidelines state that antibiotics are not routinely required for preschool-aged children with CAP well enough to be managed in the outpatient setting.1 [See Section V, recommendation 41]

The potential benefit of any treatment needs to be weighed against possible adverse effects from the treatment. In the case of antibiotic therapy for CAP in the preschool-aged child, the evidence shows that most of these children will not benefit (due to the viral etiology3,1), and would be unnecessarily exposed to the risk of antibiotic side effects, including diarrhea, C. difficile colitis, rashes, allergic reactions, and antibiotic resistance.22

*What factors do you consider when deciding whether a patient with pneumonia should be admitted to the hospital?*

Current IDSA guidelines recommend that children with respiratory distress and hypoxemia (defined as pulse oximetry reading less than 90%), age less than 6 months, CAP due to resistant pathogens (i.e. MRSA), and those for whom outpatient observation and/or treatment may be suboptimal should be admitted to the hospital for management.1

Key points:

* Viruses cause the majority of pneumonias in children, particularly in those under 5 years of age
* Consider the most likely etiology when deciding on whether to treat a preschool-aged child with antimicrobials
* Hospital admission for pneumonia should be reserved for young infants and/or those with hypoxemia, respiratory distress, or resistant pathogens

Greg returns 2 days later with higher fever up to 103.5F and now has nasal flaring with crackles noted in the left middle and lower lung fields and absent breath sounds at the left lung base. Pulse oximeter reading is 89%. You place him on supplemental oxygen and decide to admit for further management. Chest X-ray obtained on admission shows lobar consolidation in the LLL.

Question #3:

Which antibiotic will you order empirically?

1. Amoxicillin
2. Ampicillin
3. Ampicillin plus azithromycin
4. Ceftriaxone
5. No antibiotic

The correct answer is B.

Discussion:

This patient is demonstrating signs of bacterial disease (acute worsening, focal lung exam and alveolar infiltrates on radiography)23 and is ill enough to be hospitalized, so should be treated with an antibiotic. Moderate quality evidence led to the IDSA guideline recommendation to start ampicillin or penicillin G for fully immunized infants and school-aged children who are hospitalized for CAP. Exceptions to this recommendation include high local S. pneumoniae penicillin resistance, incompletely immunized children, life-threatening illness, and complicated infections (i.e. empyema). In these cases, third-generation cephalosporins and/or antibiotics with MRSA coverage (i.e. vancomycin, clindamycin) may be considered.

Studies have shown that using narrower-spectrum beta-lactam antibiotics (amoxicillin, ampicillin) as first-line treatment for CAP does not lead to higher rates of treatment failure or other adverse events.24

The addition of a macrolide is recommended when M. pneumoniae or C. pneumoniae infection is strongly considered. A recent large epidemiologic study of CAP in hospitalized children found M. pneumoniae infection to be 6 times more common in children 5 and older than in younger patients. In this case, the patient’s age makes her less likely to benefit from the addition of a macrolide antibiotic.3 Of note, even in cases of mycoplasma-confirmed infections, there is not clear evidence of benefit of treatment with a macrolide.25

1. Define Number Needed to Treat (NNT) and Number Needed to Harm (NNH).
   1. Ask learners if a disease has 100% mortality and therapy reduces to 50%, how many patients need to be treated to benefit 1? (the answer is 2). The converse can also be asked for NNH. If a treatment has an adverse effect in 25% of those receiving it, how many would need to be treated to experience one adverse effect (the answer is 4).
   2. Number needed to treat – number of patients needed to treat for one to have benefit. Calculated, 100/percent absolute risk reduction
   3. Number needed to harm – number of patients needed to treat for one to experience an adverse effect. Calculated, 100/percent absolute risk increase

1. What is your threshold NNT? Maximum NNT to justify treatment? How does knowing the NNH help? There is of course no simple answer to this question as it can be complex, informed by patient and provider risk tolerance of the condition compared with the possible adverse effects, as well as other associated costs of treatment or failure to treat. Also, the NNT and NNH cannot necessarily be directly compared, but must be weighed against the actual severity of harms. For instance, one might weight the risk of treatment failure much higher than the risk of candidal infection, and therefore allow for a higher NNT or lower NNH. However, with a more serious adverse effect (i.e. anaphylaxis), the NNH would need to be significantly higher than the NNT to justify benefit in initiating treatment.

Key Points:

* Narrow-spectrum beta-lactam antibiotics (amoxicillin, ampicillin) are appropriate first-line treatment for patients with mild-moderate CAP
* NNT and NNH are useful statistical tools available to guide practitioners in the practice of HVC\*
