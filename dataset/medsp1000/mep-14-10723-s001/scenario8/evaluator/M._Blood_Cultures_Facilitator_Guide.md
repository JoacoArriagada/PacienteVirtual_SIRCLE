**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Blood Cultures - Fevers, Fight Songs, and Fagan Nomograms**

Learning Objectives:

At the end of this case, participants will be able to

1. Incorporate the values of patients and families into the care plan\*
2. Identify potential harms and costs of obtaining unnecessary blood cultures
3. Define the Likelihood Ratio (LR) and interpret its meaning in a clinical scenario\*
4. Use a Fagan Nomogram to determine a patient’s probability of having disease based on the pre-test probability and the LR of the test result\*

Discussion:

The need for answers to diagnostic puzzles often drives medical professionals to pursue extensive diagnostic evaluations and even intervention often without considering the patient’s or family’s goals of care.3 This child is 11 years old and from a small community 3 hours away from the hospital. Her father coaches the local high school track team and her mom coaches the cheerleading squad. Homecoming is this upcoming weekend. As the medical team, at what point do we stop hunting if our patient is improving? How important is finding the answer to the medical team? To the patient and family?

For children hospitalized with fever of unknown origin, these hospitalizations often are extremely stressful for parents. Typically, the child has been sick for over a week, had multiple visits to the PCP, numerous outpatient tests, and the patient and family are usually exhausted emotionally and mentally by the time they are admitted to the hospital. Additionally, fever phobia and the medicalization of elevated temperatures often lead families to have high expectations for “answers.” Coupled with the fact that the “easy” things “have been ruled out,” families are often afraid that their child may have a serious or life threatening disease such as cancer. Straightforward conversations eliciting the patient’s and family’s fears, concerns, and expectations can set up opportunities for shared decision making and matching the medical team’s goals of care to the family’s values.

A variation of the following script can be useful:

“I know this process has been exhausting for your whole family. It is really difficult to watch your child be sick for so long and feel like you can’t make them better. I understand you’ve been back and forth to the doctor several times and now have been hospitalized here with us. What are the things that most concern you about Rosa? What do you hope for this hospitalization? We want to partner with you to not only make your daughter feel better, but also to ensure that our goals of care are in line with what you are hoping for. Right now, we’ve done a thorough work-up. Lots of different specialists have seen and examined Rosa. She’s had multiple lab tests, imaging, and even undergone invasive procedures to try to find a source. All of which have helped to rule out a lot of major life threatening diagnoses, including some of the really scary things like cancer. Fortunately, they have all been negative and Rosa is now improving! That is one of the advantages of a tincture of time. Kids’ bodies are amazing and often are smarter than we are. Our tests are great and can give us a lot of useful information, but no test is perfect and some tests can ultimately lead to more harm than good. At this point, since Rosa is looking so much better, what are you hoping for?”

Question 2:

These conversations are ideally initiated at the time of admission, as opposed to at the end of the diagnostic evaluation. If you were the admitting intern, how might you adapt the above script to align expectations and prepare the family from the beginning?

Discussion**:**

Ultimately, as medical professionals we strive to improve the health of patients and their families, not simply find answers. The tendency towards over-work-up and over-diagnosis contributes to rising healthcare costs and poor stewardship of resources.2 During discussions with families, it may be preferable to focus on “What matters,” rather than “What’s the matter.” Having straightforward conversations with families regarding their goals and values strengthens the team’s ability to provide the highest quality care for patients and develop trusting relationships.

Key Points:

* Early conversations with patients and families regarding their values and goals of care strengthen a therapeutic alliance and allows for shared decision-making\*
* Incorporating patient level factors into decision-making is crucial, especially when standards of care are nonexistent, conflicting, dated, or contrary to existing evidence\*

Question 3:

On the morning of hospital day #6, Rosa has not had a fever in over 24 hours. You are planning on discharging her. You receive a text page from Rosa’s bedside nurse: “Rosa has a temperature of 38.8, but otherwise appears well. Do you want to draw a blood culture?”

You evaluate Rosa and find that her other vital signs are reassuring and that her physical examination is normal.

Which of the following is the best next step in Rosa’s management?

Obtain a repeat blood culture now

Obtain a blood culture and start empiric antibiotics since she spiked another fever after a period of being afebrile

Monitor fevers and repeat a blood culture if she remains persistently febrile

1. Engage in a discussion about risk estimates of pathogenic bacteremia with parents that would help the team make an informed decision.

The Correct Answer is D.

Discussion:

Obtaining a blood culture in this case could benefit the patient through the potential to identify a pathogenic infection causing her persistent fevers and subsequently to direct appropriate antibiotic therapy. It is important to remember that every medical intervention has potential harm, including “routine” labs. Engaging families in discussion allows for centering the conversation on a shared mental model for decision-making, improving trust and communication. It allows for the team to discuss benefits and harms, while specifically acknowledging and addressing family concerns or worries. This form of shared decision making provides an opportunity for the team to engage in truly informed decision making. As stated best by Dr. Kevin Powell, “Clinical judgment is balancing the risk of missing something preventable that will actually harm the child against the harms of more tests”.4 What are the harms of obtaining blood cultures?

Small group activity:

Hand out learner copies of the following table, with the right column empty. Ask participants to brainstorm potential harms of obtaining blood cultures.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Potential Harms** |  |
| Emotional stress | * Sleep disruption * Patient anxiety/discomfort * Parental worry over results * Over medicalization |
| Physical stress | * Additional needle stick, potentially multiple * Sleep disruption of parents and child |
| Cost | * Charge for blood draw and blood culture processing * Charge for antibiotic sensitivities * Additional bed charge per day for extended hospitalization as awaiting results or if contaminant identified * Charge for antibiotic |
| Prolonged patient stay if repeat cultures are ordered or if empiric antibiotics are initiated | * Additional financial cost to the healthcare system (loss of bed for another patient) * Patient/family missing out on life events * Antibiotic exposure and resistance * Potential development of *C. difficile colitis* * Newly discovered allergy * Additional invasive testing and management (i.e. lumbar puncture) * Subspecialty consultation |

Key points:

* Consider the clinical indications and potential benefit specific to your patient before ordering a blood culture for fever
* There are potential benefits, harms, and direct and indirect cost to every intervention. For blood cultures, a few of the risks and costs include complications of blood draw, peripheral IV placement, contaminants, unnecessary antibiotics, hospital lab charges, and emotional affects on patient.

Question 4:

A set of blood cultures are drawn overnight (patient’s fourth of hospitalization) and instead of discharging her, she remains hospitalized without initiation of antibiotics. She remains afebrile. However 26 hours later, the blood culture grows gram-positive cocci in clusters

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management now that the new culture is positive?

* 1. Repeat blood culture
  2. Repeat blood culture only if she develops another fever
  3. Repeat blood culture and start empiric antibiotics
  4. Wait for final identification of organism

The correct answer is D.

Discussion:

The Likelihood Ratio (LR) is the likelihood that a given test result would be expected in a patient with the suspected disorder compared to the likelihood that that same result would be expected in a patient without the suspected disorder.

Mathematically, the formula is:

Probability (test result/disease)

LR (test result) = ----------------------------------------------------

Probability (test result/no disease)

Likelihood ratios quantify the degree to which a particular test result changes the probability of disease in a patient.

#### Neg. Test Pre-test probability Positive Test

|-----------------|----------------|---------------|---------------|

1% 10% 50% 90% 99%

**Probability of Disease**

For dichotomous tests, such as a blood culture, LR’s are positive or negative. The positive likelihood ratio (LR+) is the degree to which the probability of disease increases if the test is positive. Accordingly, the negative likelihood ratio (LR-) is the degree to which the probability of disease decreases if the test is negative. Likelihood ratios close to 1 have minimal clinical significance since there is no distinguishable difference between the measured event and the background events occurring around it (posterior probability).

See Table below:

**Interpretation of Likelihood Ratios**

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| + LR | Negative LR | Effect |
| > 10 | < 0.1 | Large or strong |
| Between 5 and 10 | Between 0.1 and 0.2 | Moderate |
| Between 2 and 5 | Between 0.2 and 0.5 | Small or weak |

#### The pre-test probability of a febrile 11 year old hospitalized patient having a pathogenic bacteremia is low. While the precise prevalence is unknown, the widespread use of the heptavalent pneumococcal vaccine (PCV 7) has significantly reduced the overall incidence of bacteremia to less than 0.7% in vaccinated children 3 to 36 months. 5,6

Next**,** calculate the post-test probability. To do this, convert the pre-test probability to pre-test odds, then multiply the pre-test odds by LR and convert the resultant post-test odds to post-test probability. This is a lot of math.

* 2 simplier ways:
  + Use a smartphone app (ie: EBM Tools, DynaMed, Epocrates)
  + Use the Fagan nomogram that estimates the post-test probability of a disease if the pre-test probability and the LR are known.

To use the Fagan nomogram (Figure 1) to predict the probability that a blood culture in febrile child with growth after 24 hours represents a true bacteremia:

* Pre-test probability of being bacteremic, based on the literature, 0.7%
* Positive LR is 2.25
* Resulting post-test probability: 1.75% that the positive blood culture represents a true bacteremia

To apply this then to a specific patient, like our patient Rosa, one must adjust the likelihood ratio formula to reflect the specific contextual factors for that patient. The general numerator that we used to calculate the above LR is the “probability of a true bacteremia with a positive blood culture that grew out after 24 hours (sensitivity).” To apply the formula to Rosa, the numerator would need to read the “probability of a well-appearing individual with 3 prior negative blood cultures within the same clinical setting having a true bacteremia with positive blood culture that grew out after 24 hours.” Although we do not have the data to calculate that numerator, one can infer that the sensitivity is likely less than the 4.5% predicted by the literature, thus making the post-test probability of Rosa’s positive blood culture representing a true infection <1.75%.

Rosa stayed in the hospital an additional 2 nights due to the blood culture obtained, waiting for speciation (Coagulase-negative staphylococci) as well as to be afebrile for an additional 24 hours.
