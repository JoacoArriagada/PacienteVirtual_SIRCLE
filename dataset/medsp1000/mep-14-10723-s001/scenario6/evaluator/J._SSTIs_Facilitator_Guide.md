**Pediatric High Value Care Vignette:**

**Skin and Soft Tissue Infections (SSTI)**

**Facilitator Guide**

**Vignette 2:**

Roy is a 7-year-old male who presents to Urgent Care with a two-day history of a red, painful area on his right buttock. He reports that there was no specific trauma to this area but that he had participated in several outdoor games over the past several weeks at various camps. He has had several bug bites from being outdoors. His mother believes he may have had a low-grade fever the night before but did not take his temperature. He has no chronic medical conditions and takes no medications. He has no known allergies to medication.

His physical examination is notable for an oral temperature of 37.7°C, a heart rate (HR) of 78 beats per minute (bpm), a blood pressure (BP) of 94/70 mm Hg, and respiratory rate (RR) of 12 per minute. He is anxious about having to lie on his back as he states it hurts to lie on the lesion. His CV, Lung and Abdomen exams are unremarkable. On examination of his right buttock, there is an erythematous, warm, tender, 4 x 3 centimeter area on the right, with a fluctuant area that has a small indentation noted in the upper pole with a small scab. No other remarkable findings.

Question #1:

Which next step in management would be the highest value approach?

1. Start antimicrobial therapy with clindamycin
2. Obtain labs, blood culture and start IV clindamycin
3. Incision and drainage (I&D) with culture of drainage
4. Perform an ultrasound-guided needle aspiration
5. Ultrasonography of the area for drainage

Answer:

C (or E as resources allow)

Discussion:

Patients often present to care with an indeterminate SSTI, without obvious purulent drainage to differentiate an abscess from simple cellulitis. In these cases, further investigation and observation may be needed. Determining the need for I&D with or without ultrasonography is based on availability and experience with that modality. There is increasing evidence that bedside ultrasound has superior sensitivity and specificity for determining drainable collections compared to physical exam alone.8 If your facility has the resources and trained ultrasonographers, this modality maybe of value to avoid possible unnecessary I&D.

This patient has a mild to moderate cutaneous skin abscess. Incision and drainage is definitive therapy and has proven superior to antibiotics alone. Needle aspiration has demonstrated variable yield and studies of this method are limited to the era of *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, prior to the rise in CA-MRSA. Although the 2011 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines recommend wound cultures in certain circumstances, the routine use of wound cultures in uncomplicated abscesses in otherwise healthy individuals is often unnecessary.1  Wound cultures are likely to be of greater benefit than blood cultures, CBC, CRP and other labs in the otherwise well-appearing patient.

Adjunctive therapy with antibiotics compared to I&D alone may not provide increased value, though the size of the abscess may be a helpful distinguishing feature. In a prospective observational study of a pediatric population with culture-confirmed CA-MRSA skin and soft tissue abscesses, Lee et al. found that patients with abscesses less than or equal to 5 cm in diameter treated with I&D and antibiotics had no increase in cure rate compared to those treated with I&D alone. However, patients with an abscess greater than 5 cm in diameter treated with I&D alone were more likely to fail therapy.9 Duong et al. conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized controlled trial in a pediatric emergency department comparing 10 days of oral TMP/SMX versus placebo after I&D and found no difference in overall failure rate; however, the patients receiving antibiotic had developed fewer new lesions at the 10-day follow-up (26% in placebo group versus 13% in antibiotic group). This effect abated by the 3-month follow-up visit, with no significant difference in new lesions between the two groups. In this study, size of the abscess did not correlate with treatment failure.10 This evidence generally supports avoiding antibiotics after I&D; however, patient-level factors such as access to care and concern for new lesions should factor into shared decision-making with families.

Question #2:

After the procedure, Roy's mother is concerned about this recurring. After asking about her family and social history you determine that his father has a history of recurrent abscesses requiring treatment. Although Roy has never had a SSTI before, she would like to be able to prevent this from happening again.

Your response to this mother is...

1. That recurrence will not likely occur once the patient is done with antibiotics.
2. That with the family history of likely CA-MRSA that recurrence is possible
3. That eradication of the organism is difficult but that some strategies with body hygiene and decontamination may be helpful.
4. Roy's father should get his doctor to treat him with oral antibiotics and that should decrease the chance of recurrence in the household.
5. B and C
6. All of the above

Answer:

E

Discussion:

This patient’s story and family history are concerning for colonization for community-associated MRSA and recurrent skin and soft tissue infection is characteristic of this strain, with estimates of recurrence ranging from 10% to 23.8%.10,11 Although these recurrences may be due to infection with a different strain, others result from persistent cutaneous carriage of the original strain after resolution of the initial infection. The spread of CA-MRSA to household contacts is problematic. Zafar et al. found that 20% of household contacts of patients with community-associated MRSA skin and soft-tissue infections carried MRSA, with half the MRSA strains related to the patient's infective isolate. Previous studies have reported household MRSA-carriage rates of 14.5%. Evidence suggests that more than 60% of households of children hospitalized with community-associated MRSA infections include one or more family members who had a presumed MRSA infection in the previous 6 months.13

Currently there is no strong evidence for how to treat MRSA carrier status. It may be possible to break the cycle of recurrent infection with the combined use of systemic antibiotics and topical antiseptics. The likelihood of clinical infection after colonization by community-associated MRSA appears to be considerable. At this point the strongest evidence suggests decolonization should not be routine but considered in select cases when the patient develops recurrent SSTI despite optimizing wound care and hygiene measures. If decolonization is done, oral antimicrobial therapy is not standard or recommended.1 Nasal decolonization with mupirocin can be done twice daily for 5-10 days with or without concurrent body decolonizing regimens such as a skin antiseptic solution (chlorhexidine) or dilute bleach baths for 5-10 days. Daily decontamination of personal items can be done but with weak evidence that it is effective. If recurrent abscesses develop they should be cultured and drained early, followed with appropriate antibiotics for 5-10 days. Patients with recurrent staphylococcal infections despite appropriate therapy and management, frequent infections, or resistant strains should be evaluated for neutrophil disorder, especially if it starts at a young age and persists.1,13

Key Points:

* I&D is first line treatment for simple abscess.
* Antibiotic therapy is reserved for severe, extensive or rapidly progressing abscess with or without cellulitis.
* In non-purulent cellulitis or indeterminate SSTI, ultrasound if available may be done to exclude drainable abscess.
* Preventive education on personal hygiene and optimal wound care strategies should be done with all patients to avoid recurrent infections.
* Decolonization to be considered if recurrent SSTI with patient or in household members despite optimal wound therapy, appropriate personal, and environmental hygiene.
* Oral antimicrobials are for active infections and not for decolonization.
