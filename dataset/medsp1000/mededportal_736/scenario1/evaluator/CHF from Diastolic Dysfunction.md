# 1. **Title**: A human-patient simulation of a patient with undiagnosed congestive heart failure from diastolic dysfunction who presents preoperatively for an elective trans-urethral resection of the prostate (TURP). 

# 2. **Goal and Target Audience**:  This simulation was developed for a medical student anesthesiology elective simulation course.  We also believe this simulation could be valuable for anesthesiology residents. For the first four years (2002-2006) we did this simulation by running it on a Laerdal® SimMan™ simulator while manually changing vital signs.  We have now programmed the scenario and are eager to objectively analyze participants’ performance.

The scenario focuses on an area of anesthesiology that is sometimes difficult to teach, and probably underrepresented in much medical student anesthesiology curricula-preoperative assessment, evaluation, and treatment.  The goal of the scenario is that participants will recognize the patient’s CHF, and, after consulting with the patient’s surgeon, cancel his surgery for that day.  Participants should also arrange for follow up care for the patient by conferring with a simulated internal medicine or primary care physician.  During the debriefing we point out that some anesthesiologists would start acute therapy for the patient’s hypertension, and as part of systems-based practices an experienced anesthesiologist would also contact the operating room staff.

# 3. **Overview of the scenario**:

## 3.1. <u>Pre simulation</u>: 

Participants are assigned to provide peri-operative care for a 74-year-old man having an elective TURP.  They are given a preoperative history and physical exam (attached), dated two weeks prior to the surgery, which states the patient reported shortness of breath.  According to the H &P a chest x-ray, electrocardiogram, and lab values were ordered, but results are not reported.

In pre-simulation material participants are given a review article1 describing the TURP syndrome and its anesthetic implications.  Immediately before performing the simulation participants are asked to present the patient to an anesthesiology faculty facilitating the scenario. We focus the discussion on the TURP syndrome and anesthetic techniques appropriate for TURP (general or regional anesthesia), but we minimize questions or concerns about the patient’s medical condition (e.g. “You’ll have to talk to the patient about that”).

## 3.2. <u>Simulation</u>:

### 3.2.1. *The simulated patient* (written for a Laerdal® SimMan™) is in a preoperative holding area.  Participants are to perform a history and physical exam, recognize the patient’s CHF, cancel the surgery, consult with the urologist and patient’s primary care physician, and notify the OR staff that the patient will not be having surgery. We anticipate that a number of participants will begin treatment of the patient’s hypertension and CHF.

### 3.2.2. *The optimal performance* is to perform a history & physical assessment, place monitors on the patient, start a peripheral IV, and treat the patient (see below for treatment objectives).  Participants should order an ECG, chest X-ray, and may order other lab studies. Participants should then communicate their findings with the urologist, cancel the patient’s surgery for that day, and then communicate with the operating room staff and a primary care physician who will assume follow-up care for the simulated patient. 

### 3.2.3. Consenting the patient to anesthesia is a “fail” in the scenario. The simulation faculty has the options of stopping the scenario and doing a complete debriefing then, or, as we choose, may review the history and physical with the participant as the “attending who has just shown up,” and help the participant recognize the situation.  Once that recognition has occurred we restart the scenario and let the participant continue.

## 3.3. Environment

### 3.3.1. *Lab Set Up* –A pre-operative holding area

### 3.3.2. *Manikin Set Up* – This scenario was written for a SimManTM simulator.   We do not use moulage or make up to make the mannequin appear cyanotic, but that could be tried. The mannequin is sitting up right and unmonitored.  It has a respiratory rate of 24 breaths/minute, crackles for breath sounds, a BP of 180/110, an SpO2 of 90%, and a heart rate of 110 with normal sinus rhythm and left ventricular hypertrophy QRS morphology.  We would program an S4 heart sound, if that were available.

### 3.3.3. *Props* – A peripheral IV, simple face mask, and oxygen source are needed.  Drugs programmed for use in the simulation include metoprolol, esmolol, labetalol, nitroprusside, hydralazine, fenoldapam, verapamil, sublingual nifedipine, furosemide, enalapril, and phenylephrine.  A 12-lead ECG (displayed below) shows sinus tachycardia, left axis deviation, and LVH with strain.  A chest X-ray (below) shows mild to moderate CHF. Electrolyte and blood count lab values are available, as described below.  Video recording capability should be available to record the learner’s experience for subsequent review and evaluation.

### 3.3.4. *Distracters* – The TURP syndrome discussion distracts the participants’ attention from preoperative evaluation.

## 3.4. Actors

### 3.4.1. *Roles* –Holding-area nurse, patient voice, urologist, OR staff, attending anesthesiologist, and primary care physician.

### 3.4.2. *Who may play them* – Anyone trained in the objectives of the scenario.  In our practice this is usually one simulation facilitator.

### 3.4.3. *Action Role* 

#### 3.4.3.1. Nurse: orients and proctors the participants.  Provides requested medications, equipment and laboratory studies.

#### 3.4.3.2. Patient: answers questions as per scripted H & P.

#### 3.4.3.3. Urologist: listens to participant’s presentation and asks about treatment options and rescheduling procedure.

#### 3.4.3.4. Primary Care Physician: listens to presentation and collaborates on patient care.  Can ask questions related to further preoperative workup and optimization before rescheduling. 

#### 3.4.3.5. OR staff: Acknowledge the participant’s notification.

**Event:** Furosemide, 20-40 mg

![](images/f52fc924ab42bf28a0fa982c36926d6bda84a77a80a5c18ecb49c1b4059e708f.jpg)

**Trend:** 

	**Literature:** Furosemide lowers blood pressure by decreasing intra- and extra-vascular fluid by acting on the kidney as a loop diuretic2.  It will increase the patient’s oxygen saturation and decrease the respiratory rate by removing fluid from the extra-vascular space of the lungs,which, according to local clinical experts, should also result in a decrease in heart rate. Published onset of action is 5 minutes, with duration of action on the order of hours2.

**Comment from AS:**  The onset of action is reported to be 2 to 5 minutes with a peak effect seen within 30 minutes4.  The clinical reactions to the pharmacotherapy may take longer than programmed, but, again, this was done to demonstrate the clinical benefit of the agent within the scenario’s time frame.

**Event:** <u>Greeting</u>

**Action:** Marks time T=0 for the scenario.

	**WRM Comments:** A clearly demarcated start is helpful in a scenario as it anchors the participant’s actions into a time frame. # 6. **Instructor’s Notes**

## 6.1. Anticipated events

### 6.1.1. This scenario can be accomplished with the participant performing events in any order.  However, we anticipate that experts would perform the scenario as: Greeting, H&P, monitors placed (pulse oximeter, ECG, and non-invasive BP), oxygen placed, order chest x-ray and 12-lead ECG, treat blood pressure appropriately, administer furosemide, call surgeon and primary care physician (PCP), cancel the case, discuss situation with patient, and alert OR staff.    

## 6.2. Potential complications/error paths

### 6.2.1. The only disqualification error path is to consent to anesthesia.

### 6.2.2. Other errors within the simulation include choosing an inappropriate drug for the patient, which causes the described changes in vital signs.  Again, the inappropriate choice of beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers needs to be carefully debriefed.

### 6.2.3. Many MS IVs wish to order an echocardiogram or stress test.  They tend to think like primary care doctor and suggest admitting him to the hospital.  During the debriefing I point out the role anesthesiologists would play in this patient’s work up and treatment and how they would work with the PCP to further this patient’s treatment and prepare him for the OR at a later time.  

### 6.2.4. Some students give medication to treat the hypertension or oxygen for the low saturations, but then are concerned when the vital signs are not immediately improved. The facilitator needs to encourage the participant to wait for a few moments to see the effects of their interventions.  

### 6.2.5. All of our medical-student participants have been coached into calling the surgeon, arranging for follow up care, and alerting the OR staff.
