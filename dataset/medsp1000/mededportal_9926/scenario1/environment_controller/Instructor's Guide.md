**Instructor Guide**: Respiratory distress in the recovery room following general anesthesia for total thyroidectomy (simulation)

**Conceptual background**

This scenario involves a patient in the recovery room complaining of difficulty breathing following general anesthetic for total thyroidectomy. The scenario was developed for anesthesiology residents as a supplement to their current traditional curriculum of clinical training and didactic teaching. The simulation environment offers the opportunity to reinforce what residents were experiencing in the operating room and discussing in their lectures. Depending on the experience of the learner and the goals for the curriculum, the debriefing session can focus purely on clinical objectives or non-technical skills such as calling for assistance, working as an interdisciplinary team and improving communication.

**Practical implementation**

The simulator environment should be set-up to look like a post-anesthesia recovery room, as this is where the scenario takes place. The resources available to the learner should be whatever is standard in the recovery room where they work. A high-fidelity mannequin is ideal for this scenario so that continuous vital signs are available, and the mannequin can react to the actions of the learner. Moulage on the neck representing a surgical incision with hematoma increases fidelity. This patient will require intubation. The learner may ask for a glidescope or fiberoptic intubation cart. If your simulation center has this equipment, they may be used. If not, you can notify the learner that all of the glidescopes and fiberoptic equipment are currently unavailable (being used in the operating room, broken, being cleaned). A cricothyroidotomy kit and scalpel should be available. A crash cart with defibrillator should be available. The length of the scenario is meant to be ~15 minutes, followed by debriefing.

**Successful implementation**

This scenario has been utilized with junior and senior anesthesiology residents. There is variation in management of this scenario based on level of training, prior experience and attitude. The most junior residents will be unsure as to how best manage the airway and will most likely always request assistance. They are less aggressive in their discussion with the recovery room nurse and surgeon. There is not much discussion regarding differential diagnosis or anticipation of what should occur next. Senior residents feel more comfortable managing the airway and are often surprisingly assertive in their discussions with the surgeon. Any learner who has had prior surgical training will be more likely to open the incision at the bedside.

Even following successful intubation the patient will still continue to desaturate and progress to cardiac arrest. This forces the learner to consider additional etiologies for respiratory insufficiency including aspiration or pulmonary embolus. They also must consider myocardical ischemia or arrhythmia resulting in cardiac arrest.

The roles of the confederates are important not only to the fidelity of the simulation, but to guide the discussion during the scenario. The surgeon can be quite hesitant to open the neck and force the anesthesia learner to do it if they feel it is absolutely necessary. Or the surgeon can be only involved peripherally, tied up with another patient, and leave the decisions to the anesthesia learner. The recovery room nurse should be helpful, but not offer any information. One event that occurs in the case is that the nurse flushes the IV and the patient receives an inadvertent bolus of remifentanil. The nurse should announce they are flushing the IV; the learner may or may not pay attention. Communication between the nurse, surgeon and programmer is advantageous if available.

Moulage on the neck is very helpful to increase the fidelity of the scenario. We use a midline bandage with a hematoma underneath for the learner to examine and manipulate. Equipment to perform a cricothyroidotomy should also be available.
