**Additional Topics for Discussion and/or Scenario Director Education:**

1. **Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS)**
   1. ARS is a set of signs and symptoms that occurs after an individual has been exposed to high levels of ionizing radiation, specifically gamma, x-ray, or neutron radiation, over a relatively short period of time.3,4
   2. In the early phases, symptomatology can be nonspecific, so related differential diagnoses may include aplastic anemia (only differentiated from ARS via the history), sepsis, drug reaction, IBD, and thallium exposure.5
   3. ARS can be divided into a few different subsyndromes depending on the magnitude of dose absorbed. The hematopoietic subsyndrome occurs at radiation doses greater than or equal to about 1 Gray (Gy).6 The gastrointestinal subsyndrome occurs at doses greater than or equal to about 6 Gy.6 The neurovascular subsyndrome occurs at doses greater than or equal to about 8 Gy.6 Another subsyndrome of ARS is Cutaneous Radiation Syndrome (CRS), which describes the effects of ionizing radiation (particularly gamma or neutron radiation, but in some instances, beta particles) on the skin. Although it is not one of the three classical subsyndromes of ARS, the threshold for the cutaneous subsyndrome is about 3 Gy.7,8
   4. The LD50 at 60 days for acute, whole-body radiation exposure is 3.5-4 Gy without supportive care and 5-6 Gray with supportive care.6
   5. Management of ARS
      1. Cutaneous
         1. Priorities for treating this patient’s cutaneous pathology are infection control, wound care, and pain management.1
            1. Topical medications to consider include Class II-III steroids, antibiotics, and antihistamines.
         2. Burn/Reconstructive Plastic Surgery should be consulted in these patients, albeit this may not necessarily fall within the responsibilities of the EM physician.
      2. Hematopoietic
         1. For a grade 2 (H2) patient such as the one in this simulation, admission to the hospital and administration of cytokines (colony stimulating factors, CSF) to regenerate immune cells is indicated.6,9
            1. The FDA has recently approved four medications (CSFs) for the treatment of ARS, specifically the hematopoietic effects: filgrastim, pegfilgrastim, sargramostim, and romiplostim.
         2. Preventing or mitigating further neutropenia and sepsis are key components of the management of the hematopoietic subsyndrome of ARS.6 Besides cytokines as just described, antimicrobial treatment is also warranted due to the increased risk of infection. This is important because “patients with radiation-induced immunosuppression have minimal or absent clinical signs and symptoms of an infection.”6
         3. Infectious Diseases should be consulted for these patients, albeit this may not necessarily fall within the responsibilities of the EM physician.
2. **Patient Handling/Hospital Operations**
   1. Decontamination Considerations
      1. In this case, the patient was exposed to ionizing (gamma) radiation, not contaminated with radioactive material emitting ionizing radiation. This distinction is important. Therefore, the patient would not have needed decontamination.
      2. When obtaining the history and assessing whether decontamination is necessary, it is important to note that just removing someone’s clothing can remove up to 80-90% of contamination and washing the skin with plain soap and water (i.e., taking a shower) will remove 95-99% of any contamination on that skin.10,11 Even if our patient has been contaminated during the incident, he presented two weeks afterwards—plenty of clothing changes and showers would have reasonably occurred during that period of time—so he most likely would not have required any gross decontamination. Ultimately, however, it is always safer to presume contamination until it can be ruled out with a radiation survey meter (i.e., Geiger counter).6
      3. If a patient does present to the ED grossly contaminated with radioactive materials, current guidelines state that universal precautions are effective against becoming contaminated, thus health care professionals are not in danger from interacting with this patient.6 Moreover, life-saving interventions should take precedence over decontamination procedure.6
      4. Specific decontamination procedures are outside the scope of this simulation debrief, but the copy of the REAC/TS Radiation Patient Treatment algorithm is a useful initial resource.
   2. Radiation Safety Officer (RFO)
      1. The hospital’s RFO, likely a health physicist, is an invaluable resource. While roles can vary by institution, the RFO should be consulted and may be able to assist with biodosimetry, assuring efficacy of decontamination procedures, and determining if the hospital’s disaster management policy should be implemented, such is in the event of a mass casualty incident.
3. **Case Background**
   1. The simulated patient in this case was unknowingly exposed to Cobalt-60, a gamma-emitting radioactive isotope, at his job at the scrap yard. While going through a pile of scrap after his lunch break, he discovered a piece of damaged equipment in a pile of other scrap and began tinkering with it, trying to figure out what it was. The damaged piece of equipment in question was a compromised industrial radiography camera housing a Co-60 source. He spent a total of 1 hour within 1 foot of the device, receiving a whole-body dose of 3.7 Gy. Of that 1 hour, he spent 15 minutes with his hands within 6 inches of the source, receiving a total dose of 6.4 Gy to that area.
4. **Case Development Notes for Scenario Director Reference**
   1. Calculation of dose
      1. For this scenario, we assumed the source of ionizing radiation to be Cobalt-60 because this is a common isotope used as a pigtail source in gamma ray projectors (cameras) in the field of industrial radiography.
      2. In this case, the patient found the device in the scrap yard and tinkered around with it for a while–this was important when considering the total time exposed and distance from the source. Whole body dose was calculated using a 1-hour exposure time at 1 foot from the source. The higher dose absorbed by the hands (because the patient was tinkering with the device only part of the hour with the hands naturally closer to the source than the rest of the body) was calculated by adding together the dose received in 15 minutes at 6 inches with the dose received in the other 45 minutes at 1 foot (i.e., ¾ the whole-body dose).
   2. Notes about the patient’s history/physical exam/lab findings
      1. Time to vomiting is an important for estimating dose. The average time to vomiting for a 3 Gy dose is 2.62 hours and 1.74 hours for a 4 Gy dose.6 Since the pt’s whole body dose was 3.7 Gray, we included the onset vomiting about 2 hours after exposure in the HPI.
      2. While the hematopoietic subsyndrome can occur at doses greater than or equal to 0.7-1 Gy, we ensured that the patient received a total dose greater than 2-3 Gy because this is the threshold for “clinically significant hematologic-related illness.”6
      3. The patient received a dose slightly over 6 Gy to the hands, which was higher than the dose absorbed by the skin over the rest of the body because the patient’s hands were closer to the source for part of the exposure. Because CRS has dose determinant effects, the hands exhibited more severe injury (erythema, pain, swelling) than the rest of the body (epilation) at the time of presentation. And just like ARS as a whole, CRS also presents by way of several phases (prodromal, latent, manifest Illness, and recovery). This provided the reasoning and justification for the current physical exam findings because the patient was in the manifest illness phase.
         1. Pancytopenia
            1. Considering the dose received and the time of presentation after exposure (2 weeks, which loosely coincides with the start of manifest illness phase), we had our patient exhibit a grade II (H2) hematological response to the acute radiation exposure. This is evidenced by the lab values for the patient–specifically the absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), absolute neutrophil count (ANC), and platelet count–which were based on established depletion kinetics.
