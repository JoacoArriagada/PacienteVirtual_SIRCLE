| **Brief narrative description of case** | Will Rogers is a 47-year-old male who presented to the Emergency Department three hours ago with hand pain and redness. He was unknowingly exposed to ionizing radiation two weeks prior to presentation.  Learners will be expected to perform a history and physical exam and order/interpret appropriate labs and imaging in order to recognize the exposure to ionizing radiation and make a diagnosis of Acute Radiation Syndrome. They will then be expected to consult with the proper services and initiate supportive care. |
| **Primary Learning Objectives** | By the end of this activity, learners will be able to:   1. Demonstrate the ability to effectively obtain a history and physical exam. 2. Recognize the signs and symptoms of the hematopoietic and cutaneous subsyndromes of Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS). 3. Provide appropriate supportive care to a patient presenting with the hematopoietic and cutaneous subsyndromes of ARS. 4. Identify appropriate subsyndrome-specific interventions for a patient presenting with the hematopoietic and cutaneous subsyndromes of ARS. |
| **Critical Actions** | 1. Obtain a pertinent patient history to include an HPI, ROS, PMH, social/occupational history, and medications/allergies. 2. Perform a pertinent physical exam to include assessment of the patient’s skin, cardiovascular function, lungs, and abdomen. 3. Interpret abnormal vital signs. 4. Consider sepsis. 5. Order appropriate laboratory tests to include complete blood count (CBC), basic metabolic panel (BMP), coagulation tests (PT, INR, PTT), serum lactic acid level, urinalysis, and blood cultures. 6. Recognize exposure to ionizing radiation and establish a diagnosis of ARS. 7. Consider the need for patient decontamination. 8. Provide appropriate supportive care to include fluids, broad spectrum antibiotics, and an antipyretic/analgesic. 9. Consult appropriate disciplines, such as toxicology, hematology, internal medicine, REAC/TS, and/or the hospital’s radiation safety officer. 10. Admit the patient. |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

In this case, a patient was unknowingly exposed to ionizing radiation, developed Acute Radiation Syndrome, and is presenting to the ED about two weeks following that exposure. The patient, Mr. Rogers, is a 47 y/o male who presents with a chief complaint of painful, swollen, red hands that initially appears to have occurred without obvious underlying cause.

We anticipate that the learners will first obtain vital signs, which will reveal that, in addition to the chief complaint, the patient is mildly hypotensive, borderline tachycardia, and has a fever. This will suggest that the patient may have an underlying infection when taking into account lab results that will be discussed shortly. The HPI will reveal that the patient had some more mild cutaneous symptomatology and vomiting a couple weeks ago after work, a clue towards thinking about occupational exposures. However, while obtaining the history has the potential of revealing the radiation exposure to the learners, we do not anticipate that their questioning will be thorough enough to entirely uncover this occupational exposure or that they will have the knowledge to connect ionizing radiation exposure to tinkering with a broken device at a scrap yard. The physical exam will reveal widespread epilation and a few ecchymoses in addition to the hands being erythematous, edematous, and painful. Labs will primarily reveal pancytopenia, which in combination with vital signs, suggests underlying infection. If ordered, x-rays do not reveal any significant findings. At this point, we anticipate that learners will administer fluids and antibiotics to stabilize the patient and prevent sepsis.

After vitals stabilize, we anticipate the rest of the simulation to be focused on critical thinking to discern the underlying diagnosis. We expect that learners will have difficulty in doing this even after calling consults, so, this is when we will employ the ESP to ask the patient about a picture he showed them earlier of the device he was tinkering with in the scrap yard the afternoon before his initial symptomatology two weeks prior to presentation. This device is an industrial radiography camera, and in the picture given to the learners, this device is clearly marked with the universal symbol of radiation (trefoil). We expect that this will be the moment that learners will determine that this patient is experiencing the effects of an acute radiation exposure and begin consulting with the pharmacy and hospitalist service. For admission and/or possible treatment recommendations, learners may also request a consultation with toxicology, hematology-oncology, or burn surgery, for example, and the scenario director will also voice those roles. Information about treatments is detailed in the Debriefing Materials (Appendix E) and will provide the scenario director with the appropriate information to guide those conversations if they are called to act as a consultant.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. Failure to perform a thorough history: We found that most groups of learners experienced difficulty in recognizing the exposure to ionizing radiation in part because they did not perform a thorough history and/or did not probe further into the information provided by the patient after initial questioning. We anticipated the inherent difficulty in establishing this rare diagnosis by incorporating into the case an image of an industrial radiography camera with the universal radiation warning symbol on it (see prompting instructions in the “Instructor Notes - Case and Branch Points” section above for more details).
2. Uncertainty about the proper service to consult: Many groups of learners displayed some doubt about which service(s) they should consult. We included specific information in the “Management of ARS” section of the Debriefing Materials to cover this.
