**Ideal Scenario Flow**

The Lead Fellow and Support Fellow enter the room, where the ICU nurse provides a brief patient history. The patient’s voice is hoarse and muffled and responds to the Lead Fellow’s questions with “Can’t. Breath.” The Lead Fellow acknowledges the patient distress and provides reassurance. The Lead Fellow asks for full vital signs and asks the Support Fellow to examine the patient with a focus on the airway examination. The Support Fellow reports severe lip and tongue swelling. The Lead Fellow voices concern for angioedema and possible anaphylaxis and questions the ICU intern about any recent medications. Medical treatment is ordered, which could include some combination of intramuscular epinephrine, antihistamines, and/or steroids. Nebulized and topical lidocaine, in addition to oxymetazoline are requested for topical preparation of the airway. The Lead Fellow ensures conscious sedations medications are readily available in preparation for possible awake intubation. In addition, the Lead Fellow voices concern for impending airway obstruction and need for immediate airway control while anticipating a difficult airway. Anesthesia and surgical airway experts are requested, and when unavailable, the Lead Fellow asks the Support Fellow to prepare for intubation, as the airway compromise will progress sooner than the bronchoscope can arrive. The plan for intubation is quickly but completely voiced with a backup plan including laryngeal mask airway and the ultimate possibility of surgical airway, and all equipment is brought to the bedside. Sedation and neuromuscular blockade are administered, and the Support Fellow is unable to intubate despite multiple modalities and the patient continues to deteriorate. During this time, the Lead Fellow coordinates equipment and personnel for a surgical airway. After 5 minutes and progression of hypoxemia to 80% the Lead Fellow calmly but deliberately announces the decision to proceed with a surgical airway, at which time a surgeon arrives to perform the procedure.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. Intubation: Manikins have varied capability to simulate upper airway obstruction. Some learners may force the endotracheal tube beyond a simulated obstruction, at which point the respiratory therapist can attach the bag valve mask and report inability to bag.
2. Angioedema: Manikins have varied capability to simulate angioedema of the oropharynx. One solution is to place red, enlarged artificial lips on the external lips of the manikin to make the pathology more obvious to the learners. These could be made from small pieces of paper or foam.
3. Uncertainty about doses of induction medications: Some learners may be uncomfortable with dosing of induction agents. One option is to have the intern ask if they should call the pharmacist for recommendations, leave the room, and return shortly thereafter with the appropriate dosing information.
4. Administration of general anesthetic or neuromuscular blockade: Given the high risk that a patient with angioedema may progress to a “Can’t intubate, can’t ventilate” situation, the administration of propofol or neuromuscular blockade, causes the scenario to progress to Act 3 in which the patient deteriorates. This may be one of the key points to review during the clinical debrief.
5. Failure to articulate a full plan for intubation: Learners may fail to articulate that the final back-up plan for intubation is a surgical airway. They may fail to clearly inform the team at what point (e.g. after how many intubation attempts or at what level of SpO2) they will transition to a surgical airway attempt. They may fail to ascertain who in the simulated team is willing to attempt surgical airway if the surgeons do not arrive in time.

**Debriefing Materials: Angioedema**

Causes

* Allergic (mast-cell mediated): often associated with allergen exposure and accompanied by other signs of allergic reaction (e.g., urticaria) and anaphylaxis (e.g., vomiting, bronchospasm, hypotension).
* Bradykinin-mediated: Not associated with urticaria or other symptoms of allergic reaction. Typically has a more prolonged time course. Relationship between trigger and onset of symptoms if often not apparent or temporally near. This is mechanism for **ACE-inhibitor induced angioedema,** which may develop within a week of starting or increasing the mediation, or after years or use. **Hereditary angioedema** (C1 inhibitor deficiency) also leads to increased bradykinin generation.
* Other less common causes: histamine-mediated, infection, calcium channel blockers, herbal medicines

Diagnostic Workup

* Medication and exposure history
* Labs: Consider CBC, metabolic panel, CRP, ESR, serum tryptase (if anaphylaxis suspected), and complement (C4) levels
* Consider laryngeal evaluation with bronchoscope, video laryngoscope, or flexible laryngoscope

Treatment

* Airway Management
  + Endotracheal intubation: awake approach preferred in upper airway obstruction
  + If concern for acute upper airway obstruction, avoid medications that contribute to respiratory depression or loss of airway tone
    - Ketamine & dexmedetomidine are ideal drugs
    - Neuromuscular blockade should only be considered if you have a good initial and back-up plan to deal with failure
* Medications
  + If suspected allergic angioedema, or unknown etiology, consider: epinephrine 0.3 mg IM, steroids, antihistamines
  + If hereditary angioedema is suspected, treatment options include the following: Icatibant (bradykinin receptor antagonist), ecallantaide (kallikrein inhibitor), FFP, and purified C1 inhibitor concentrate
