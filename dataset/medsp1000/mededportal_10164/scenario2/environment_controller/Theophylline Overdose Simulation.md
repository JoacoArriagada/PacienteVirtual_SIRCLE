**Abstract:** This scenario utilizes a standardized patient who presents with tachycardia, nausea, and vomiting. The patient believed that they had taken 10 tablets of prednisone at 8 hours and 1 hour before presentation (20 tablets total) but then discovered that they had accidently taken 20 theophylline tablets, not prednisone.

4. **Environment**
   1. **Simulation Environment Setup:** Simulation lab Emergency Department room, medication area, audio-visual control room, debrief room/area as defined in **Appendix A**
   2. **Manikin Setup:** A simulation manikin with moulage as described in **Appendix B** or a standardized patient with virtual vital signs can be used for this scenario. An endotracheal intubation manikin can also be set up.
   3. **Audiovisual Material:** Audio-visual feed from room with capture of standardized patient and vital signs monitor.
   4. **Props:** See **Appendix A** for additional setup details.
5. **Actors**
   1. **Roles & Role Activities:**
      1. Patient: Adult standardized patient with simulated vital signs preferred.
      2. Physician(s): One to three participant physicians.
      3. Nurse (confederate): At least one nurse is required for this scenario. The nurse will carry out orders and, when needed, redirect the learners. See **Appendix D**.
      4. Respiratory Therapist and/or Critical Care physician focused on airway (confederate): Initial role is to provide oxygen therapy if needed, but as the case progresses to possible intubation then the role of this confederate is to perform a simulated intubation.
6. **Case Narrative**
   1. **Scenario Background**
      1. Chief Complaint: A 55 year old patient presents to the ED with the complaint of 4 hours of severe nausea and vomiting accompanied by a new coarse tremor noted in the arms and legs bilaterally. Secondary complaints include palpitations, abdominal pain, and lightheadedness.
      2. History: A medical history of moderate to severe COPD controlled with a multi-drug regimen at home. The patient had some worsening of COPD over the last 48 hours and recognized this as a COPD exacerbation. The patient believed that they had taken 10 tablets of prednisone at 8 hours and 1 hour before presentation (20 tablets total). Shortness of breath and wheezing completely resolved but soon after the patient developed severe nausea, vomiting, and tremor.
      3. Extended History: The true story is that the patient has accidently taken 20 theophylline tablets, not prednisone.
      4. Medications: The patient has a medication list in the pocket and will identify the medication that they took the 20 tablets of as theophylline when asked by the medical learner. If the medication list is not discovered in a timely manner, the nurse may assist. See **Appendix E** for the complete list.
      5. Allergies: NKDA
      6. Family and Social History: 100 pack year history of smoking cigarettes, quit within the last 5 years. No alcohol or illicit drug use. No family history of cardiac disease.
   2. **Scenario Initial State**
      1. Patient Habitus and Presentation: Sitting up in bed actively vomiting when the medical learner first arrives. Active coarse tremor visualized. No tachypnea is seen and the patient is not wheezing.
      2. **Physiology:** T 37.2, P 130, BP 98/58. O2 saturation 93%. Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT) on monitor
      3. **Focused exam:**
         1. **General**: Reveals a patient in distress with signs of recent vomiting. Patient complaining severe nausea, abdominal pain, tremor, and palpitations.
            1. **Head, Ears, Eyes, Nose, Throat:** No evidence of trauma. Pupils are equal, round, and reactive with intact ocular movements. No discharge other than crusted vomitus at corners of mouth.
            2. **Skin:** Normal color and turgor, no diaphoresis or rash
            3. **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardia, pulse rate of 130. No murmurs, rubs or gallops.
            4. **Lungs:** No wheezes or crackles
            5. **Abdomen:** soft, nondistended, tenderness, nonspecific
            6. **Neurological:** Coarse tremor
   3. **Scenario Branch Points**
      1. **Scenario B:** The patient is experiencing the same symptoms with progression to a seizure about 5 minutes into the simulation.
         1. **Changes in Patient Condition:** The patient’s condition will deteriorate over the course of the scenario. After several minutes the patient will have a tonic-clonic seizure. The patient’s seizure will have little response to IV benzodiazepines but will resolve if IV propofol or phenobarbital is given. After the seizure, the patient’s O2 saturations will start to immediately drop if the medical learner has not initiated the process for elective intubation. The oxygen saturation will continue to drop until a simulated endotracheal intubation takes place.
      2. **Seizure:** Tonic-clonic seizure will subside only when given IV propofol or phenobarbital
      3. **Laboratory Studies:** Laboratory results are located in **Appendix J**
      4. **X-ray and EKG:** Results are located in **Appendix H and Appendix I**
      5. **Medications:**
         1. **IV Normal Saline Bolus**: If IV Normal Saline bolus is given, baseline blood pressure will improve slightly
         2. **Lorazepam 0.1mg/kg:** If given for seizure, the seizure will not respond to this medication
         3. **Phenobarbital 20mg/kg:** will break the patient’s seizure but will result in respiratory depression and require endotracheal intubation
         4. **Propofol 5mcg/kg/minute drip:** will break the patient’s seizure but will result in respiratory depression and require endotracheal intubation
         5. **Activated Charcoal 1g/kg (aqueous solution +/- sorbitol)**: For this scenario, activated charcoal will not improve the patient’s immediate condition, but is overall important to the long term stabilization of the patient
         6. **Ondansetron 8mg IV:** will stop the patient’s nausea and vomiting
         7. **Phenylephrine 100mcg:** Will result in an increase in blood pressure
         8. **Norepinephrine 8-12mcg/minute drip:** will result in an increase in blood pressure
      6. **Consultation of Specialist(s)**
         1. A Toxicologist should be consulted once the diagnosis of theophylline overdose is made
         2. A Nephrologist should be consulted for consideration of hemodialysis
         3. After sedation is given and intubation takes place, Critical Care Service should be consulted for a focused hand-off report and admission to the hospital.
7. **Instructor Notes**
   1. **Scenario Flow:** Instructors should provide the scenario background card from **Appendix F** to the learners just before the start of the scenario.
   2. **Virtual Vital Signs:** Instructors may directly control or vary the patient response on-the-fly.
   3. **Scenario Programming:** Variable vital signs may be directed by the instructor or planned for execution based on a trigger event. Triggers may include time, medications and seizure activity.

**Appendix D**

**Confederate Clinician Notes**

Issue: Learners fail to recognize deterioration as evidenced by vital signs on the monitor.

Possible Resolutions:

* Watchful waiting
* Take actions that would support vital signs such as stating, “I’m going to start some fluids”
* Re-cycle the blood pressure cuff
* Change the EKG lead that is displayed on the monitor

Issue: The standardized patient forgets to start having a seizure when appropriate.

* This issue should be addressed before the start of the scenario in pre-briefing with the standardized patient.
* We have found the best solution is to have a pre-determined signal in order to remind the SP:
  + One option is a discrete contact signal, such as having the confederate squeeze the toe of the SP as a reminder
  + A second option is a signal more obvious to all involved participants, such as having the confederate move to the bedside, quickly bring the patient’s head of the bed down, and state, “I think He/she is seizing”.

Issue: The learners are focused on patient symptoms and are not progressing towards identifying an underlying diagnosis.

Possible Resolutions:

* The confederate should indicate that the patient is vomiting.
* If the learners stall, the confederate might say, “I don’t think this patient is going to improve until we identify and treat the underlying cause.”

Issue: The learners are off-track and do not recognize this case as a possible toxicity/Theophylline overdose.

Possible Resolutions:

* The confederate can “find” a medication list among the patient’s things and point out the Theophylline
* The confederate might emphasize the relevant signs and symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and tremors or other symptoms or facts such as the underlying diagnosis of COPD.
