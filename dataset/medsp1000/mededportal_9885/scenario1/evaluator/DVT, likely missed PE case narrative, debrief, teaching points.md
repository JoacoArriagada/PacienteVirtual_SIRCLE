# Teaching/discussion points

The deep venous system of the lower extremities:

in the calf include the anterior tibial vein, the posterior tibial vein, and peroneal veins   
in the thigh, include the superficial femoral vein, the deep femoral vein, and join to form the common femoral vein, becoming the external iliac vein at the inguinal ligament

Pain or discomfort is common, but swelling is variable, especially early in clot formation. Homan’s sign is unreliable. Massive deep vein thrombosis (DVT) can result in arterial spasm, appearing as a pale, swollen leg (cerulea alba dolens). Blue leg (phlegmasia cerulea dolens) is the results of massive ileofemoral DVT with extensive vascular congestion and venous ischemia, producing a painful, cyanotic leg. These conditions require more aggressive interventions than uncomplicated deep vein thrombosis.

Virchow’s triad (endothelial injury, stasis, hypercoagulability) globally describes the risk of DVT, and calculating a Wells score can help with risk assessment for DVT:

At the time of the first visit, this patient only complained of fleeting episodes of chest pain and feeling that it was anxiety-related. She did not have dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, hypoxia, or alterations in heart rate, respiratory rate, or hypoxia. Prior to the complaint of unilateral leg pain, her Wells score would have been zero, and with the pre-test probability being judged so low, PE was probably not on the differential diagnosis to any significant degree.

When she developed the unilateral leg pain, and had some suspicion of DVT, her score would be 3, putting her at moderate risk. The negative ultrasound probably eliminated PE from further consideration at that time, though it is important to recognize that only $40 \%$ of ambulatory emergency department patient with PE have a demonstrated DVT. The Revised Geneva Score for Pulmonary Embolism and the Pulmonary Embolism Rule Out Criteria (PERC Rule) were developed and published as objective tools to guide decision making. The Revised Geneva score would be 6 (3 points each for unilateral leg pain and heart rate between 75-94 beats per minute), placing her in the moderate probability group for PE. The PERC Rule was developed to eliminate ordering a diagnostic test in low risk patients, but this patient had two features that would suggest some further testing (age over 50 years and estrogen use). D-dimer testing may have been a reasonable choice at that time instead of chest imaging, though rheumatoid arthritis may give a false positive level. Ultimately, a combination of clinical judgment, pre-test probabilities, and sensible testing strategies are needed for

evaluating patients for DVT/PE.

As discussed above, there is clear justification for repeat ultrasound imaging of the left leg. The finding of the DVT in this patient should prompt treatment. Though she did not have any chest symptoms at the time of her second presentation, she had potential symptoms (chest pain) of a pulmonary embolism at the first presentation, and had no imaging beyond a chest x-ray. Upon hospital admission, the patient did undergo VQ scan imaging that suggested a small pulmonary embolism, but the delayed finding of the VQ mismatch did not change the management decisions. It could be argued that this patient did not require the imaging of the chest on the second visit, as she has a known DVT, and more peripheral emboli may be missed with imaging, and the diagnosis of PE could have been made presumptively.

What if the patient did not previously develop chest pain and leg pain? If she had no other symptoms other than isolated calf pain, should she have been treated with anticoagulation? There are conflicting opinions. There is evidence to suggest that isolated, asymptomatic DVT may be managed without anticoagulation, but observed with repeat ultrasound evaluations. Some subsets of patients (those with cancer, having undergone orthopedic procedures, and immobile patients) may require anticoagulation, as they appear to be at higher risk for propagation.

Anticoagulation is most often the treatment for DVT/PE, and the recommendations for anticoagulation are similar for stable patients with PE as for most isolated DVT. The choice for anticoagulation may include unfractionated heparin, low molecular weight heparin, and oral anticoagulants. Until recently, warfarin was the only available oral anticoagulant. It often takes several days to achieve a therapeutic INR, so bridging with either unfractionated heparin or subcutaneous low molecular weight heparin was necessary. It is inexpensive and still widely used to treat DVT/PE, but does have well known problems, including bleeding risk, monitoring needs, drug interactions, and dietary limitations. Newer oral anticoagulants such as Rivaroxaban are more expensive, but seem to be as effective, have lower bleeding risks, and lack the inconvenient monitoring and dietary restrictions. If bleeding problems were to develop, Rivaroxaban may be more difficult to reverse.

The choice of which anticoagulation strategy to use depends on several factors, such as patient preferences, cost of medications, and available follow up. If such factors are not prohibitive, it would not be unreasonable to begin treatment from the emergency department and discharge the patient home with outpatient primary care follow up. Patients with less well established resources or difficulties should be anti-coagulated and admitted. Patients who have contraindications for anticoagulation may be treated with a caval interruption device.
