# LGBT Health

#### Learning Objectives

1. Describe some of the unique health needs and challenges of LGBT individuals.
2. Develop strategies to increase student comfort level with asking patients about LGBT identity, including questions of pronouns, sexual practices, and health risks.

You are seeing a patient in an outpatient clinic while on your primary care rotation. Mary (pronouns: she, her, hers) is a 16-year-old female presenting with abdominal pain for the past several months. She denies any recent trauma. She does not report any past medical or surgical history, medications, or allergies. While interviewing Mary, you notice that she seems apathetic and emotionless as she speaks, and she rarely makes eye contact. Her answers to specific questions about her symptoms are vague and inconsistent. Her physical exam is normal. Mary has only had one prior encounter at this clinic for STI testing 8 months ago, where she reported being sexually active with male and female partners. Before ordering an extensive workup of Mary’s abdominal pain, it is important to find out more about the circumstances in Mary’s life during which she developed the pain.

Questioning and screening guidance retained from the source:

* How are things at home or school? Do you feel safe in your neighborhood and at school?
* Has anyone ever picked on you? Can you tell me about it? Was this because you are LGBT?
* At any time, has anyone hit, kicked, choked, threatened, forced him or herself on you sexually, touched you in a sexual way that was unwanted, or otherwise hurt or frightened you?

Teaching points tied to Mary:

* Mary is a sexual minority youth, which means statistically, she is more vulnerable to homelessness, bullying, and physical or sexual violence, and substance abuse, all of which can profoundly affect her health and access to healthcare.
* Mary’s behavior while being interviewed is an important signal for screening.

If asked who she lives with:

She says that she has been on her own for a while. She ran away from home about 6 months ago when her mother found out about her girlfriend and she has been living at various friends’ houses. She stopped going to school because she was being bullied by some of her classmates daily and she was failing several classes. She reports daily alcohol and marijuana use, and denies other illicit drug use.

Sexual-history guidance retained from the source:

* Begin the sexual history by reassuring the patient that it is routine and confidential.
* Ask open ended non-judgmental questions.
* Do not make assumptions or allow personal prejudice.
* Be specific and use language mirroring the patient’s language.
* Example questions:
  1. Tell me a little about your sexuality.
  2. Have you been sexually involved with anyone during the past year, including oral, vaginal, or anal sex, or other kinds of sexual practices?
  3. What genders have your sexual partners been in the past?
  4. What genders are your current sexual partners?
  5. How many sexual partners have you had in the last year?
  6. Do you have any sexual concerns or questions you’d like to discuss?
  7. Do you have any concerns or questions about your sexuality, sexual identity, or sexual desires?

Responses if these questions are asked:

Mary states that she does not have a specific sexual identity. Currently, she is sexually active with one female partner. She has been sexually active with both male and female partners in the past involving oral, vaginal, and anal sexual contact.

Mental-health screening prompt retained from the source:

* Who do you turn to when you feel sad or need someone to talk to?

Responses if mood is explored:

Mary reports feeling down and anxious ever since leaving home. She doesn’t really have anyone to talk to about her problems because she does not want to be judged. She has started cutting her wrists since leaving home, which takes her mind off other problems in her life. She denies thoughts of suicide and thoughts of hurting others.

Evaluator-relevant teaching points:

* LGBT individuals are almost three times more likely than others to experience a mental health condition.
* LGBTQ teens are six times more likely to experience symptoms of depression than the general population.
* LGBTQ youth are more likely to attempt suicide, experience suicidal thoughts, or engage in self-harm.
* Underlying mental health problems can manifest as physical problems; the source points to Mary’s abdominal pain and emotional flatness as an example.
