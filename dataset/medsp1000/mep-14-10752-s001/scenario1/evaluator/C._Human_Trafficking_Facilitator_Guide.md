# Human Trafficking

#### Learning Objectives

1. Identify common “red flags” which might identify human trafficking victims in a clinical setting.
2. Learn pitfalls that clinicians often face when working with victims of human trafficking and other violent situations.
3. Understand what medical professionals can do to help a patient who is or is not ready to leave their trafficker.

You are an attending physician working in the ER. An 18-year-old Asian female arrives with the chief complaint of “rash on leg.” When you meet her, she is thin and appears pale and uncomfortable. She is accompanied by her boyfriend who seems very protective of her. You ask her why she is here, and the patient looks at her boyfriend quickly, then stares at the floor without answering your question. Her boyfriend explains that she speaks very little English and can’t understand you well. He immediately states that he is willing to serve as the interpreter. He says that she is afraid of doctors so she will be more comfortable if he stays. You notice that while the boyfriend is talking, the patient shifts her gaze between the boyfriend and the floor but never looks anywhere else.

Decision Point 1: You wonder if you should accept the boyfriend’s offer to translate.

* Accept the boyfriend’s offer to translate.
* Wait for the Thai translator to help with the history and exam.

If the examinee accepts the boyfriend’s offer to translate:

You ask the patient what brought her to the ER and wait for her boyfriend to translate. Instead of relaying your question to the patient, he immediately responds that she is here to get medication for a rash that developed after she touched what was probably poison ivy. He also states that they are in a hurry since they have a flight later in the day to visit Thailand, so they would like to get the medicine immediately.

You ask the patient to lift her pant leg to examine the rash and realize that this looks like a chronic, progressive skin infection that has been neglected for a while.

Emphasize that this was the incorrect answer choice.

Why using a translator is a good idea:

* The average layperson family member is less likely to know specific medical terminology in both languages.
* Compared to a professional interpreter, they cannot be objective and could potentially have an ulterior motive in concealing parts of the patient’s history.

When the phone Thai translator is used and the boyfriend is out of the room:

The patient is a poor historian and is vague and inconsistent with the details of how she got the rash and how long ago it appeared. She is robotic and emotionless in her responses to questions.

Physical exam findings:

* bruise around left eye
* faded scars along forearms and neck
* significant redness and pus in the lower thigh to groin area
* poor quality tattoo on the right breast of a crown with the name “Jason”
* pain in the left abdomen when light pressure is applied

Social history and interpretation:

* She does not smoke but drinks socially.
* She pauses when asked whether or not she uses illegal drugs but ultimately says no.
* She immigrated to the United States from Thailand two years ago and has been working with her boyfriend’s family’s massage parlor since she arrived.
* She hesitantly mentions that she is required to socialize with clients after hours to help with the business.
* The scars and bruise are indicative of violent situations.
* Idiopathic abdominal pain is a common symptom in patients who have experienced rape or other sexual trauma.
* Advanced dermatological findings can indicate severe neglect and unhygienic living conditions.
* Tattoos can also be indicative, especially if they bear male names or symbols of currency.
* The flat affect may indicate underlying psychiatric conditions.
* The social history is suspicious in a few ways.
* The fact that she is required to interact with clients after hours is a significant red flag for sex trafficking.
* Certain businesses, such as massage parlors, nail salons, and cantinas, are often implicated in human trafficking.
* Trafficking victims are not often forthcoming with personal information if their trafficker is present during the examination.

Human trafficking teaching points:

* Human trafficking is defined as “the recruitment, transfer, harboring, or receipt of person, by means of the threat or use of force or other forms of coercion…for the purpose of exploitation.”
* There are two main types of human trafficking: sex trafficking and labor trafficking.
* Many trafficking victims come into contact with healthcare providers, but less than 20% reported that their healthcare providers were aware of their status.
* Populations most vulnerable to trafficking include homeless individuals, runaways, victims of abuse, and those with learning or physical disabilities.

Decision Point 2:

* Warn the patient that in the United States, prostitution is illegal and she should get out of her situation immediately and report her trafficker.
* Initiate a basic mental health screening and ask specific screening questions for human trafficking.

If the examinee warns the patient:

* This is not the best option.
* Victims of human trafficking often feel powerless to leave their situation.
* Traffickers use physical and mental abuse, threats of harm, forced substance addiction, and threats of deportation.
* It is more helpful to focus on building patient rapport so that you can gradually encourage them to seek help in the future.

Screening questions the source says may be helpful:

1. Can you leave your job or situation if you want?
2. Can you come and go at home or work as you please?
3. Have you been threatened if you try to leave?
4. Have you been physically harmed in any way?
5. What are your working or living conditions like?
6. Where do you sleep and eat?
7. Do you sleep on a bed, on a cot, or on the floor?
8. Have you ever been deprived of food, water, sleep, or medical care?
9. Do you have to ask permission to eat, sleep, or go to the bathroom?
10. Are there locks on your doors and windows so you cannot get out?
11. Has anyone threatened your family?
12. Has your identification or documentation been taken from you?
13. Is anyone forcing you to do anything that you do not want to do?

If the examinee asks the screening questions:

* She reluctantly answers yes when asked if she has ever felt threatened by violence in her everyday surroundings.
* She admits that she is sometimes forced by her boyfriend and his family to do things that she does not want to do.
* If asked whether she has ever traded sex for protection, food, or money, she does not answer and stares at the floor.

If the examinee asks whether she has considered trying to leave:

She says that she is scared of trying to find a way out of her current state because she does not know the English language or anything about the city, does not have any money of her own, does not have a support system, and is afraid of what would happen to her if she tried to leave. She says she would just like to be treated for her infection.

Disposition and rationale:

* Human trafficking victims may not be ready to leave their situation, even when they are offered help.
* Healthcare providers should not aggressively force victims to leave or testify against their trafficker.
* For patients who are interested in leaving, providers can offer to call the National Human Trafficking Hotline.
* If the patient is not yet ready, the provider may give the patient this information for when they are ready.
* If the patient feels comfortable seeking help, child or adult protective services may be enlisted.
* If a patient is in imminent danger, it may be necessary to contact law enforcement.
