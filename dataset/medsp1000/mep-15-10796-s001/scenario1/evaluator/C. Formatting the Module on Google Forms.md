# Cancer Screening for Transgender Patients

# Case 1

Ms R is a 50 year old transgender woman with T2DM, obesity, who presents to your clinic for routine follow-up.

1. What additional questions would you ask Ms R to help guide your counseling about cancer screening?

# Case 1 - Asking the Questions

ANSWER: Rule number one for cancer screening in transgender people: If an individual has a particular body part or organ and otherwise meets criteria for screening based on risk factors or symptoms, screening should proceed regardless of hormone use. Let's break that down--you're going to need to ask:

- Is it okay for me to ask some questions about your medical history so I can provide you with accurate recommendations for cancer screening? Prior (or current) therapies may be gender affirming, but transgender patients can experience significant distress talking about organs they have that are dissonant with their gender identity. Limit questions to those that are relevant to the current visit or problem.

- Are you on cross hormone therapy? To which Ms R responds that she's been on high dose estrogen for 5 years as well as finasteride. Length of hormone exposure impacts breast cancer screening (more on this later).

- Did you receive gender-affirming surgery? Ms R has not undergone a vaginoplasty, but had breast construction surgery 2 years ago.

- What are your goals for cancer screening? Patients may have done their own research on cancer screening or heard from friends/family about screening. With research somewhat limited on this subject, eliciting patient preference is key to having a shared decision making conversation.

Do you have any family members with cancer? To which Ms R responds, "No."

- Ask about current sexual practices: this may clue you into whether anal HPV testing is indicated.
- Chart review: HIV Status? Transgender patients are at increased risk of HIV infection. HIV in turn imparts a 3-fold risk of cervical cancer, a 2 fold risk of lung cancer, and a 3 fold risk of liver cancer.

2. What screening would you offer Ms R?

# Case 1 - Screening Guidelines

ANSWER: Colonoscopy +/- Mammography and PSA.

EXPLANATION: Ms R is due for a colonoscopy regardless of her gender or birth sex. She does not have a cervix, so pap is not indicated. Mammography and PSA in transgender women is more complicated:

- Mammography: Transgender women have a lower risk of breast cancer than non-transgender women. A Dutch study found an estimated breast CA incidence of 4.1/100000 person-years in transgender women compared to 155/100000 person years in cisgendered women. Furthermore, the risk of breast CA in transgender women receiving <5 years of hormone therapy is exceedingly low and expert opinion suggests breast screening should not commence in transgender women until at least 5 years of estrogen therapy has been completed. When would you start if she's had significant hormone exposure? Expert opinion suggests mammography every 2 years after age 50. One other thing should be considered: 50% of transgender women are classified as having dense breast tissue, increasing the risk of false negative results.

- PSA: A few considerations: 1) her use of 5a-reductase inhibitor may lower the PSA value, 2) her estrogen exposure likely reduces risk of prostate CA (as would orchiectomy had she had that procedure performed). For these reasons, if she chooses to screen for prostate CA, a ULN cutoff for the PSA could be pulled down to 1.0 ng/mL, though the evidence level for this is expert opinion.

3. Ms R agrees to colonoscopy and mammography, but declines PSA. She expresses some concern about mammography, noting that she occasionally experiences some breast tenderness at baseline. How do you respond?

# Case 1 - Mammography

ANSWER: C.

Breaking it down:

A) While there is a study that shows significantly less pain experienced with mammography compared to expected pain among transgender women, "I wouldn't worry about it" incompletely addresses concerns about baseline breast tenderness.

B) From the same study, only 2% of transgender women say they wouldn't return for repeat mammography because of discomfort following their first test.

C) Breast tenderness, nodularity, and pain can be experienced by women for up to 5 years following initiation of hormone therapy. Mammography is unlikely to cause sustained pain on top of this.

D) Clinical breast exam is inferior to mammography for breast cancer screening in transgender women.
