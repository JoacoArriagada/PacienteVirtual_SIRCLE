# Cancer Screening for Transgender Patients

# Case 2

Mr P is a 42 Y transgender male who underwent prior bilateral mastectomy and has been on testosterone therapy for over 10 years. He has not undergone TAH/BSO. He has not received any pap smears in the past 10 years. His family history is negative for cancer. He is interested in hearing what cancer screening he's due for.

4. To better understand Mr P's risk of HPV-related cancers, how would you ask him about his sexual history?

# Case 2 - Asking the questions

ANSWER: The Fenway institute uses the following question in their EHR (note it's important to show that multiple boxes can be checked: "Do you think of yourself as..."

[ ] Lesbian, Gay, or homosexual
[ ] Straight or heterosexual
[ ] Bisexual
[ ] Something else
[ ] Don't know

This provides a non-judgmental entry into asking more specific questions (you can preface this by checking in to make sure you and the patient agree on the terminology for genitalia and other sexual terms):

- What are your preferences when having sex with your partner? Mr P responds that he has receptive anal and vaginal sex with his male partner.

- Ask about other practices (both insertive and receptive--don't make assumptions based on their gender).

- Don't pass up the opportunity to ask about protection! Mr P says his partner uses condoms 100% of the time.

5. What cancer screening will you offer to Mr P?

# Case 2 - Screening

ANSWER: Pap smear for cervical cancer screening +/- anal pap for anal cancer screening

EXPLANATION:

- TVUS: there is a theoretical risk of increased unopposed estrogen in transgender men (excess exogenous T converts to estrogen ^+ patient is anovulatory), but there is no evidence to suggest transgender men are at increased risk for endometrial cancer and routine screening is not recommended.

- Mammo: the risk of breast ca in trans men arising from residual breast tissue following bilateral mastectomy is unknown. There is no evidence to support routine mammography in this population.
- Colo: there is no indication for transgender men to initiate screening colonoscopies earlier than nontransgender men (=50).

- HPV-related cancers: Transgender men who have sex with non-transgender men (trans MSM) have been noted to report inconsistent condom use during receptive oral, vaginal and anal sex, and are at increased risk for high risk HPV infection. He should be offered a cervical pap smear. You should have a shared decision making conversation about anal paps with Mr P.

6. True or False: Cervical Cancer screening for transgender men follows the same initiation, interval, and cessation schedule as non-transgender women.

7. True or False: Transgender men have a 10-fold lower risk of unsatisfactory path results from their pap compared to non-transgender women.

8. True or False: a painful pap experience is correlated with non-adherence to future screening and colposcopy in transgender men.

9. True or False: Vaginal estrogens can be used one week prior to exam to improve transgender men's experience with paps.

# Case 2 - Pap Smears

ANSWERS:

1) TRUE - start at 21, every 3 years until 30, then either q3 or 5yrs depending on whether HPV cotesting is sent, and stop at 65.
2) FALSE - transgender men have a 10-fold HIGHER risk of unsatisfactory path, likely related to testosterone mediated atrophic cervicovaginitis.
3) TRUE - there are many resources available to help providers limit pain and discomfort related to paps. The patient-created content is some of the best:
http://checkitoutguys.ca/sites/default/files/Tips_Paps_TransMen_0.pdf
4) TRUE - much of the discomfort of the exam stems from testosterone-mediated vaginal atrophy. This can be abated somewhat by a short course of vaginal estrogens.

Mr P agrees to a pap smear today, which you complete without difficulty. High risk HPV co-testing is negative, and the pap shows no atypical cells.
