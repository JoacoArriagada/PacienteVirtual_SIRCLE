| **Brief narrative description of case** | This simulation is part of a core curriculum and is designed for anesthesia residents and trainees to experience the demanding parent and the hurried surgeon for a patient who is not NPO, requires an emergent surgery for a post-tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy hemorrhage, and is a difficult intravenous access. This simulation should function as an instrument for educating, evaluating, and reinforcing material learned through self-study, didactics, and on the job experience. This simulation-based experience allows for training that (1) reduces patient risk by giving trainees a chance to experience this perioperative situation in a high-fidelity simulated environment before treating an actual patient, (2) is observable and repeatable and, as such, allows for ongoing evaluation of learner progression, (3) allows for the freedom to make clinical management errors and reflect upon different actions in the future. This scenario is used to gain experience in the management of this clinical condition, as well as for evaluation, and self-identification of weaknesses in the learner’s knowledge base as it relates to the perioperative management of early post-tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy hemorrhage. This simulation is not designed to be a summative assessment tool but, rather, a source for enhancement in knowledge and performance in this clinical situation.  By the end of this simulation, the learner should be able to:   1. Discuss the diagnosis and management of post-tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy hemorrhage in the pediatric population   (Anesthesiology Milestones: Medical Knowledge 1, Patient Care 6)   1. Discuss and manage comorbid conditions and their perioperative implications   (Anesthesiology Milestones: Practice-based Learning and Improvement 4, Interpersonal and communication skills)   1. Discuss preparation for emergent surgery in a non-NPO bleeding pediatric patient without an IV who is a difficult IV placement   (Anesthesiology Milestones: Patient Care 1, Patient Care 2)   1. Discuss and demonstrate performing a rapid sequence induction along with a working knowledge of the ASA difficult airway algorithm   (Anesthesiology Milestones: Patient Care 5, Patient Care 8)   1. Discuss areas of strength and areas for improvement discovered during the simulation   (Anesthesiology Milestones: Professionalism 4) |
| **Primary Learning Objectives** | 1. Discuss the diagnosis and perioperative management of post-tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy hemorrhage    1. | **Critical Actions** | The learner should take the following steps in caring for the simulated patient:  - Request vitals/labs/studies/NPO status/past medical history /previous anesthetic history including post-operative care unit information  - Prior to induction, The learner should attempt conventional routes of IV access  - When conventional routes are not successful, the learner should  - Further postpone induction until IV/IO access is achieved  - Identify further strategies for IV placement including ultra-sound guidance of peripheral line or central line placement, intraosseous access, or lastly a venous cutdown  - Verbalize use of pharmacologic assistance such as topical anesthetics (ex. EMLA cream), subcutaneously injected local anesthetics and/or with careful parenteral premedication (i.e. nasal dexmedetomidine or rectal midazolam) so that the patient is able to protect their airway and tolerate IV/IO placement.  - Begin intravascular volume replacement immediately once access is established prior to induction  - Request that blood products to be available in the operating room (initially O+ for this male patient and O- if it were a female, until a type and screen is complete and then type specific uncrossmatched blood once a T&S is complete) – Of note, based on each individual institutional policies and practices, it is not always standard to obtain a pre-op T&S on elective outpatient T&A. Type specific uncrossmatched blood should be considered first unless the patient is persistently unstable because of the risk/benefit profile  - Request that the ENT surgeon be readily available for possible emergency surgical airway and communicate the overall plan to the ENT doctor  - Select for induction, propofol, ketamine, or etomidate as acceptable choices at appropriate doses to minimize hypotension along with succinylcholine ideally (+/- atropine) or rocuronium  - Have airway equipment available in case of difficulty (i.e. large Yankauer suctions x2, Miller and/or Macintosh blades, CMAC, Glidescope, Bougie, LMAs, etc…) and demonstrate a working knowledge of the ASA difficult airway algorithm  - Treat post-induction hypotension, which includes:  - *NS or LR fluid administration*  - *pressors prn (phenylephrine, ephedrine, etc…)*  - *head down positioning*  - *Depth of anesthesia being adjusted to not exceed 1 MAC of volatile agent*  - Suggest placing an additional IV +/- placing an arterial line in order to obtain blood for labs (at a minimum critical care Hgb and/or Hct) and for typing and screening the patient. An arterial line should be considered if frequent lab draws may potentially be required, if hemostasis proves difficult for the surgeons, and/or if hemodynamic instability is deemed to need more aggressive beat to beat monitoring than a non-invasive BP cuff can yield.  - Prior to electrocautery, the learner should decrease the inspired oxygen concentration in order to minimize potential for airway fire (decrease FiO2 to < 30%)  - Place a gastric suction catheter and empty the stomach contents prior to extubation  - Extubation should take place once the patient is fully awake for optimal airway protection |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

*The anesthesia provider is emergently called to the second stage recovery area to evaluate Henry, a 10 year old male, 73 kg, Height 140 cm, and a BMI of 37 who is noted to have a tonsillar bleed with nausea and hematemesis. He is 6 hours status post adenotonsillectomy performed for treatment of suspected obstructive sleep apnea. Nursing reports that his discharge to home was imminent, and that his IV had recently been removed. The Otolaryngologists have requested to return to the OR immediately for hemostasis with electrocautery. He did not have any preoperative testing such as a polysomnogram, imaging, or other tests apart from a CBC, which showed* WBC 5.2; Hgb 14.9; Hct 45; plt 285*. He takes no prescription medications. The anesthesia record from earlier notes general anesthesia was induced via mask induction with nitrous oxide and sevoflurane. The record indicates that the patient was easy to mask ventilate, and that direct laryngoscopy using a Macintosh 3 blade resulted in a Cormack-Lehane grade 2a view. A 6.0 mm Oral cuffed RAE endotracheal tube was placed easily on the first attempt. Also noted in the patient’s chart is the phrase “difficult IV” but no further details. He received IV morphine intra-op and in the recovery room totaling 4.5 mg along with ketorolac 30 mg IV and oral Tylenol in the recovery area for pain. The patient consumed 2 popsicles, ice-chips, and 12oz of apple juice within the last 2 hours. He is anxious and crying, and his mother requests that he go to sleep by mask induction with IV placement after he is asleep “like he was earlier today, because he is deathly afraid of needles.” On transport to the OR he has an episode of hematemesis of ~ 150 ml.*

*The learner’s anesthesia plan is requested. The learner should plan to attempt vascular access prior to induction with rapid sequence in this patient who is a second general anesthetic with recent intubation, blood in the airway, likely swollen/edematous airway from his recent surgery, nausea/hematemesis, and not NPO. Prior to inducing general anesthesia via RSI or inhalational, the learner’s attempt to gain vascular access via conventional routes will be unsuccessful with now an even more agitated and crying patient with continued oropharyngeal bleeding. The learner will need to explore other routes to gain intravenous access in the presence of a hurried surgeon who is rushing the anesthesiologist and requesting a mask induction to “speed things up.” These other unconventional routes include: ultra-sound guidance of peripheral line or central line placement, intraosseous access, or a venous cutdown. This should be successful only after the learner verbalizes use of medication assistance such as topical anesthetics (ex. EMLA cream), subcutaneously injected local anesthetics and/or with careful parenteral premedication (nasal dexmedetomidine or rectal midazolam).*

*The learner should then begin intravascular volume replacement therapy with IV/IO NS or LR and ask for blood products to be in the room. The learner may request that the ENT surgeon be readily available for possible emergency surgical airway in the case of a difficult airway. The learner should have a large Yankauer suction available and functioning prior to anesthetic induction. Learner should then proceed with careful induction by rapid sequence followed by intubation by method of choice (Macintosh blade, Miller blade, Glidescope, CMAC, Bougie, etc…), with emergency airway management tools available to demonstrate a working knowledge of the ASA difficult airway algorithm (the facilitator should elect to have the trainee use a bougie to place the ETT over).*

*Mild to moderate hypotension will occur shortly after induction during the early maintenance portion of the procedure. This should be properly addressed by NS or LR fluid administration, pressors prn, head down positioning, and proper depth of anesthesia being adjusted to not exceed 1 MAC of volatile agent (assuming patient does not move with incision). The learner at this point may suggest placing an additional IV +/- placing an arterial line in order to obtain blood for labs (at a minimum critical care Hgb and/or Hct) and for typing and screening the patient. An arterial line should be considered if frequent lab draws may potentially be required, if hemostasis proves difficult for the surgeons, and/or if hemodynamic instability is deemed to need more aggressive beat to beat monitoring than a non-invasive BP cuff can yield. Prior to electrocautery, the learner should decrease the inspired oxygen concentration in order to minimize potential for airway fire.*

*The maintenance portion of the procedure should then proceed uneventfully with good hemostasis obtained by the surgeon and stable vitals with the use of pressors and fluid boluses as needed. The critical care labs that were sent intra-operatively will return as a Hgb of 11.3 and a Hct of 34.*

*Prior to emergence and extubation the learner should suction out the gastric contents (which would include liquids drunk by the patient and blood swallowed). The learner should extubate this patient only once fully awake when all extubation criteria have been met to assure the best possibility that the patient will be able to protect their own airway.*

*Should the learner at any point elect to perform an inhalational induction (not the ideal scenario flow), an alternate pathway (Path B) in the simulation would be taken. In this scenario, soon after the patient loses consciousness with mask inhalation of volatile anesthetic, hematemesis and subsequent aspiration will occur during the second stage of deepening, which will lead to a simulated bronchospasm and laryngospasm and the inability of the learner to mask ventilate the patient. The learner may at this point give IM succinylcholine to treat the laryngospasm but upon laryngoscopy will find a swollen and bloody airway making intubation unsuccessful. The patient will quickly become hypoxic followed by bradycardic and hypotensive which will progressively worsen and lead to ventricular tachycardia with progression to ventricular fibrillation and asystole. The learner should initiate a surgical airway and commence ACLS/PALS protocol. Attempts by the learner to resuscitate the patient will result in return to spontaneous circulation.*

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. *Failure to identify NPO status: Some junior learners may not recognize the importance of a rapid sequence induction in order to minimize the risk of pulmonary complications in a patient with a full stomach. It is important to recognize that even if this child had not consumed apple juice and popsicles in the recovery room, he would still be considered and treated as a full stomach in the setting of airway bleeding with hematemesis.*
2. *Failure to perform a rapid sequence induction secondary to external influences: Some learners may be lured into an inhalation induction because of a demanding parent, a hurried surgeon, or an agitated pediatric patient.*
3. *Failure to obtain IV/IO access prior to induction: Some trainees may not know to suggest the unconventional routes of obtaining intravascular access, including: ultrasound guidance to assist with peripheral or central IV access, intraosseous access, or a venous cutdown in order to properly perform a rapid sequence induction.*
4. *Failure to troubleshoot a difficult airway/laryngoscopy: Some junior learners may not request to have readily available, additional airway equipment such as a Glidescope, CMAC, Bougie, LMA, multiple laryngoscope blades, and other devices in case difficulties arise.*
5. *Failure to adjust FiO2 levels prior to airway electrocautery use: Some junior learners may not have recognized the importance of lowering the FiO2 prior to electrocautery use to minimize the potential for an airway fire.*
6. *Failure to decompress the stomach prior to extubation: Some junior learners may not have identified the need for gastric suctioning in a patient with a full stomach, prior to emergence to minimize the risk of aspiration and pulmonary complications.*
