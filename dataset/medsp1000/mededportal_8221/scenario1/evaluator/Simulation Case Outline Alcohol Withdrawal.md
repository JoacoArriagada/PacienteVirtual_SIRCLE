**Simulation Case Outline**

1. **Title** *Alcohol Withdrawal*
1. **Learning Objectives**
   1. **Primary**
      1. *Recognition of Alcohol Withdrawal Syndrome*
      2. *Management of Alcohol Withdrawal Syndrome*
   2. **Secondary**
      1. *Importance of thorough history including social history.*
      2. *Obtaining additional information from other sources (i.e. family, EMS, old records) when available.*
   3. **Critical Actions List**
      1. *Obtain social history of daily alcohol use until recently quit.*
      2. *Correct electrolyte abnormalities, give MVI and thiamine.*
      3. *Appropriate airway protection and management.*
      4. *Treatment with appropriate doses of benzodiazepines.*
      5. *Admission to Intensive Care Unit.*
   4. **Secondary Actions List**
      1. *If intubated, maintain sedation with propofol or benzodiazepine drip.*
   5. **Duration of Events**

*Case duration is approximately 25-30 minutes depending on appropriate care and disposition.*

1. **Environment & Equipment**
   1. **Setting**

*Night (11:30p) shift at a busy community ED. Consultants available by phone.*

* 1. **Manikin**

*High fidelity simulator.*

* 1. **Props**

*Monitor, suction equipment, BVM, intubation equipment, crash cart, mock medications / drips.*

1. **Roles**

*Patient, patient’s wife, nurse, patient liaison, ICU physician, hospitalist physician.*

1. **Case Narrative**
   1. **Pt. bio:** *65y/o male (5’11” 180lbs) presents by private vehicle. Brought by wife.*
   2. **Complaint / HPI:** *Patient complaining of not feeling well today. No pain. “I just feel bad.” Denies prior episodes. +palpitations, +diaphoresis, no CP, mild SOB, no abdominal pain, +”shaky”, +”nervous”, +nauseous after dinner tonight.*
   3. **PMH, meds, allergies, SH, FH:** *No medical problems but has not seen a doctor in 30 years, no meds, NKDA, +tobacco, denies alcohol (when explored quit 2 days ago “because my wife has been asking me to quit for years.” Was drinking 1 fifth (750ml) of whiskey/day, no drugs, no significant family history.*
   4. **PE:** *Vitals: HR - 118, R - 22, BP - 184/102, T - 37.9 0C. Well developed, anxious appearing male. Skin is diaphoretic and flush. Head/Eyes/Ears/Nose/Throat –normal. Neck supple. Lungs clear. Cardiac regular tachycardic. Abdomen soft, +bowel sounds, non-tender, no guarding/rebound. Extremities -normal. Neurologic with intact cranial nerves, motor equal, +tremor, no sensory deficit.*
   5. **Data (labs, EKG, xray):** *WBC 11.1, Hgb 10.3, Plt 68. Na 137, K 2.9, Cl 103, CO2 18, BUN 32, Cr 1.4, Glu 98, Ca 9.0, Mg 1.1, Phos 3.0, AST 242, ALT 115, ALKP 133, T. protein 5.8, alb 3.1, T. bili 1.5. Serum alcohol negative. Utox negative. UA trace protein, 1+ urobili, otherwise normal. EKG sinus tachycardia @110bpm. CXR initially clear, if pt vomits can early aspiration. Head CT negative.*
   6. **Narrative & sequence of events:** *Patient initially brought back by triage nurse because “patient just doesn’t look good.” Patient will give a vague history and physical only remarkable for abnormalities noted above. If SH is asked, pt should initially state he does not drink. (Patient denies alcohol because he quit 2 days ago.) MD should further explore if patient ever drank and quantity; then will get history of daily alcohol use until recently quit. If physician skips social history initially, pt will either vomit/aspirate requiring intubation or seize and be post-ictal for remainder of case. Then doctor will need to obtain history from wife who is in waiting room. Once given history of stopping alcohol, MD should begin treatment for alcohol withdrawal. Small doses of benzodiazepines will give very short times of improvement. Patient will stabilize once given large enough doses of benzodiazepines, started on a benzodiazepine drip or if propofol is used for sedation after intubation. At that point patient can be admitted to ICU.*
   7. **Branch points & complications:** *Patient should start mildly tachycardic and mildly hypertensive (see vitals) but should immediately trend up on both parameters. Trend should continue throughout case until stabilized. During history and physical, pt should complain of nausea and if not give anti-emetics, immediately following physical will vomit and aspirate. Lung sounds will become gurgling and pulse ox will drop despite oxygen. Pt will require intubation. If physician treats nausea pt will not vomit but will have a seizure shortly after physical. Seizure will resolve with benzodiazepines. After seizure pt is post-ictal, combative and attempting to pull out IV, pulling monitor lines off, removing oxygen. Mental status will force sedation or intubation. Small doses of benzodiazepines will transiently improve HR & BP slightly, then upward trend will restart. IV fluid bolus will cause slight improvement in HR but upward trend continues. If HR & BP continue to rise, pt will develop CP and EKG will show ischemia. MD should give multiple doses of benzodiazepines and use higher doses. Can have nurse question high dose benzodiazepine and physician should explain need for higher doses due to cross-tolerance with alcohol. If MD tries to admit patient to floor, hospitalist should refuse because “patient doesn’t sound stable enough for the floor.” Patient will stabilize once several higher doses of a long acting benzodiazepine are given or patient placed on a continuous drip.*
2. **Tips for Instructors**
   * 1. *Initial HR & BP and trend upward should cause a sense of urgency in treating MD. Over the span of the 20 minute simulation, the HR increase without stabilization can be 80-100 beats per minute (bpm) and the BP increase can be 50mmHg. Small doses of benzodiazepines can improve HR 10-15bpm and BP 15 mmHg over 1-2 minutes, then improvement stops and upward trend restarts. Large doses of benzodiazepines can decrease HR 30bpm and BP 40mmHg over 5 minutes. Additional large doses will have similar improvement and stabilize vitals (stop upward trend) after 2nd or 3rd dose. Benzodiazepine drip will decrease HR and BP over 7 minutes and stop upward trend.*
     2. *Patient should distract MD during history by complaining of “feeling really bad” and “I feel sick to my stomach since dinner” etc.*
     3. *If pt unable to give history and doctor does not ask for wife, patient liaison or nurse can prompt by saying “his wife wants to know his condition and is asking to talk to you.” Wife can further prompt by asking “Is this due to his drinking? I’ve been asking him to quit before it kills him.”*
     4. *If MD has pt intubated and sedated on propofol and calls to admit to ICU, admit MD should ask about diagnosis and why pt is improved. “Doesn’t he need to be on a benzodiazepine?”*
3. **Debriefing**
   1. *Discuss value of thorough history and options for additional information (family, EMS, old records, PCP.) Discuss the issue that some patients tend to hide information especially regarding topics outside the social norm. Emphasize the importance of history in condition that do not have specific diagnostic test.*
   2. *Discuss patient management including treatment options regarding different benzodiazepines, routes of administration, dosing schedule. Discuss other important issues in treatment such as correcting electrolytes, especially potassium and magnesium, and giving thiamine and folate which are often low due to poor nutrition.*
   3. *Discuss communication and teamwork during case. Include developing a rapport and using non-judgmental communication with patient, keeping family informed of treatment and condition, and positive interactions with co-workers.*
   4. **Teaching Questions:**
      1. *What are the 4 phases of Alcohol Withdrawal Syndrome?*
         * 1. *Hyperadrenergic: tremulous, anxiety, insomnia, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis, palpitations, elevated blood pressure.*
           2. *Hallucinosis: visual, auditory, tactile but otherwise clear sensorium.*
           3. *Seizures: generally tonic-clonic*
           4. *Delirium Tremens: visual hallucinations, disorientation, agitation.*
           5. *Patients can have any or all phases, usually will have similar picture as previous episodes but may worsen each time.*
      2. *What is the pathophysiology of seizures in alcohol withdrawal?*
         * 1. *Alcohol enhances GABA (inhibitory neurotransmitter). Brain down regulates receptor response (tolerance).*
           2. *Alcohol inhibits NMDA receptor for glutamate (excitatory amino acid). Brain up regulates receptors.*
           3. *Removal of alcohol leads to less inhibitory GABA and more excitatory NMDA which causes symptoms including seizures.*
      3. *What are alternatives to benzodiazepines for treatment of alcohol withdrawal?*
         * 1. *Benzodiazepines are first-line due to cross tolerance with alcohol. Also enhances GABA effects. Higher than expected doses are required. Dose based on symptoms.*
           2. *Barbiturates also work on GABA channels. Causes sedation and may require intubation.*
           3. *Propofol works by opening chloride channels (inhibitory) without GABA and blocks excitatory neurotransmitters. Need airway control.*
           4. *Carbamazepine is used in outpatient settings and in Europe for mild withdrawal. Eases cravings.*
           5. *Other medications:*

*Haloperidol may help in hallucinosis but lowers seizure threshold. Does not replace benzodiazepine.*

*Beta-blockers / central alpha-agonist may give symptom relief but does not treat seizures or Delirium tremens.*

*Phenytoin does not help alcohol withdrawal seizures.*

*Baclofen, gabapentin, lamotrigine, pregabalin, topiramate, tiagabine, valproic acid, vigabatrin all under investigation or preliminary studies show some potential.*
