**Informed Consent with Standardized Patients**

Resources included:

Health Literacy Power point presentation

Informed Consent Power Point presentation

Standardized Patient Scenarios and Training Modules

1. Jan Allen for blood transfusion

Door signs for standardized Patient Encounters

Communication skills checklist for assessment of SP encounter

Explanation

This curriculum was developed for fourth year medical students as part of a larger capstone course (“Gateway to Internship”) at Jefferson Medical College. Students going into all specialties participated in these encounters. They first received the Health Literacy presentation as a formal but interactive didactic, followed by the Informed Consent presentation several days later. They interacted with standardized patients and were given real time feedback from the standardized patients. The SPs then also completed the communications checklist which was reviewed by the course director. Students traditionally have not been trained in obtaining informed consent, but are expected to obtain informed consent beginning in internship. With the majority of their patients having basic or below basic health literacy, it is critically important that students understand how to describe medical procedures in ways their patients can understand, and what components are required for proper informed consent and decision making. Students don’t always understand how to ensure their patients feel comfortable asking questions of their doctor or admitting they don’t understand something. Students and interns don’t always fully understand a procedure themselves or what information a patient needs to know about a procedure to help inform the decision. The educational intervention resulted in 77% of our students using Teach Back as a method to improve patient understanding of the proposed procedure as assessed by the Standardized Patients.

Educational Goals and Objectives with competencies

Health Literacy Didactic—The learner should be able to:

* 1. Increase awareness of how your communication affects your patients’ health (ICS, SBP)
  2. Discuss and practice techniques to improve communication skills with patients (PBLI, ICS)

1. Informed Consent Didactic—The learner should be able to:
   1. Define informed consent and the required elements to achieve it (MK, ICS)
   2. Describe who should be obtaining informed consent (MK, ICS)
   3. Develop an approach towards obtaining informed consent (ICS, PBLI)
   4. Name resources available to assist with informed consent (MK, SBP)
2. Standardized Patient encounter—The learner should be able to:
   1. Explain the indications/risks/benefits of a blood transfusion (PC, ICS)
   2. Demonstrate the Teach Back technique (ICS)
   3. Incorporate feedback from standardized patients into practice (PBLI)

KEY to abbreviations:

MK=medical knowledge

PC=Patient care

ICS=interpersonal and communication skills

SBP=systems based practice

PBLI=Practice based learning and improvement

P=professionalism

Implementation

Each power point lecture is approximately one hour long. They include imbedded activities to encourage active learning and engagement of the student. There are notes included for each presentation (see Notes view) and additional recommended reading for the facilitator. The standardized patient encounters were each 20 minutes long with 5 minutes of feedback allotted at the completion of each encounter. Forty students participated in our course. We had several flights of students rotate through the SP experience and the SPs gave feedback in real time, rather than a large debriefing at the end. Additionally, each SP formally documented the students’ performance using the communication checklist. We recorded the encounters if students wished to view their performance. We used our hospital’s actual consent forms for Blood and Human Source Products and General Procedures as props in the room.

Tips for success

This curriculum was well received by our students. The immediate feedback from the standardized patients was greatly appreciated by the students and they reported that they used feedback from the first encounter as they entered the second encounter. They requested more information to be given to them about the procedures they were requesting consent for prior to the encounters. The scenarios are generalizable enough that almost any specialty or level of learner could interact with them. Many students requested more encounters to continue to refine their communication techniques. We had explained to the SPs that these encounters were purely about the informed consent process, but should have clarified that the students had the history and physical already and should not be evaluated on that piece of the encounter. Several SPs gave feedback about limited history taking to the students which was confusing to the students as they were given that information on the door sign. Limitations to incorporating this curriculum include the cost of training and using standardized patients.
