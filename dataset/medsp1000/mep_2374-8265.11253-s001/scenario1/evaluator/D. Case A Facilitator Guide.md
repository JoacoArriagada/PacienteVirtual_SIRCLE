**Session Background**

This session on disability and the healthcare provider experience was developed by a team at Stanford Medicine for use during the Stanford Conference on Disability in Healthcare and Medicine. Disability is deeply under-examined in medical education, and this session provides a critical framework for participants to analyze the impact of disability identity and ableism in the experiences of providers with disabilities (e.g. physicians, nurses, medical support staff). This session -- offered alongside the partner session on patient experiences with disability in the medical field -- is designed to nurture awareness of structural ableism within the professional medical community and the barriers disabled providers face when seeking accommodations.

**Learning Objectives**

1. Providers with disabilities can face social, cultural, and structural barriers in seeking rightful accommodations.
2. Systemic ableism can manifest as attitudes and behaviors that drive barriers for providers with disabilities.
3. Addressing ableism requires confronting larger systemic problems that encompass all providers, with or without disabilities.

**Case A**

Dr. Michael Smith, a radiology resident, has a recent diagnosis of a chronic illness and is immune-suppressed. As the first wave of COVID-19 patients arrived, he became anxious about being exposed to the virus. He didn't know to whom to turn and felt intimidated to ask, but finally decided to ask his program director if he could read imaging studies from home. His program director was skeptical, did not take him seriously, and did not provide reassurance of such accommodations. Dr. Smith's program director suggested that working remotely might make the other faculty less confident in his abilities as a physician (compared to his non-disabled peers). He was also told that residents couldn't work from home, only faculty. Dr. Smith was made to feel guilty when he was told that *all* healthcare providers are taking a risk during the COVID pandemic and that the ones who had died were "heroes." (Adapted from Poullos, Just Security, 2020)

**Discussion Questions**

1) What are the resident's possible barriers to disclosure and request for accommodation?

2) How can providers with disabilities be supported during times of crisis?

**Discussion Points:**

1. Ask participants if they consider Dr. Smith’s request to his program director as a “reasonable” accommodation. Discuss why or why not. It is important to note that if someone has documented accommodations that have been approved by a disability services provider, those accommodations should be taken as reasonable, not re-evaluated on an individual basis.
2. Consider how ableist standards lead to providers with disabilities feeling “guilt” when self-advocating for their needs.
3. For example, think of what assumptions the program director made about how remote work would damage Dr. Smith’s reputation as a provider amongst his professional peers.
4. Widespread ableist attitudes that negatively impact providers with disabilities are also affecting their non-disabled peers. Stigma against disability is reflected in the work environment and work culture for everyone, perpetuating poor health hygiene and burnout for providers, regardless of disability status.
5. Medical school curricula and medical educators foster awareness of accommodations for providers with disabilities. Sessions to chiefs and directors can improve leadership understanding and provision of accommodations.
6. Allyship between providers can help combat barriers to reasonable accommodation in professional medical settings. By supporting each others’ needs, a healthier culture in medicine can be developed.
7. There are larger implications for the safety and well-being of disabled providers when providers are labeled as “heroes” (or martyrs), particularly during times of crisis. There are ableist constructs that blur the balance between professional and personal responsibility. Being risk-averse in order to protect one’s health should not be looked down upon. Providers are human too, and medicine must make room for that.
8. There are social or cultural norms that may have been barriers to Dr. Smith’s disclosure and request for accommodations. Medicine’s culture values overworking and self-sacrifice. The expectation that by entering medicine, providers are “signing up for it”, disempowers healthcare workers. Overworked and unwell providers make for an unsafe and compromised experience for patients as well.
9. Medical hierarchies should not interfere with responsible provision of accommodations, regardless of whether the provider is an attending or a resident. Fear of lack of accommodations can impact the inclusion of providers with disabilities in medicine, and especially in specialties that present additional barriers to accommodation.
10. Available accommodations and assistive technologies are frequently being updated. Institutions should do their best to remain aware of available options and work alongside providers with disabilities to accommodate their inclusion using available resources.
