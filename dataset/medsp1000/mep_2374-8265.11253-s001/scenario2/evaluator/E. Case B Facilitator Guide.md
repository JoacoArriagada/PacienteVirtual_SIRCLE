**Session Background**

This session on disability and the patient experience was developed by a team at Stanford Medicine for use during the Stanford Conference on Disability in Healthcare and Medicine. Disability is deeply underdiscussed in medical education, and this session provides a critical framework for participants to analyze the impact of disability identity and ableism in the experiences of patients with disabilities. This session, offered alongside the partner session on the experience of healthcare providers with disabilities, is intended to broaden participants’ understanding of the disability culture, barriers faced by the disability community, and equity and inclusion of those with disabilities.

**Learning Objectives**

1. Patients with disabilities face social, cultural, and structural barriers in seeking medical care.
2. Systemic ableism can manifest as attitudes and behaviors that drive health disparities for patients with disabilities.
3. Addressing ableism requires confronting larger systemic problems that encompass all levels of medical training and practice.

Mrs. Jones, a 30-year-old female patient, has spina bifida (SB), a congenital malformation of the spine, which can result in lower extremity paralysis and autonomic dysfunction. She is a wheelchair user and requires bladder management. Mrs. Jones missed her menstrual period and thinks that she may be pregnant. She presents to her OB/GYN physician for evaluation. A pregnancy test is performed, which is positive. The physician enters the exam room, sits down, and says, "I think you should think about abortion." Mrs. Jones is caught off guard, then becomes angry. She explains that this is a desired pregnancy. "Oh, I didn't know, the physician said. I just worry that you might not be able to take care of the baby and that the baby could be taken away." Sobbing, Mrs. Jones leaves the clinic. She finds another provider who was much more supportive. However, even when she was in the hospital having her child, Mrs. Jones couldn't shake the fear that somebody was going to take away the child because they felt that she couldn't take care of him. (Adapted from Streur et al., Disability and Health Journal, 2019)

**Discussion Questions**

1) What went wrong in this interaction, and why?

2) How can we, as healthcare professionals, facilitate a respectful dialogue so that patients can partner in their healthcare?

3) What changes could we make to healthcare education to reduce stigma against patients with disabilities and improve the patient experience?

**Discussion Points:**

1. The physician should not have made assumptions about the patient’s wishes regarding the pregnancy and should have provided a thorough exam and counseling for the patient.
2. Patients, regardless of ability, deserve to be included in medical decision-making
3. Any of the healthcare providers she interacted with should have reassured Mrs. Jones that her disability is not legal grounds for taking away her child.
4. Physicians should recognize patients with disabilities as whole persons. Physicians should not assume a patient’s healthcare or lifestyle preference based on their disability. Additionally, when a patient has a caregiver or partner in the room, the physician should make sure to continue to address the patient and not the accompanying individuals. Talking to a caregiver instead of the patients themselves, dismisses patient autonomy and competency.
5. Disability does not equate to inability---in this case, having SB does not inherently make the patient incapable of being a good parent
6. Healthcare trainees should be taught the potential complications of disabilities alongside the innovations that support patient goals regardless of a patient’s (dis)abilities.
7. Patients without disabilities face inherent privilege in the healthcare system, as their encounters are not circumscribed by the barriers posed by bias, prejudgment, and limited provider training on managing disabilities.
8. Provider training on ableism can assist in deconstructing stigmas and assumptions about patients with disabilities.
9. Patients with disabilities are often not provided proper health exams because providers are not comfortable with examining disabled patients, or do not have the infrastructure or knowledge for doing so. For example, this patient should have received a gynecological exam given her pregnancy status; however, even beyond provider biases, barriers such as clinic set-ups that do not allow patients with disabilities to self-transfer to an exam table, or even access the exam table at all.
10. Including healthcare providers with disabilities can improve healthcare in two ways: (1) by decreasing stigma through peer-peer interactions, and (2) using their knowledge from being on “both sides of the curtain” to improve care for patients with disabilities.
