| **Brief narrative description of case** | Patient is a 15-year-old girl with a history of mild intermittent asthma, otherwise healthy, who presents with agitation and hallucinations secondary to intentional ingestion of jimson weed seeds.  She presents to the emergency department (ED) via ambulance with altered mentation, tachycardia, and hyperthermia. She has signs and symptoms of anticholinergic toxicity and subsequently develops a generalized tonic clonic seizure that resolves with benzodiazepines. Seizure and postictal period are complicated by airway obstruction leading to hypoxemia and respiratory failure requiring intubation. QRS widening develops in the setting of tachycardia, then progresses to ventricular tachycardia (VT) with pulse but poor perfusion. Throughout the case, the patient’s body temperature is also rising.  Anticipated team actions include primary and secondary surveys, treatment of seizure with benzodiazepines, rapid sequence intubation, treatment of widened QRS with sodium bicarbonate (jimson weed is not clearly known to have sodium channel blocking effects but sodium bicarbonate has been used for widened QRS in prior case reports of jimson weed toxicity, given potential for sodium channel blocking effects of other anticholinergics (tricyclic antipsychotics (TCAs), diphenhydramine)), treatment of VT with pulse and poor perfusion per PALS (Pediatric Advanced Life Support), and initiation of external cooling measures. |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

Participants enter the room to find the patient agitated, reaching out in front of herself although nothing is there, intermittently attempting to get up out of bed, speaking nonsensically. She appears flushed and is warm to the touch. She is immediately placed on cardiorespiratory monitors and a full set of vital signs is obtained, demonstrating tachycardia, tachypnea, mild hypertension, and hyperthermia. A primary survey is completed, and signs of anticholinergic toxicity are noted. A blood glucose is requested and is within normal limits. A peripheral IV is requested and blood is obtained for labs during PIV placement. Given her significant tachycardia, an ECG is requested which demonstrates widening of the QRS which was not initially seen on cardiac monitoring. Given concern for anticholinergic toxicity and ECG evidence of QRS widening, a bolus of sodium bicarbonate is administered for cardiac membrane stabilization. Shortly after the ECG is obtained, the patient begins to have a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Supplemental oxygen is placed, but SpO2 continues to drop, and obstructive airway sounds are noted, so jaw thrust and/or a nasopharyngeal airway is utilized with improvement in SpO2. The seizure resolves following two doses of benzodiazepine, after which the patient is minimally responsive with a GCS of 7 and continues to desaturate without jaw thrust and/or nasopharyngeal airway, necessitating rapid sequence intubation. As this is occurring, the team leader asks another team member to alert the PICU. After intubation, SpO2 rapidly improves. However, blood pressure then begins to drop precipitously, and the cardiac monitor demonstrates possible ventricular tachycardia, although central pulses are still able to be palpated. The team recognizes likely ventricular tachycardia with a pulse and poor perfusion and follows the associated PALS algorithm. Defibrillation pads are placed for synchronized cardioversion. As this is happening, a second dose of sodium bicarbonate is also being prepared and administered. Cardiac rhythm returns to sinus tachycardia after one shock of at least 1 J/kg, after which blood pressure and perfusion improve. However, repeat vital signs demonstrate continued increase in body temperature, now dangerously high at 40.5°C, so external cooling measures are initiated, with a cooling blanket, ice packs, wet towels/sheets, and/or evaporative cooling strategies for rapid reduction of body temperature. Could also consider administering physostigmine at this time as the presentation is consistent with severe anticholinergic toxicity. The team continues to provide care as the PICU arrives and handoff occurs for ongoing management of this critically ill patient. The team demonstrates effective teamwork and communication throughout, recognizing changes in the patient’s condition and responding quickly and appropriately to these changes.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. Failure to recognize signs and symptoms of an anticholinergic toxidrome: The team may fail to recognize the presentation of anticholinergic toxicity and thus fail to consider the possibility of a toxic ingestion and thus may not anticipate the life-threatening complications of this, including seizure and dysrhythmia.
2. Failure to request ECG and/or recognize wide complex tachycardia on cardiac monitoring: Initially, the patient’s cardiac monitoring demonstrates significant tachycardia with a narrow complex. The team may not request an ECG, which will demonstrate the first evidence of QRS widening. If an ECG is not requested, the cardiac monitoring will change and start to demonstrate widened QRS. The team may not notice this change on cardiac monitoring and thus may not consider administration of sodium bicarbonate for cardiac membrane stabilization.
3. Failure to follow the correct PALS algorithm: The patient develops a cardiac rhythm consistent with ventricular tachycardia on the monitor and has perfusion changes, but does continue to have palpable central pulses, necessitating synchronized cardioversion but not defibrillation or chest compressions. It is possible that given the ventricular tachycardia and perfusion changes, the team may move towards defibrillation rather than synchronized cardioversion.
4. Failure to initiate cooling intervention: While managing the seizure and ventricular tachycardia, the team may fail to note the progressively increasing temperature and the need for cooling to bring the temperature into a safe range.
