**“Doc, I think I got something stuck…” Instructor Guide**

**Learning Objectives**

1. Learning Objectives/Critical Actions
   1. Perform primary assessment/reassessment (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Deficit) as appropriate based on initial and evolving patient condition
   2. Perform a focused present and past medical history and focused physical exam
   3. Recognize the presence of a critical airway threat – foreign body obstruction
   4. Take appropriate steps to reclaim the airway – object removal, surgical airway, etc.
      1. Magill forceps use under direct laryngoscopy
      2. Emergent cricothyroidotomy

**Implementation and Tips**

This case has had three trial runs with a total of 7 medical students so far. The major initial changes were to adjust the mechanics of the resistance to positive pressure. The initial design was to have the SimMan with complete lung resistance (“can’t bag”), however, it was discovered that this stops respirations by program design. Therefore, there would be an apneic patient with no lung sounds. Stridor is intended to be a major cue for the learners. Thus the initial conditions had to be altered to audible stridor with no lung resistance, and thus no airway changes. Once either the patient decompensates or the learners attempt to provide positive pressure ventilation there needs to be resistance. With the Laerdal technology, this is difficult to achieve – the available options of laryngospasm, tongue edema, and trismus provide no PPV resistance and are physiologically unrelated.
 Additionally, it was found that learners often heard wheezing when listening to the lung sounds that were programmed as stridor. This is an area in which the confederate nurse can guide the learners. The stridor should be audible without a stethoscope and the nurse can mention that he/she is hearing an odd noise or simply “I hear stridor, are you sure?” when the learners say, “I hear wheezing.” The level of prompting should be based on the experience of the learners.
 Finally, in debriefing, learners reported that they appreciated learning to use the nasotracheal intubation scope. We as the teachers were able to demonstrate the use of that scope in a hands-on fashion. This was not included as an objective but it was found to be beneficial once the major objectives had been covered. This fiber-optic scope was on hand as it was used to assist in placing the obstruction in set-up, hence its availability for teaching after completion of the simulation. It has no place in this case as the scope itself has no retrieval tools attached, unlike a true endoscope/bronchoscope.
