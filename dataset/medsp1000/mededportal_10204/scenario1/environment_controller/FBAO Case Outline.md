1. Title – “Doc, I think I got something stuck…”
4. Preparation
   1. Lab set up – ED room or trauma/critical care bay. Airway equipment must be readily available including endotracheal intubation supplies and Magill forceps. Depending on the hosting institution, needle/surgical airway equipment may be provided
   2. Mannequin set up – high-fidelity simulator mannequin such as Laerdal SimMan, adult
   3. Props – normal PA chest x ray, artificial foreign body (milk carton internal seal - plastic ring) placed within the glottis or between the vocal folds, normal CBC and BMP available. Sample images available in the supplemental instructor’s guide.
   4. Distractors (optional) – additional forceps of varying types and sizes. For more novice learners, it is best to keep the Magill forceps amongst the usual airway equipment as they would be in a standard emergency department airway box. For more experienced practitioners, keeping additional distractor equipment present may be appropriate.
5. Actors
   1. Nurse – played by member of the teaching/operating group
   2. Consultant – ENT and/or anesthesia played by faculty/simulator staff (via phone only)
6. Case Narrative
   1. Available at start:
      Triage note – 40 y/o male stating “I feel like there’s something stuck in my throat.”
      VS: HR 95, BP 130/80, RR 20, SpO2 95 % RA
   2. Initial conditions
      VS as above
      Pt was drinking coffee and felt something solid in the coffee move through his mouth, causing him to choke. Initially he coughed and gasped a little, and the feeling of choking resolved partially.
      ROS: Mild shortness of breath, dry cough (like post-nasal drip irritation), no other symptoms
      PMH: none
      meds: none
      PSH: appendectomy at age 4
      allergies: none
      SH: smokes ½ pack a day (tobacco), no recreational drug use
      Physical findings:
      Oropharynx – no trauma, no foreign body visualized
      Lung sounds – stridor
      otherwise normal
   3. Scenario branch points:
      (see flowchart)
      The patient should remain in the initial conditions during the focused history and exam. Suggested: 5-10 minutes
      -At 5-10 minutes from case start learners should begin differentiating the patient’s diagnosis.
      -The patient will decompensate as listed below. This should begin at any of the following points, whichever comes first:
      1) More than 5-10 minutes in progressing to a provisional upper airway diagnosis and intervention decision. We do not recommend a programmed time interval for students, but for more senior personnel a strict time cutoff may be appropriate
      2) Completion of a specialist consultation for elective laryngoscopy
      3) Learners inappropriately sedate a stable patient
      4) The learners begin preparations for elective awake laryngoscopy (e.g. nebulized lidocaine, lidocaine jelly, etc.)
      Decompensation conditions: unresponsive with apnea, desaturation, hypotension, and tachycardia progressing to bradycardia. Until the airway is cleared, the patient continues to decompensate to ventricular fibrillation (suggested 7-10 minutes for students, 5 minutes or less for residents). Learners should begin compressions and defibrillate, resulting in ROSC with apnea and hypoxemia. The patient will not stabilize until the airway is secured, either via direct laryngoscopy with FB removal or cricothyroidotomy. Until the airway is cleared, the patient cannot be ventilated by bag-valve mask (recommended: full lung resistance settings if available) and if an advanced airway with airway clearance is not attempted, the patient continues to relapse into hypoxic VF. After 2-3 cycles of this the patient remains in PEA or asystole at the discretion of the instructors.
      - (Optional) compressions and bag-valve ventilation (BLS foreign body pathway) move the obstruction to right main stem within 3 cycles, and patient partially recovers with left lung only ventilation (pneumothorax settings on the mannequin work well for this condition – no ventilation and no lung sounds)
