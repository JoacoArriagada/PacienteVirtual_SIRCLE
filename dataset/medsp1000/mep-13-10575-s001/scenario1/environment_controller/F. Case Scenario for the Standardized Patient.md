# Clothing and setting for role-playing

Comfortable, loose clothes (e.g. ‘sweat suit’); hospital gown (provided); the SP will be sitting in a chair and not too far from her will be a cot with a sleeping baby (mannequin) covered by a blanket. Alternatively, she may be sitting in a chair with the baby in her arms.

# Description of standardize patient role

Amy is a 28-year-old woman from Canada. This is her second pregnancy. She has had one miscarriage previously but the current pregnancy has gone smoothly and she is very excited about the birth of her first child.

She regularly saw her family physician in Ottawa and feels she has a good rapport. She was offered routine prenatal testing but declined it as she had no specific concerns as her family history does not have any specific medical or genetic conditions that she knows about. She had a brief period of spotting early in her first trimester and it never recurred. She has no history of hypertension or known diabetes. She doesn’t have any history of health problems. She didn’t smoke or take any medication, alcohol, or drugs during this pregnancy.

The fetal ultrasounds done in the first trimester revealed a normal nuchal translucency measurement. The ultrasounds done at the beginning of the second trimester were similarly reported as normal without any notable malformations. Her GBS status was unknown but all serological results so far are negative.

She has been married for two years and her husband, Matt, has been supportive of her pregnancy. There has been some social stress as he works for the Canadian government and frequently travels for work. This will also be his first child and he is very excited about the event. He is otherwise healthy with no contributory medical or surgical history. He was adopted at birth and is not certain of his biological parents’ medical history.

She came to the hospital last night because she had contractions and her water broke. She presented at 39+4 weeks gestation in active labor and, after 20 hours of contractions, delivered vaginally without complications. The Obstetrician congratulates her and tells her that she has a new baby girl. After briefly holding her, the nurse brings the baby to a nearby isolette for routine care. Amy notices the nurse speaking in a hushed tone to the doctor who speaks quietly in return. She feels confused and worried about what is going on and asks how her baby is doing. The nurse says she is doing well. Her daughter is bundled in her blanket and brought to her. Amy and her husband decided early on that if they had a girl they would name her Jasmine. Amy thinks she is beautiful but notices that she doesn’t quite look like her or her husband. The baby keeps slipping down in Amy’s arms and she has to ask for help with readjusting her hold. Amy wants to try breastfeeding but finds Jasmine is struggling to stay latched although she’s heard this is a common thing for newborns.

After Amy is settled, her husband excuses himself with the visiting family to drive them home. A new doctor enters the room after they have left and introduces him/herself as the Pediatrician at the hospital. Amy suddenly feels anxious and wonders what they are there to talk about. She senses that something is about to happen.

# Information to help with role-playing

The doctor will deliver for the first time to Amy, the mother, the unexpected news of a Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) diagnosis.

All approaches should include the following initial components:

- The doctor introduces him/herself and encourages unknown or additional people (i.e. RN, acquaintances) to leave the room.
- The doctor inquires about Amy’s husband and clarifies where he is as well as when he will be back.
- The doctor asks whether Amy would like to hold the baby during the conversation. If she declines, the doctor should place the baby in the small bed and put it close to her so she can still see her face.
- The doctor should periodically look at the baby while speaking.
- To open the encounter, the doctor should speak positively about the baby and may congratulate Amy on the birth of her daughter.
- The doctor should ask whether Amy has noticed anything “unexpected” about her baby.

Amy should respond by saying that the baby seems to have difficulty staying in one position in her arms and that she does not really look like Amy or her husband.

Prior to delivering the diagnosis of Trisomy 21, the doctor should provide a warning sign that alerts Amy to the fact that unexpected news may be coming.

The doctor should specify that they are concerned that the baby has Trisomy 21 and ask what Amy knows about this diagnosis.

An appropriate response is that Amy is a teacher and has met children with Down syndrome through her work so she is aware that they have special learning needs but does not know much beyond this as she has never had any of the children as her students. She has also never had a relative or friend diagnosed with Trisomy 21 nor have they ever had a child with it.

If prompted by Amy, the doctor should explain the risk factors for Trisomy 21 and clarify why the diagnosis was not detected prenatally.

Amy may continue through the encounter using one of the suggested attitudes outlined below.

# 1st SUGGESTED ATTITUDE: Anxious and Expressive

As the doctor starts to deliver the news, Amy may begin to become highly anxious.

She may express her worries through a series of questions and ask about why this happened to her, why all of the ultrasounds during the pregnancy were normal, etc.

As the trainee attempts to answer her questions, she may continue to interrupt with new and additional questions without waiting to hear the appropriate answers.

As her anxiety intensifies, this may be playacted with crying, yelling, or shutting down.

If the trainee struggles with this, the simulation may need to stop for a 3-5 minute discussion and the SP may be asked to wait outside the room.

# 2nd SUGGESTED ATTITUDE: Cooperative

If the doctor has demonstrated strong initial communication skills, Amy may choose to remain receptive and cooperative to the discussion.

She may start to focus on her daughter and cry silently while she hugs and kisses her several times without seeming overly upset or distressed by the news.

Alternatively, she may simply remain pleasant and attentive with open body language and no strongly expressed worries or negative emotions.

# 3rd SUGGESTED ATTITUDE: Shut Down and Detached

Amy may appear shut down and maintain a cold demeanor both toward the trainee and the baby.

She may use non verbal communication (i.e. avoid looking at the baby, avoid face-to-face interaction with the doctor, avoid holding the baby anymore, show indifference even in her answers) to demonstrate that she is no longer cooperative to the interview.

If she continues to answer questions, she should use short phrases that are difficult to elaborate on such as: “yeah, sure”, “if you think so…”, or “okay”.

If the trainee struggles with this, the simulation may need to stop for a 3-5 minute discussion and the SP may be asked to wait outside the room.

# 4th SUGGESTED ATTITUDE: Confrontational

Amy may become very upset and aggravated by the news and openly express her frustration with rhetorical questions.

She may raise her voice and state that she doesn’t wish to proceed with the conversation until her spouse returns or comment that the doctor doesn’t seem to care about her particular circumstance.

Alternatively, she may become more antagonistic and begin to ask more assertive questions such as “are you going to take my baby away from me?” or “are you saying my baby is going to die?”

If the trainee struggles with this, the simulation may need to stop for a 3-5 minute discussion and the SP may be asked to wait outside the room.

Regardless of the attitude expressed, the doctor should wait in the room and give Amy time to express surprise, emotion, crying, or affection.

After explaining the diagnosis, the doctor should transition toward a discussion about what care will be provided for the baby. They should clearly explain what kind of investigations they are planning (ex. blood work, echocardiogram, etc.) and offer a clear timeline for what will be done over the next few days.

If Amy expresses that she wishes to wait for her husband because she will need some time to speak with him alone before any investigations are done, the doctor should acknowledge her desire and explain that certain investigations need to be done in the best interests of the baby and explain what that means and whether there is any urgent matter to investigate. Based on this information, Amy may reconsider her decision and consider going ahead with the investigations. The doctor may also decide to respect her wishes for no investigations at this time or go ahead only with some investigations instead of all of them.

The encounter should conclude with a summary of the plan as well as an effort to establish a follow-up time later that day or in the next few days for further discussion. The doctor may encourage Amy to write down her questions and offer to return when her husband is back to review the entire conversation with both of you.
