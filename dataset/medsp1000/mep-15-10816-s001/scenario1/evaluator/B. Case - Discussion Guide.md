| HPI:  Ms. Jessica Cabral is a 41-year-old female, gravida 3 para 2, who seeks prenatal care at the University Ob/Gyn clinic. Her past obstetric history includes the spontaneous vaginal delivery of an 8-pound, 7 oz. female infant 12 years ago. Her second pregnancy was complicated by the fetal diagnosis of Down’s Syndrome at 18 weeks gestation. She delivered that child via C-section at 37 weeks gestation at University Hospital 4 years ago.  *What do students understand about the pre-natal care for, and delivery of, a child with Down’s Syndrome?*  Her prenatal care would be standard, however, the patient would likely receive specific information from the genetic counselor about what to expect with the child following delivery and may have additional testing performed (i.e. fetal echocardiogram) prior to delivery, depending on what anomalies are found. Delivery may be planned between 37 and 39 weeks due to higher risk for stillbirth, but mothers may deliver via C-section or vaginally. The delivery will also depend on whether heart, GI, or other defects have been diagnosed prenatally.  *What things would a woman normally be screened for at a 16-week prenatal visit?* Assuming it’s not her first visit, this visit would consist of reviewing the initial set of prenatal labs with her physician. The physician would measure fundal height, fetal heart tones, BP, dipstick urine protein and glucose. If she had not yet done genetic screening, they would talk about the quad screen at this time. If they had done the first trimester genetic screening, at this point they would talk about drawing maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein to check for neural tube defects. Since she is 41 years old she would also be offered cell-free fetal DNA screening and amniocentesis.  Today Ms. Cabral presents for a routine prenatal visit at 16 weeks. Her pregnancy thus far has been uncomplicated. She complains of fatigue, knee pain and swelling, and thinks she may have “caught a virus or something going around at her child’s school.” She has had a low-grade fever for the last 3 days, and reports that her eyes are more red, watery and itchy than normal. She recently returned from a two-week visit to Mexico for her mother’s funeral, and reports that she has been feeling more stressed than usual lately due to her mother’s death and the recent travel. She associates her symptoms with this stress, her pregnancy, and “seasonal illnesses.” She reports no history of neck stiffness, seizures, or weight loss. She had a headache a few days ago that resolved with Tylenol.  *What is students’ differential diagnosis for her symptoms?* CMV, parvovirus, mononucleosis, toxoplasmosis. Consider the TORCH infections.  Social History:  Ms. Cabral is recently divorced, and cares for her children as a single parent. Her children’s father just moved out of state and has no contact with Ms. Cabral or her children. Her current pregnancy was unplanned, and her husband has decided not be involved in the pregnancy in any way. She and her two children live in a mid-sized suburban town, where she works as a manager at a local grocery store. Her 12-year-old daughter attends a public middle school, and her 4-year-old son with Down’s Syndrome is in a full-time public daycare program. Ms. Cabral feels constantly stressed about work and financial obligations, and worries she isn’t spending enough time with her children. She receives some support from her church group with transporting her son to his occupational and physical therapy appointments during the week, but outside of that, she has no other help with her children or around the house.  *Other than physical therapy, what other types of care might a child with Down’s Syndrome need to receive?*  Cardiology, gastrointestinal, hearing/vision care, screening for leukemia, thyroid problems, prevention of obesity, frequent primary care visits – increased risk of infection, stomach/bowel problems, early intervention developmental services, mental health/counseling, sleep issues, dental concerns, special education placement and individualized education program (IEP) development at school. Different concerns arise as someone with Down’s Syndrome ages. |
| *What do students make of this finding – abdomen soft, gravid, non-tender? And fundal height?*  This part of the physical exam is feeling for the fundus of the uterus to assess for fetal growth and make sure it matches the level of growth she should be experiencing at this stage in the pregnancy. The uterus moves out of the pelvic brim at 12 weeks, and the uterine height should reach up to the umbilicus by 20 weeks. At a 16 week visit it may be a little hard to feel fundal height, but it should be between the two benchmarks for 12 and 20 weeks. The abdomen should be soft, and it should not be tender to palpation.  *Are these results for fetal heart tones normal?*  Normal range is about 110-160 beats per minute (bpm). Based on the development of the autonomic nervous system, the fetal heart rate starts out higher earlier in the pregnancy and continues to trend downwards as the pregnancy progresses. By term, fetal heart tones are frequently in the range of 120-140. If the measurement for this patient was > 160 bpm, we would be worried about causes for fetal tachycardia. This includes maternal fever, infection, medications, hyperthyroidism, elevated catecholamines, or fetal anemia, arrhythmia, or interruption of fetal oxygenation. If the measurement for this patient was < 110 bpm, we would consider maternal beta-blocker therapy, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, hypothyroidism, or fetal heart block or interruption of fetal oxygenation.  *What do we know from the results of this urine test – negative protein, negative glucose?*  She is not high risk for pre-eclampsia or gestational diabetes at this time. |

| *What is a quad screen and what does it test for? What are the components?*  The four components are unconjugated estriol, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and inhibin-A. Each mass value is converted to the screened woman's gestation-specific multiple of the median (MoM). Multiples of the median also provide a relatively simple way to compare an individual to the entire population being screened. By definition, the central value in an unaffected singleton pregnancy is 1.0 MoM; thus, 2.0 MoM indicates that the woman is at twice the central value, while 0.5 MoM indicates that the woman is at one-half the central value. A woman's a priori risk is determined based upon her chronological age and history of previous Down syndrome pregnancy. This risk is then modified by comparing her serum marker values against published expectations. The final risk estimate is her calculated patient-specific risk of having a fetus affected by Down syndrome in that pregnancy. Second trimester levels of maternal serum AFP, unconjugated estriol (uE3), and first trimester levels of pregnancy associated plasma protein-A (PAPP-A) are lower, on average, in pregnancies affected by Down syndrome than in unaffected pregnancies; beta-hCG and inhibin-A levels are higher, on average, in affected than unaffected pregnancies. |

On ultrasound, the cerebral cortex has increased extra-axial space, the third ventricle is dilated, and both frontal horns are enlarged. And if the local department of health doesn’t run the test, it gets sent to the CDC for them to run it. It can take several weeks for the test results to return.  *How do you test for Zika?*  Routine diagnosis is made by detection of viral nucleic acid by PT-PCR and detection of IgM antibodies by ELISA.  *What are the potential outcomes for her pregnancy with these different diagnoses – zika vs. dengue vs. chikungunya?*  *Zika -* The principal clinical features of congenital Zika syndrome include microcephaly, facial disproportion, hypertonia/spasticity, hyperreflexia, seizures, irritability, arthrogryposis, ocular abnormalities, and sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL). Greatest risk for serious sequelae is when transmission occurs during the first or second trimester.  *Dengue -* Harmful effects of dengue virus infection include death of the unborn baby, low birth weight and premature birth.  *Chikungunya* – this virus does not appear to have any effect on pregnancy outcomes but is commonly included with the other two due to similar presenting symptoms.  *What does decreased fetal head circumference mean for the fetus?*  This finding can indicate developmental delay. The prognosis for microcephaly depends on the underlying cause – the prognosis is worse when it is part of a wider pattern of malformation. The major effect of microcephaly is the neurologic symptoms – intellectual disability, epilepsy, and developmental delay.  *What is the differential diagnosis for a child born with microcephaly?*  Consider genetic or presumably genetic causes (e.g., numerical chromosome aberrations, microdeletions/duplications, monogenic disorders, genetic syndromes), prenatal and perinatal brain injury (e.g., teratogenic exposure, maternal disease, birth complication), craniosynostosis, postnatal brain injury (e.g., infarct, encephalitis, nonaccidental trauma), unknown etiology. Keep in mind that a neonate born with normal head circumference does not EXCLUDE Zika either.  *What further tests or referrals would you want to order for this patient?*  Referral to a genetic counselor and a maternal-fetal-medicine specialist is indicated. If you hadn’t done testing for chromosomal abnormalities, like amniocentesis and quad screen, those should be ordered.  *What are the potential effects of Zika for this pregnancy (mom & fetus)?*  Microcephaly – with a smaller brain, this baby’s brain is more likely to develop improperly. Congenital zika syndrome – this is a pattern of birth defects found among fetuses and babies infected with Zika virus during pregnancy. Congenital zika syndrome is described by the following five features – severe microcephaly where the skull has partially collapsed, decreased brain tissue with a specific pattern of brain damage, damage to the back of the eye, joints with limited range of motion (i.e. clubfoot), too much muscle tone restricting body movement soon after birth. Optional link for more info: https://www.cdc.gov/zika/healtheffects/birth\_defects.html  Ms. Cabral is concerned about what a Zika virus diagnosis means for her at this stage of her pregnancy. She has heard on the news that Zika virus is associated with birth defects and is worried about the potential outcomes for her fetus. When you explain the results of the ultrasound to her, she wants to know what a diagnosis of microcephaly will mean for her baby’s health, and how this diagnosis will affect the rest of her pregnancy. She wants to know if Zika virus can have any other effects on her pregnancy.    She asks about her options for pregnancy termination at this stage in her pregnancy. She is overwhelmed at the prospect of caring for and financing another special-needs child. She has never previously considered an abortion and describes that she “grew up in a conservative family, and never gave much thought to the issue.” She is worried about the stigma she would face in her community if she was to have an abortion. Ms. Cabral expresses that she doesn’t know “where she would go to get a procedure like this,” what the procedure would entail, and if her insurance would cover an abortion. She wants to know what you think she should do.  *How do you counsel a patient about abortion?*  This counseling session is referred to as “options counseling.” This is a conversation in which the provider counsels a woman and helps her decide what she believes will be the best choice for her pregnancy – she can choose to continue the pregnancy to term and keep the child, continue the pregnancy to term and place the child for adoption, or terminate the pregnancy by having an abortion.  *Where can a patient obtain an abortion?*  The answer to this question is entirely location-dependent. In some big cities, abortion is readily available – either at a private physician’s office, at a public or university hospital, or at a “stand-alone” or independent clinic such as Planned Parenthood. The state laws regulating abortion are what tend to determine the location in which physicians or healthcare providers can provide abortions.  *What are some common types of laws that exist to regulate abortion provision?*  Gestational limits: States may prohibit abortion after a particular point in pregnancy (usually specified by gestational age). These laws generally contain an exception that abortion is allowed when necessary to protect the life or health of the woman.  Age regulations: Minors may be prohibited from obtaining an abortion without consent of one or both parents, or laws may specify that parents must be notified before the abortion can be provided.  Regulating type of abortion: Laws may prohibit the abortion procedure itself. One such class of laws are “partial-birth abortion bans,” also known as bans on intact dilation and evacuation, in which a fetus is extracted from the uterus in the breech position, with fetal parts intact.  Physician licensing requirements and hospital requirements: Some states regulate who may perform abortions. This may include physicians, physician assistants, nurse practitioners, and certified nurse midwives. Laws may also be in place that require abortions after a certain gestational age be performed only in a hospital. Laws can specify that a minimum number of physicians be involved in performing the abortion after a particular gestational age. Physicians may be required by law to have admitting privileges to a local hospital (and may specify distance between clinic and hospital).  Funding: States can regulate whether or not state funds may be used to cover abortion procedures. Most states have a specification that funds may be applied for abortion procedures in cases where a woman’s life is in danger or the pregnancy resulted from rape or incest. States may also restrict whether or not abortion can be covered in private insurance plans.  State-mandated counseling: Laws exist that regulate the type of counseling that women must receive before obtaining an abortion, as well as the content of this counseling. Some of the components of counseling that are currently included in abortion laws are: linking abortion to breast cancer, mental health consequences of obtaining and abortion, and the ability of fetuses to experience pain. As part of this module students are encouraged to seek primary literature supporting/disputing these claims  Waiting Periods: A state can enforce a law that requires a woman to wait for a period of time, usually 24 hours, from when she consents to the procedure and receives counseling, to when the abortion may be performed.  *What does an abortion procedure entail?*  There are different types of procedures. Depending on how far along in her pregnancy a patient is, she can choose between multiple procedure types. At this patient’s gestational age, she could choose between induction termination or dilation and evacuation (D&E). Induction termination involves a patient taking mifepristone 24-48 hours in advance of the induction, and then labor is induced with administration of misoprostol. The patient can pursue the same pain management options as a regular labor. Induction abortion allows a woman to go through the process of laboring and giving birth and may be a good option for someone who is terminating a very wanted pregnancy. It can help to provide “closure” for a woman during a time of grieving. This is also an option for patients whose fetus has been diagnosed with an anomaly, because after delivery the fetus can be sent to pathology for an autopsy. The patient can then find out more about the anomaly before pursuing further pregnancies.  The other type of abortion procedure available for this patient is a D&E. This is a surgical procedure that takes place over two days. On the first day the provider places osmotic dilators into the cervix. The dilators look like long match sticks and expand over time to help the cervix open and dilate. The patient returns to the clinic the next day for surgery (usually moderate to deep sedation). The physician will remove dilators from the cervix and use special forceps to remove products of conception. In some situations, the physician will induce fetal demise prior to evacuation of the uterus. The most common pharmacologic agents used in this process are injections of potassium chloride (KCl) and digoxin. These two agents work to produce fetal asystole prior to the procedure. These agents are commonly used in states that have “Partial-Birth Abortion Bans.” These are laws that make it illegal for a physician to remove or partially remove an intact, live fetus from a woman’s body. In these cases, inducing fetal demise before surgical abortion ensures compliance with these abortion laws.  *What options would be available to this patient if she were not so far along in her pregnancy?*  If the patient were in her first trimester, she could pursue early medication abortion or surgical abortion. Early medical abortion is an option for a woman 5-10 weeks LMP. This is a general timeline, that differs slightly based on region and clinic policies. The woman takes pills (mifepristone, misoprostol) to cause her to expel the contents of the uterus. First trimester surgical abortion can be performed anywhere from 5-14 weeks LMP (again, may vary slightly depending on location and policies of the hospital or clinic). Surgical abortion can be performed by manual vacuum aspiration, with a hand-held plastic aspirator, or by electric uterine aspiration.  *What are the potential complications of medication & surgical abortion?*  The main complications of medication abortion are cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, heavy vaginal bleeding, and infection. There is a risk that all the products will not be expelled, in which case the woman would either need to take another course of medication or pursue a surgical abortion. The complications of surgical abortion are heavy vaginal bleeding, infection, uterine perforation, damage to surrounding organs, need for additional surgery, cervical laceration, and retained products of conception.  *What is the after-care involved with a D&E*?  This depends on the physician. They may ask a patient to return to the office for a follow-up visit in 2 weeks. If the patient has been referred by an outside provider, she may instead follow up with that physician.  *How can you counsel a patient who is unsure if she wants to have an abortion?*  Generally, the woman will be counseled to take time and think about it. The most important factor is for her to understand her options and make the decision that is best for her. However, the physician must also counsel her about the time constraints – depending on where the abortion will be performed, she will need to take into account her gestational age, and how long she has to make a final decision about obtaining an abortion.  *If you, as the physician, do not agree with abortion, how do you counsel your patient? Do you have to offer them information about abortion?*  Yes, you are morally and professionally required to give the patient the correct information. You may let her know that you do not provide this kind of care, but it is your duty to help her find someone who will or refer her to another provider that you know can help her. A physician in this situation cannot tell the patient what she should do. Their job instead is to educate her about her options and refer her to other providers (MFM, genetic counselor) who can help her obtain more information to choose the option that is best for her. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists clearly states (related to counseling pregnant women with zika): “Like all pregnant women, Zika-infected pregnant women should have full access to the most complete range of reproductive options, including termination. All pregnant women infected or presumptively infected with Zika virus should be offered comprehensive options counseling, including a thorough discussion of pregnancy continuation, termination of pregnancy, and adoption. As with all patient counselling, health care providers should not seek to impose their personal beliefs upon their patients nor allow personal beliefs to compromise patient health, access to care, or informed consent.” Optional link to ACOG statement: <http://www.acog.org/Resources-And-Publications/Position-Statements/Counseling-Patients-with-Zika-Infection#.WKtqbYUcYZc.email>  *What are the professional guidelines for providers who cannot morally/ethically facilitate abortion?*  The process of refusing to participate in aspects of care based on moral/ethical beliefs is referred to as “conscientious objection” or “conscientious refusal.” The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists provides clear recommendations pertaining to providers’ moral and ethical objections to facilitating care in reproductive medicine, including abortion care. These guidelines specify that a provider may not refuse care if the refusal will negatively impact a patient’s health, if it is based on scientific misinformation, if it imposes religious or moral beliefs onto the patient, or if the refusal itself perpetuates any racial or socioeconomic inequalities that may affect the patient. ACOG’s recommendations state that in a situation where a provider refuses care of a patient, it is their duty to make sure that this patient can receive the desired/needed care in a safe, timely manner: “All health care providers must provide accurate and unbiased information so that patients can make informed decisions. Where conscience implores physicians to deviate from standard practices, they must provide potential patients with accurate and prior notice of their personal moral commitments. Physicians and other health care providers have the duty to refer patients in a timely manner to other providers if they do not feel that they can in conscience provide the standard reproductive services that patients request. In resource-poor areas, access to safe and legal reproductive services should be maintained. Providers with moral or religious objections should either practice in proximity to individuals who do not share their views or ensure that referral processes are in place. In an emergency in which referral is not possible or might negatively have an impact on a patient's physical or mental health, providers have an obligation to provide medically indicated and requested care.” This means that regardless of a providers’ personal beliefs, they must make sure that their patient has all the information and resources needed to seek abortion from another provider. This includes making sure patient has referral to a provider that can provide these services in a safe and timely manner. Optional link to ACOG Committee Opinion: https://www.acog.org/Clinical-Guidance-and-Publications/Committee-Opinions/Committee-on-Ethics/The-Limits-of-Conscientious-Refusal-in-Reproductive-Medicine  *If she asks, “what do you think I should do,” can you give her your opinion?*  You can let her know your own personal opinion if you feel that is appropriate, but you must let her know that it is her choice and you cannot make the decision for her. Let her know it is your job to make sure she has all the information surrounding the potential outcomes and her options. Reinforce that the choice is hers, not yours, to make. |
