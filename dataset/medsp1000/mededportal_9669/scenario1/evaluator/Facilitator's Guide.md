**Resource Description:**

This 90-minute simulation provides participants with hands-on experience practicing the coma examination in a safe environment. It requires only a low-fidelity mannequin (or a simple life-sized doll), with eyelids that can be opened and a head that can be turned. No technical support is needed, and the simulation can be run in any room large enough to accommodate a 6-foot table or hospital bed and the participants.

During the introduction, participants discuss the cranial nerves responsible for brainstem reflexes. When the simulation begins, participants play the role of treating physicians. They must demonstrate the ability to take a focused history from a family member, perform a brain death examination, review the medical record (including vital signs and ventilator logs, a CT scan, and laboratory evaluation), counsel the family member, and manage conflict. After the debriefing process, all participants type a brief summary of the case, email it to the faculty, and discuss it with the group. The optimal size of the group is 4-6 participants. However, the simulation can be run effectively with larger groups.

**Target Audience:** pre-clinical and clinical medical students, residents

**Objectives:**

The objectives of this simulation are aligned with those of the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) publications. The neurology clerkship core curriculum guidelines[[1]](#endnote-1) state that medical students should have the ability to examine patients with an altered level of consciousness and understand the general principles for evaluating brain death. The AAN Resident Core Curriculum [[2]](#endnote-2) states that residents should be able to diagnose brain death, discuss psychosocial issues (including end-of life care), and discuss commonly used scales for rating severity of illness in critically ill persons.

Participants will perform the following:

1. Discuss the cranial nerves responsible for brainstem reflexes.
2. Obtain a history from a distraught family member.
3. Demonstrate the following techniques of examining a patient with altered level of consciousness: mental status (level of arousal, response to auditory stimuli, response to visual stimuli, response to noxious stimuli applied centrally and to each limb individually); and cranial nerves (response to visual threat, pupillary light reflex, oculocephalic reflex, vestibulo-ocular reflex, corneal reflex, gag reflex).
4. Review the medical record, including the vital signs and respiratory logs, and laboratory evaluations.
5. Determine whether the patient meets criteria for brain death.
6. Counsel the family member.
7. Within 5 minutes, type a clear and concise note (100 words or less), summarizing the case.

**Background and Literature Search:**

In March of 2011, we began the first neurology clerkship for the inaugural class of the Florida International University Herbert Wertheim College of Medicine. Our third-year medical students had learned about coma, brainstem reflexes, and brain death during the second and third years. They had had traditional methods of teaching, such as reading assignments, lectures, and participation in patient care. However, we noted that our students were submitting H&Ps of actual coma patients that were lacking the appropriate level of detail.

We conducted a literature search on MedEdPORTAL using the terms “brain death” and “coma.” There were no published simulations on the determination of brain death. Utilizing a low fidelity mannequin, we developed a coma simulation that would require students play the role of physicians and conduct a complete brain death examination. In addition, we took the opportunity to review cranial nerve/brainstem anatomy and common lesions by developing an extensive checklist to review during the introduction (file name is “coma demonstration”).

In order to allow students to practice managing conflict, we scripted in a disruptive physician and a distraught family member.

**Development:**

At the time we developed this simulation, our simulation center was under construction, and our high fidelity mannequins were on order. We found a broken mannequin in the storage closet, and found it to be perfect for the brain death examination. We set up the doll in a large room and placed it on a massage table. Over time, we added components to the set to make the simulation seem more realistic. We covered the massage table with sheets, intubated the mannequin, added respiratory tubing and an iv pole with normal saline. We video recorded an ICU monitor that displayed normal vital signs and a normal EKG tracing. We recorded a second video of an ICU monitor, in which the EKG converts to asystole and the oxygen saturations and blood pressure plummet. When we run the simulation, the faculty member projects the ICU monitor videos onto the wall-mounted screen, or from a lap-top computer.

Currently, we have a number of high-fidelity mannequins and a high-tech simulation center at our disposal. However, we have continued to use the low-fidelity mannequins and various classrooms for the coma simulations. Part of the reason is logistics and cost—there is no need to schedule and pay technicians to run the simulation in its current form. However, the main reason is that we do not need a high fidelity mannequin or high-tech simulation center for this particular session.

Over the two years we have run the simulation on third-year medical students, we have made only minor adjustments to the scripts and the simulation itself. We have, however, developed a longer and more extensive checklist to be utilized during the demonstration phase of the session. Our medical students frequently ask questions about the cranial nerves involved in each of the brainstem reflexes and the presentation of common lesions. Outcomes will be presented below.

**Participant preparation:**

Participants should prepare by reading a standard neurology textbook about the examination of the coma patient, the differential diagnosis of coma, and brain death. A suggested resource is as follows: Greenberg DA, Aminoff MJ, and Simon RP. Clinical Neurology, 8th edition,McGraw-Hill Global Education Holdings, LLC. 2012.

For higher level participants, such as residents, it would be advantageous to require the same reference materials listed under faculty preparation.

In order to help students prepare for the interactions with the family member we include the following instructions in our syllabus.

Interacting with an emotional family member

Family members may be emotional, angry, or distraught. Grief may be expressed as anger and directed at the physician. If this occurs, don’t be caught off-guard. There are a number of ways one can diffuse the situation.

1. Empathy: Unless you have been in the situation, it can be difficult to imagine a family member undergoing a brain death examination. The family member feels a complete loss of control, and is looking to you to provide answers. You may feel you are being attacked or blamed, but realize this is part of a normal grieving process. Tell the family member that you realize this is an extremely difficult situation.

2. Introductions: Introduce yourself, and shake hands with the family member. Ask what is his or her relationship with the patient. Afterwards, refer to the patient as “your mother.”

3. Comfort: Pull up a chair, and invite the family member to have a seat. This will make the family member more comfortable and give you more room to complete your examination.

4. Reassurance: You can’t reassure the family member that everything will be alright. You can, however, reassure the family member that you will conduct a thorough examination and answer all questions to the best of your abilities. This is the hardest thing for medical students, because you want to give hope and offer treatment or a cure. However, at this moment, the family member needs facts and guidance through this process.

5. Using lay terminology: Ensure that you use lay terminology. The family member is overwhelmed with grief and may have difficulty comprehending everything that is happening. Avoiding medical jargon can help.

6. Conducting the examination: You may wish to refer to the exam as a coma examination or examination of brainstem reflexes (rather than a brain death examination). Until you complete the examination, you won’t know whether or not she meets brain death criteria. In addition, the family member may ask if her mother feels any pain, or is aware of her surroundings. Since she has been unresponsive for 2 days, and not on any medications, you can assume she cannot. You will have a better idea when you attempt to elicit a response to pain. This is necessary for a complete assessment of the patient. The rest of the examination is not particularly uncomfortable or painful. You can guide the family member through the examination as follows.

a. Corneal reflexes: This is not a painful test. It feels similar to getting an eyelash stuck in one’s eye.

b. Cold calorics: This is not a painful test, but may feel uncomfortable. If the patient were awake, it would cause a dizzy sensation, like getting water in one’s ear.

c. Gag reflex: This is the sensation one might feel when placing the toothbrush too far back in your throat. You are trying to elicit a gag response.

d. Sternal rub, pinching of the extremities: These tests are designed to elicit pain. They are a necessary component of the examination. If the patient responds to pain, it is considered a normal response.

e. Apnea test: if the patient is not breathing over the ventilator, an apnea test will be necessary and appropriate. It is the final test performed in the brain death examination. You can assure the family member that you will follow proper procedure, and that all precautions will be taken. If her mother becomes unstable, you will abort the test and place her back on life support. If she does not become unstable, you will place her back on life support when the test in completed.

Brain death:

Brain death is the irreversible cessation of all brain and brainstem functions. When performed correctly, there is no chance of recovery. State laws contain slight variations from each other and from the current American Academy of Neurology (AAN) guidelines. In this simulation, we will use the AAN guidelines.

If the patient meets brain death criteria, there is no need for a family member to make decisions about withdrawal of care. At that time, the patient meets the legal definition of death. The patient is declared dead, and a death certificate will be completed. The family should be informed the patient has died.

Oftentimes, a family member will ask if it is possible to leave the ventilator running for another hour or two, in order to have family or friends come in to say goodbye. Typically these requests are granted, depending upon the resources of the hospital.

Organ donation:

The treating physician serves as the patient’s advocate; therefore, does not broach the subject of organ donation. The organ procurement organizations will be responsible for screening the patient and discussing this with the family member. If the family member wants to discuss it with the physician, that is acceptable.

Faculty participating in this simulation should be comfortable examining coma patients. **Instructor notes:**

Timeline:

0-20 minutes Demonstration and discussion of the coma examination

21-40 minutes Simulation

41-80 minutes De-briefing

81-90 minutes Typing and review of case summaries

Demonstration of the coma examination:

During the session, a faculty member leads the demonstration around the bed of the mannequin. Participants take turns examining the mannequin and describing the cranial nerves involved in each of the reflexes. The included checklist (file name “coma demonstration”) is suggested as a resource.

Simulation:

Once the demonstration has been completed, the faculty member should inform all participants that each will be required to write a brief paragraph summarizing the case at the conclusion of the debriefing session. The summaries will be emailed to the faculty member and reviewed with the group.

Next, the faculty member should assign roles, distribute scripts, assemble the clipboards, and affix the head CT to the wall, as follows.

2 participants play the role of treating physicians. Hand them their instructions and ask them to step out of the room.

1 participant plays the role of a disruptive physician signing out the case to the incoming team. After saying his or her piece, that individual may also be needed to redirect the physicians. Hand this person the disruptive physician script.

1 participant (or a faculty member) plays the role of the distraught child of the patient. Hand this person the family member script.

1 or 2 participants can observe the technique of the physicians and give feedback at the conclusion of the session (this part is optional, if there are not enough participants). Hand those individual additional copies of the brain death checklist.

2 clipboards should be assembled for the physicians.

Clipboard # 1 contains the following: Serum studies; Urine toxicology screen; Vital signs log; Respiratory care services mechanical ventilation sheet;

Clipboard #2 contains the following: Checklist for determination of brain death

The head CT should be taped to the wall near the patient.

The summary of the case should be in the possession of the faculty member.

Small groups of up to 6 participants work best. However, we have run the session with 8 or more students in the room.

Once the scripts have been distributed, ensure that every participant understands his or her role. The faculty member will then start the normal video of the ICU monitors. (At the beginning of the session, the EKG monitor will show normal sinus rhythm. The blood pressure, pulse, and oxygen saturation will be within normal limits.) At this time, the physicians will have had the opportunity to read their instructions. They should be invited into the room and the simulation will begin.

Optimal management path**:**

The disruptive physician will say his or her piece and leave the simulation (taking a seat as an observer). The family member will then enter the scenario. The family member will be distraught and/or angry. Typically, the physicians will introduce themselves, console the family member, and take a history. Next, the physicians will ask the family member to take a seat while they conduct the physical examination. The family member may ask questions during the examination, and the physicians will answer the questions to the best of their abilities.

Ideally, the physicians should recognize the patient does not yet meet brain death criteria, because she is breathing over the ventilator. However, she has been unresponsive for 2 days, has lost all of her brainstem reflexes, and has a very large left-sided intraparenchymal hemorrhage with extension into the ventricles. The physicians should inform the family member that the prognosis is extremely poor.

Although the physicians should not broach the subject, the family member should inform the physicians the patient is opposed to organ donation. After all of the above have occurred, the ICU monitors should be changed to the asystole version. Alarms will go off. The oxygen saturations will start trending down and the blood pressure will cycle again and be undetectable. Respirations will continue, as the patient is still on the monitor. The physicians and family member will turn their attention to the monitor. When the patient goes asystole, the physicians usually call a code. Occasionally, the family member (who is the health care proxy) recognizes the prognosis is extremely poor and indicates the patient would not wish to be prolonged in that condition. The physicians would respect the family member’s wishes and would not code the patient. They would console the family member.

Alternative path:

Alternatively, the physicians may not notice the patient is breathing over the ventilator. Oftentimes, they call for the respiratory therapist to help with the apnea test. The disruptive physician will then re-enter the scenario to redirect them. No apnea test is indicated because the patient is breathing over the ventilator. At that point, the physicians recognize the patient does not meet brain death criteria.

After the above occurs, the asystole video will be played. Alarms will go off. The oxygen saturations will start trending down. The blood pressure will cycle again and be undetectable. Debriefing Plan**:**

The debriefing process should start with allowing the physicians to express how they feel. Allow the participants to get this out of their systems, before moving on to the more technical and clinical aspects of their performance.

Next, allow the simulated family member of the patient to give feedback to the physicians. You may ask, “How did you feel you were treated by the physicians?” Other observers can comment on how the physicians interacted with the family member.

Next, ask the observers with the brain death checklist to comment. “Did you agree with the way the brain death examination was performed?” “Did the physicians address all of the necessary items on the checklist?” “Could you give any feedback to the participants on their overall assessment of the patient?” “Did you agree with how the head CT was interpreted?”

Finally, the faculty member should weigh in on any of the above topics. Management and prognostication of patients with intracerebral hemorrhage can also be addressed.
