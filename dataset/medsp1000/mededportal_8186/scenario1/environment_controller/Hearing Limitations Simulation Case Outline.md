I. Interviewing a Grandparent Caregiver with Age Related Hearing Loss

II. Environment:

A. Location: Pediatric ER   
B. Young child (4-5 years) mannequin, on monitor   
C. Props- no special props needed. Recommend having chairs for the evaluating resident to sit in, but placed in such a manner that the resident must move them to an ideal location for communicating effectively with the historian. If possible have a television on in area at beginning of case. May add other distracters that may be present in an ER, such as cell phones, frequent paging.

III. Roles

A. ER nurse, Older Adult Caregiver (age 65 or greater if possible), voice for mannequin. The mannequin is used in this case to provide realistic distractions during the interview of the adult caregiver, as may be encountered during a pediatric visit. The mannequin may appear somnolent at first, but become increasingly more vocal during the case, making statements such as, “I want to go home, I feel fine.”

IV. Case Narrative

A. Scenario Overview

1) 5 year old child has been brought to the ER by grandmother with the chief complaint that the child is really sleepy. Pediatric resident to elicit history. The historian is the child’s grandmother (GM), who is his primary caretaker. Grandmother has hearing impairment, which is not revealed during the history gathering. If asked, GM reveals she found an opened bottle of medication in the bathroom floor. If asked, the medication is her blood pressure medication, Clonidine. The scenario proceeds as written below whether or not the residents identify what medication was ingested, as it would in a real life situation. Since the patient in this scenario has ingested a small dose of Clonidine he requires supportive care, but no specific antidote or treatment. GM can give the following information if asked:

2) Past Medical History- child has a history of asthma and ADHD

3) Medication- child is on albuterol prn, and concerta; he has no known drug allergies; Grandmother is on clonidine, Lipitor and glipizide, grandfather is on Captopril and Plavix.

4) Family History- GM has history of hypertension, diabetes type II, hyperlipidemia, and rheumatoid arthritis; GF has hypertension and history of CAD; dad’s history is unknown and mom has a history of asthma and drug dependency.

5) Social History: Patient is in Grandmother’s custody and doesn’t attend daycare.

B. Presenting situation: Resident admission team (may include upper level resident with intern, $+ / -$ medical students) receive a call from the ER to admit a child who is suspected to have ingested a medication. The team proceeds to the ER to gather a history from the child’s guardian. The team is informed that the ER suspects Clonidine ingestion and that the child is physiologically stable. They are told that the child needs to be admitted for 24 hour observation.

C. Scenario conditions initially- Grandmother is hard of hearing, so has difficulty understanding the resident’s questions. She is seated at the right side of the child’s bed (far side from window) and is trying to look intently at the resident’s face. She does not understand all of the resident’s questions and appears to answer different questions than what has been asked.

Grandmother should be advised to inconsistently respond to the resident’s questions unless the resident is seated across from her at eye level, and is speaking slowly and clearly. The mannequin voice can be used to provide realistic distraction during the case, with the child stating that he would like to go home, and that he is fine.

1) If asked what happened, Grandmother gives history that child had been behaving normally that morning, playing alone in the house while she did laundry and cleaning chores, but when it was time to eat lunch her grandson was very sleepy, which is very unusual for this child with ADHD.

2) Grandmother had a hard time awakening him and then keeping him awake, so she went to the ER. She reports his symptoms have been present for 2.5 hours or so.

3) If asked, Grandmother reports that she keeps her medications in the bathroom next to the sink so she can remember to take them. She says the medications are on a shelf above the sink, so she doesn’t think he would have bothered them. She gets the easy to open pill containers due to her arthritis.

4) Common questions posed to grandmother and their answers:

i. Why is her grandson in her custody? The grandson is in his grandmother’s custody because his mother is currently in a drug rehab program. The patient has lived with his grandmother most of his life.

ii. Where did grandmother find the patient? She found him lying on the floor of the living room asleep. It was not his naptime, so she thought this was very unusual.

iii. Does she know how many pills he took? No, she just noticed there were some pills spilled on the floor, and then rushed him to the hospital. She just had her prescription filled 3 days ago, so the bottle was nearly full.

iv. Has her grandson even gotten into something like this before? No, he has never had an accidental ingestion. He has been to the ER before after sticking a bean in his nose.

v. Does the grandmother know what medication he took? She isn’t sure. She has 3 medications on her bathroom sink, one for her arthritis, one for her diabetes, and her blood pressure medicine. She thinks he may have took her Clonidine, but isn’t sure.

vi. Does grandmother know of a safer location she could store her medications? The grandmother states that it can be hard for her to remember to get to her medications and asks for suggestions of safe locations to store the medications in the future.

vii. Where does the grandmother get her prescriptions filled? At the local Wal-Mart.

viii. Is anyone at home that could count the number of pills? The patient’s grandfather is at home, and when asked, counts the pills and finds that 2 are missing.

ix. What is the dose of clonidine she takes? She isn’t sure, but it is 0.1mg.

x. Had the patient had any symptoms prior to a few hours ago? No, he was completely fine this morning.

5) Exam (if performed):

i. General: child is somnolent, but arousable ii. HEENT: miosis, dry mucous membranes

iii. CV- bradycardic, no murmurs   
iv. Lungs- clear to auscultation but slightly slow respiratory rate   
v. Abdomen- normal exam   
vi. Neuro exam- slightly hypotonic throughout, somnolent

6) Physiology:

i. Heart rate in the 60s, blood pressure on the low side 80/40 or so   
ii. Respiratory rate in the mid teens   
iii. Weight is $2 5 \mathrm { k g }$   
iv. No fever

D. Scenario branch points- the branch points listed below do not affect the outcome of the case, but are simply provided in the case the residents chooses to pursue one of the listed courses of actions. It is not necessary to pursue or discuss the branch points during the case.

1) Administration of naloxone- the residents may potentially decide to administer naloxone, which has been used in the case of clonidine ingestion, although the efficacy of naloxone in clonidine ingestion is still being investigated. If given, the patient has minimal increases in his heart rate and blood pressure.   
2) Drug screen- residents may potentially request a drug screen during the case. Urine drug screen may be reported as negative, and if requested thin layer chromatography can be sent, but results will not be available until for 1-2 days.
