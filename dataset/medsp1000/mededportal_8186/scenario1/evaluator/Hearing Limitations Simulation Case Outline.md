I. Interviewing a Grandparent Caregiver with Age Related Hearing Loss

II: Audience: Pediatric Residents and $3 / 4 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ year Medical Students

III. Learning Objectives

A. Primary: 1) History taking and communication with an older adult caregiver with hearing limitations 2) Medication safety counseling   
B. Secondary: 1) Care of a child with clonidine ingestion 2) Recognition that age-related medical conditions in older adult caregivers impact pediatric care.   
C. Critical actions checklist 1) Team should turn the TV off prior to gathering history 2) Resident should remain at eye level with the historian while gathering the history. 3) Resident should maintain eye contact with grandparent while gathering history. 4) Resident should speak slowly in a clear voice. 5) Team should ask about other medications in the home. 6) Team should ask where medications are stored in the home. 7) By the end of the case, the team should have identified that the caregiver has hearing loss.

IV. Environment:

A. Location: Pediatric ER   
B. Young child (4-5 years) mannequin, on monitor   
C. Props- no special props needed. Recommend having chairs for the evaluating resident to sit in, but placed in such a manner that the resident must move them to an ideal location for communicating effectively with the historian. If possible have a television on in area at beginning of case. May add other distracters that may be present in an ER, such as cell phones, frequent paging.

V. Roles

A. ER nurse, Older Adult Caregiver (age 65 or greater if possible), voice for mannequin. The mannequin is used in this case to provide realistic distractions during the interview of the adult caregiver, as may be encountered during a pediatric visit. The mannequin may appear somnolent at first, but become increasingly more vocal during the case, making statements such as, “I want to go home, I feel fine.”

VI. Case Narrative

A. Scenario Overview

1) 5 year old child has been brought to the ER by grandmother with the chief complaint that the child is really sleepy. Pediatric resident to elicit history. The historian is the child’s grandmother (GM) (or grandfather (GF)), who is his primary caretaker. Grandmother has hearing impairment, which is not revealed during the history gathering. If asked, GM reveals she found an opened bottle of medication in the bathroom floor. If asked, the medication is her blood pressure medication, Clonidine. The scenario proceeds as written below whether or not the residents identify what medication was ingested, as it would in a real life situation. Since the patient in this scenario has ingested a small dose of Clonidine he requires supportive care, but no specific antidote or treatment. GM can give the following information if asked:

2) Past Medical History- child has a history of asthma and ADHD

3) Medication- child is on albuterol prn, and concerta; he has no known drug allergies; Grandmother is on clonidine, Lipitor and glipizide, grandfather is on Captopril and Plavix.

4) Family History- GM has history of hypertension, diabetes type II, hyperlipidemia, and rheumatoid arthritis; GF has hypertension and history of CAD; dad’s history is unknown and mom has a history of asthma and drug dependency.

5) Social History: Patient is in Grandmother’s custody and doesn’t attend daycare.

B. Presenting situation: Resident admission team (may include upper level resident with intern, $+ / -$ medical students) receive a call from the ER to admit a child who is suspected to have ingested a medication. The team proceeds to the ER to gather a history from the child’s guardian. The team is informed that the ER suspects Clonidine ingestion and that the child is physiologically stable. They are told that the child needs to be admitted for 24 hour observation.

C. Scenario conditions initially- Grandmother is hard of hearing, so has difficulty understanding the resident’s questions. She is seated at the right side of the child’s bed (far side from window) and is trying to look intently at the resident’s face. She does not understand all of the resident’s questions and appears to answer different questions than what has been asked.

Grandmother should be advised to inconsistently respond to the resident’s questions unless the resident is seated across from her at eye level, and is speaking slowly and clearly. The mannequin voice can be used to provide realistic distraction during the case, with the child stating that he would like to go home, and that he is fine.

1) If asked what happened, Grandmother gives history that child had been behaving normally that morning, playing alone in the house while she did laundry and cleaning chores, but when it was time to eat lunch her grandson was very sleepy, which is very unusual for this child with ADHD.

2) Grandmother had a hard time awakening him and then keeping him awake, so she went to the ER. She reports his symptoms have been present for 2.5 hours or so.

3) If asked, Grandmother reports that she keeps her medications in the bathroom next to the sink so she can remember to take them. She says the medications are on a shelf above the sink, so she doesn’t think he would have bothered them. She gets the easy to open pill containers due to her arthritis.

4) Common questions posed to grandmother and their answers:

i. Why is her grandson in her custody? The grandson is in his grandmother’s custody because his mother is currently in a drug rehab program. The patient has lived with his grandmother most of his life.

ii. Where did grandmother find the patient? She found him lying on the floor of the living room asleep. It was not his naptime, so she thought this was very unusual.

iii. Does she know how many pills he took? No, she just noticed there were some pills spilled on the floor, and then rushed him to the hospital. She just had her prescription filled 3 days ago, so the bottle was nearly full.

iv. Has her grandson even gotten into something like this before? No, he has never had an accidental ingestion. He has been to the ER before after sticking a bean in his nose.

v. Does the grandmother know what medication he took? She isn’t sure. She has 3 medications on her bathroom sink, one for her arthritis, one for her diabetes, and her blood pressure medicine. She thinks he may have took her Clonidine, but isn’t sure.

vi. Does grandmother know of a safer location she could store her medications? The grandmother states that it can be hard for her to remember to get to her medications and asks for suggestions of safe locations to store the medications in the future.

vii. Where does the grandmother get her prescriptions filled? At the local Wal-Mart.

viii. Is anyone at home that could count the number of pills? The patient’s grandfather is at home, and when asked, counts the pills and finds that 2 are missing.

ix. What is the dose of clonidine she takes? She isn’t sure, but it is 0.1mg.

x. Had the patient had any symptoms prior to a few hours ago? No, he was completely fine this morning.

5) Exam (if performed):

i. General: child is somnolent, but arousable ii. HEENT: miosis, dry mucous membranes

iii. CV- bradycardic, no murmurs   
iv. Lungs- clear to auscultation but slightly slow respiratory rate   
v. Abdomen- normal exam   
vi. Neuro exam- slightly hypotonic throughout, somnolent

6) Physiology:

i. Heart rate in the 60s, blood pressure on the low side 80/40 or so   
ii. Respiratory rate in the mid teens   
iii. Weight is $2 5 \mathrm { k g }$   
iv. No fever

D. Scenario branch points- the branch points listed below do not affect the outcome of the case, but are simply provided in the case the residents chooses to pursue one of the listed courses of actions. It is not necessary to pursue or discuss the branch points during the case.

1) Administration of naloxone- the residents may potentially decide to administer naloxone, which has been used in the case of clonidine ingestion, although the efficacy of naloxone in clonidine ingestion is still being investigated. If given, the patient has minimal increases in his heart rate and blood pressure.   
2) Drug screen- residents may potentially request a drug screen during the case. Urine drug screen may be reported as negative, and if requested thin layer chromatography can be sent, but results will not be available until for 1-2 days.

VII. Instructor Notes

A. Since this case is primarily about communication, the patient has already been stabilized and has relatively normal vital signs. If the residents are accustomed to participating in simulation cases they may become too focused in watching the monitors in the room and may not pursue getting the history from the guardian. To prevent such a situation, it may be worth advising the case participants that their role is primarily obtain a history.   
B. Tips to direct the actors- The actors should be advised to act as if they have significant hearing loss such that the residents should have to actively try to overcome their communication gap. As stated before, the grandmother should be advised to not consistently respond to the resident’s questions unless the resident is seated across from them at eye level, and is speaking slowly and clearly. She should seem to ignore questions from learners who are not directly in front of her. She should appear distracted and worried about the child.   
C. Since this is primarily a history gathering exercise, there are not any potential medical complications, only difficulty eliciting a clear history. a. If the residents do not identify the ingested substance there will be no change in the patient’s clinical condition. During debriefing, residents can be asked how they can try to figure out what medications are in the home if the caretaker is not sure.

D. The exercise should end after about 15 minutes and the residents and Grandparent move to the Debriefing area.

VIII. Debriefing/Teaching Plan

A. Debriefing 1) Should occur with the group with review of video if possible. 2) Knowledge support available should include Poison Control Toxicology brief for Clonidine.

B. Key points/general principles:

1) Effective communication: the historian in this case had hearing impairment. Strategies to improve communication in this situation include:

i. Speak slowly and clearly. Do not shout. ii. Make sure the light source falls upon your face (not behind it) so your face is easy to watch.   
iii. Try to eliminate as much background noise as possible.   
iv. Don’t eat candy/chew gum or cover your mouth while speaking. v. Try to look at the person you are communicating with instead of looking at objects such as a computer.   
vi. Repeat your statement if it not understood, but try to use different phrasing.   
vii. Position yourself 3-6 feet away from the hearing impaired person.   
viii. For females, try to lower the tone of your voice (Age related hearing loss is in the high frequencies, so lower voices are heard more clearly).   
ix. Have the caregiver repeat back any instructions you may have given to make sure you were clear: “Can you repeat back to me what we have discussed so I can make sure I explained it all clearly?” Use role play here: appoint a resident and someone to act as a grandparent. Ask the learner how they would insure that the grandparent had understood their instructions.

2) Management of clonidine ingestion:

i. Mostly supportive ii. Can use activated charcoal if within 2 hours of ingestion iii. Treat hypotension/respiratory suppression if significant iv. Naloxone has been used at times; however published studies regarding its use are inconclusive if beneficial.

3) Grandparents and Ingestions:

i. Estimated that grandparent’s medications are involved in 10- $2 0 \%$ of unintentional pediatric ingestions 1   
ii. In one study, $6 / 1 1$ toddlers admitted for clonidine ingestion took a grandparent’s medication 2

iii. Often times physical accessibility to the medication is more important than the medication container (child proof cap versus unsecured container) 1

iv. When counseling on medication storage, it is important to recommend storing the medication in a safe location that would not be accessible to the child, yet available for the grandparents. There are locking medication storage boxes that are available, or a high locking cabinet is a good option. Work with the grandparent to identify a solution that will work for them and keep the child safe. This is another opportunity for role play. Ask a learner to identify how the grandparent stores their medicines and then counsel about safe medication storage.

C. Questions to Facilitate Debriefing:

1) During feedback, the Feedback Tool which was completed during the case by the instructors can be reviewed with the case participants. In addition the questions below can be used to facilitate discussion.

2) What was your main challenge to getting the history from the caregiver?

i. Was it frustrating?   
ii. What do you think was going on that affected communication?   
iii. What techniques can you use to communicate with someone with hearing impairment?   
iv. What techniques can you use to confirm your instructions were understood/heard correctly?   
3) How might this experience change the way you approach history taking?   
4) How would you address storage of medications in the future?   
5) What techniques seemed to work best in eliciting the history?   
6) What are some methods to determine what medication(s) may have been ingested if the caregiver is unable to name the medications in the home?

IX. Pilot Testing and Revisions

A. Pilot testing- This case was initially piloted to a small group of residents and students in August of 2009. Since the beginning, 26 residents and medical students have participated in this simulation case. Each case has 4-5 learners, with 1-2 pediatric residents serving as the leaders.

a. During pilot testing the following answers were given to the debriefing questions offered above:

i. What was your main challenge to getting the history from the caregiver? Most participants reported having a hard time getting direct answers from the caregiver and felt like they had difficulty maintaining the caregiver’s attention. Almost all the participants reported frustration.

ii. Most participants recognized that the caregiver had hearing impairment. When asked what techniques can be used to improve communication with people who are hearing impaired, many suggested speaking louder, while others suggested speaking more slowly.

iii. A few residents suggested using repeat back techniques to insure the caregiver correctly heard instructions.

iv. When asked how this experience would change their approach to history taking, residents thought they would be more conscious of the possibility of hearing impairment in historians.

v. How would address storage of medications in the future? In most case, we would have the residents role play providing anticipatory guidance to the grandparent actor during debriefing. Some residents appeared very uncomfortable in this role, while others took to it enthusiastically. During the role playing the grandparent often named barriers to safely storing medications, such as forgetting to take hidden medications or difficulties opening containers. The residents were encouraged to explore acceptable alternatives, such as daily alarm reminders or child proof storage boxes with the grandparents.

vi. When asked what techniques worked best when obtaining the history? The residents often correctly noted that the historian responded best when the resident was seated across from them and when the resident offered encouragement, such as a light touch or attentive listening techniques. The actors had been trained to respond best when the resident sat across from them and maintained eye contact.

vii. When asked how they could determine what medications were in the home, a few residents thought about calling the caregiver’s pharmacy to find out what medications were in the home. In addition, one could call the caregiver’s medical providers to obtain a medication list, although this option may be time prohibitive.

B. Performance Expectations and Management Mistakes- The performance expectations are that the residents will be able to overcome communication gaps in order to effectively obtain a history from a caregiver with age related hearing loss. Since residents do not have to make medical decisions in this case there are no management mistakes per se; instead the residents may give up on obtaining a history when it proves difficult. As stated earlier, the case may proceed more smoothly if the participants are made aware that they only need to obtain a history during the exercise.

C. Evaluation form - please see Feedback tool. Evaluation form may be completed by an observer watching the case in real time or by video review if case is taped. A Participant Evaluation form is also provided for participants to evaluate the case.
