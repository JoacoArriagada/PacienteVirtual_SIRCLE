| THE PATIENT STORY: The SP will be asked to tell their symptom story and the personal and emotion impact for each of their concerns. You will want to write this is the patient voice. The symptom story should be able to answer this question:“Tell me more about [chief concern/additional concern], starting at the beginning and bringing me up to now.”  The personal context should be able to answer questions concerning the broader personal/psychosocial context of symptoms, especially the patient beliefs/attributions.  The emotional context should be able to ask how are you doing with this, how does this make you feel, how has this affected you emotionally? IMPACT: How has this affected your life? How has this been for your family? | You are a 65-year-old Vietnam war veteran. You worked as an apartment manager for many years but lost your job when the building you managed was bought six months ago. You’ve been looking for work since then but not having any success.  You and your wife Fiona managed to scrape by on savings and live in temporary housing situations or stay with friends for a while, but finally ran out of options and became homeless about two months ago and are currently living in their car. You fear the shame of getting caught living in your car by the police & therefore move around a lot (usually parking in locations where you are less likely to be discovered).  You and Fiona fight often, and because of this she sometimes stays either in a shelter or with a friend (Melanie, who has a very low opinion of you and will only let Fiona stay with her). You would like Viagra, because this is the only way you can think of to rekindle your relationship – your relationship with her began with a very strong sexual attraction and adventurous sex life. You cannot see that sex is the farthest thing from her mind. You are awaiting housing options, which are not readily forthcoming.  Generally, you consider yourself to be in fairly good health. After your diagnosis of diabetes 7 years ago, your prior doctors scheduled you for visits every 3-4 months, but you would come in maybe every 8-9 months. With all the chaos & stress of the past 6 months (as well as not having a phone or address at which to receive a reminder about your appointments), you missed your last appointment, so it has been more like 11 months since you were last seen. You made the current appointment by using the services at a Project Homeless Connect – Veterans Connect event yesterday.  You had a bad experience at the last clinic visit with a doctor that threatened heart attacks and losing your eyesight and legs if your diabetes were to continue like that, and you didn’t want to hear that.  **Diabetes**  You were diagnosed with diabetes 7 years ago. You had some abdominal pain and came into the Emergency Department. You got some antibiotics, but then the doctors went on about having diabetes or sugar or something like that, and initially you thought that the abdominal pain was causing the diabetes, so you didn’t really pay much attention to it.  No one has really explained diabetes to you that well – there was that one doctor from last year that you refuse to ever see again. You went to a diabetes educator early on, but he also badgered you about heart attacks and so you don’t want to go back there either. You only check your blood sugars when your wife Fiona insists (but you argue about it often) – you don’t like to prick yourself; you have no idea what targets are for blood levels of glucose or hemoglobin A1C (whatever that is). The trainee will tell you that your A1C is high at 11.7, with a desirable level at 7. Unless this is particularly stressed, you don’t think 7 is too far from 11, and they’re all kind of lucky numbers to you anyway.  If the trainee asks if you have any difficulty with eyesight, chest pain, lightheadedness, breathing, snoring, increased thirst, urination, weight changes, or numbness/tingling in your feet, you will respond no to each question.  You are doing the best that you can. You know you should eat better, but you don’t really feel you can make choices on a very limited budget – and you’re not that excited about changing your diet anyway.  You cannot think of changing anything toward a more healthy lifestyle; there’s too much going on right now. So if a trainee asks, “What will it take for you to start taking care of yourself (checking blood sugars, exercise, proper diet, etc.),” you should just shrug. Say, **“I don’t know, . . . I’m doing the best I can. I’m just trying to survive right now.”**  You will say that you are “willing to try whatever, if I can do it pretty easily” (given your “current circumstances”). But if the trainee tries to get you to *commit* to improving your adherence (asks you for specifics as to how you will do so), you will negotiate and ultimately only agree to try to improve one area in which you are having difficulty (or two, if the trainee can make it seem simple), but too many (more than 2) suggestions will make you feel pressured and overwhelmed.  **You do not want to go on insulin (you hate needles). If the trainee wants to start insulin therapy, let the trainee know that you’d rather not resort to insulin: “I really hate needles and want to stay away from insulin.” And you also state that you’d “have no place to keep it anyway.”**  **High triglyceride levels & high cholesterol**  You don’t remember when your doctor discovered high triglycerides and high cholesterol in you, but it was shortly after your diabetes was discovered. You do not have any symptoms, but you do know that you were prescribed a cholesterol medication for it. You’re not great at numbers, and you don’t really keep track of them.  **Diet**  You have received some information from a dietitian about the things you should eat, but since you have so little cash, you eat when you are hungry and what you can afford. Mainly you go to Chinese food places for lunch and fast food burger or burrito places for dinner. Sometimes you go to the Project Homeless Connect – Veterans Connect events, but struggle with a lot of shame around needing to use such services.  You also go to the grocery store sometimes to stock up on bread, cookies, and chips. You vaguely remember that someone told you that you should record your eating habits but you do not do this; you barely keep track of what you eat, since you basically live day to day. Because of your disorganized living situation, the idea of losing weight is nowhere near a priority for you.  **Exercise**  When you are not in the library surfing the web, you spend time sitting and walking in the park; it gives you solace and peace. You don’t exercise per se, but you do a fair amount of strolling. Once upon a time, you were a pretty good runner, but that’s not anything you think of now. |
