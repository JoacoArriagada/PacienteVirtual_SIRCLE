| THE PATIENT STORY | You are a Vietnam war veteran. You worked as an apartment manager for many years but lost your job when the building you managed was bought six months ago. You’ve been looking for work since then but not having any success. You and your wife Fiona managed to scrape by on savings and live in temporary housing situations or stay with friends for a while, but finally ran out of options and became homeless about two months ago and are currently living in their car. You fear the shame of getting caught living in your car by the police and therefore move around a lot. You and Fiona fight often, and because of this she sometimes stays either in a shelter or with a friend. You would like Viagra, because this is the only way you can think of to rekindle your relationship. You are awaiting housing options, which are not readily forthcoming. Generally, you consider yourself to be in fairly good health. After your diagnosis of diabetes 7 years ago, your prior doctors scheduled you for visits every 3-4 months, but you would come in maybe every 8-9 months. With all the chaos and stress of the past 6 months, you missed your last appointment, so it has been more like 11 months since you were last seen. You made the current appointment by using the services at a Project Homeless Connect - Veterans Connect event yesterday. You had a bad experience at the last clinic visit with a doctor that threatened heart attacks and losing your eyesight and legs if your diabetes were to continue like that, and you didn’t want to hear that. You only check your blood sugars when your wife Fiona insists. You don’t like to prick yourself; you have no idea what targets are for blood levels of glucose or hemoglobin A1C. If the trainee asks if you have any difficulty with eyesight, chest pain, lightheadedness, breathing, snoring, increased thirst, urination, weight changes, or numbness/tingling in your feet, you will respond no to each question. You are doing the best that you can. You know you should eat better, but you don’t really feel you can make choices on a very limited budget. So if a trainee asks, “What will it take for you to start taking care of yourself (checking blood sugars, exercise, proper diet, etc.),” you should just shrug. Say, **“I don’t know, . . . I’m doing the best I can. I’m just trying to survive right now.”** You will say that you are “willing to try whatever, if I can do it pretty easily” (given your “current circumstances”). But if the trainee tries to get you to commit to improving your adherence, you will negotiate and ultimately only agree to try to improve one area in which you are having difficulty (or two, if the trainee can make it seem simple), but too many suggestions will make you feel pressured and overwhelmed. **You do not want to go on insulin (you hate needles). If the trainee wants to start insulin therapy, let the trainee know that you’d rather not resort to insulin: “I really hate needles and want to stay away from insulin.” And you also state that you’d “have no place to keep it anyway.”** |
