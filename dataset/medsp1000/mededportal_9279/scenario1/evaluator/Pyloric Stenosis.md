Case Title: Pyloric Stenosis

Primary Learning Objectives: key learning objectives of the scenario

1. Recognize the history and physical exam findings of pyloric stenosis

2. Construct a thorough differential diagnosis for vomiting in an infant

3. Identify and manage aspiration

4. Identify and manage pyloric stenosis

Secondary Learning Objectives:

1. Review diagnosis and treatment for aspiration

2. Review diagnosis and treatment for pyloric stenosis

**CRITICAL ACTIONS**

**Scenario branch points/ PLAY OF CASE GUIDELINES**

1. **Place on monitor and obtain intraosseous access after failure to obtain venous access.**

* Once monitor is in place, the initial vital signs are unchanged.
* The nurse states: ”IV access is difficult” after 2 attempts.
* Physician or medic obtains intraosseous access (if directed by team leader).
  + If IO access is attempted, parents become upset at multiple attempts to stick child.
  + Explain necessity of intravenous/intraosseous access due to dehydration to parents in order to calm them down.
* Bolus NS at 20 cc/kg.
  + If fluids are given, vital signs: BP 88/60, HR 165, RR 30, T98.9, 98% RA
  + If fluids are not given, vital signs unchanged.

1. **Perform suction of airway after patient aspirates on vomit and becomes hypoxic.**

* The patient begins to vomit approximately 3 to 4 ounces. The vomit is projectile, non-bloody and non-bilious. The patient then begins to choke and aspirates. SpO2 decreases to 91% RA.
  + If airway is suctioned and oxygen is given, improved O2 saturation.
  + If airway is not suctioned, O2 saturations continue to decrease until patient codes.
* Optional: Chest/KUB/Abdominal Xray ordered to evaluate aspiration.

1. **Recognize pyloric stenosis, order chemistry panel, imaging and consult surgery.**

* Ultrasound is ordered with findings consistent with pyloric stenosis.
* Learner should order a Chemistry Panel (Na+, K+, Cl-, CO2, BUN, Creatinine, and Glucose). A CBC and UA are also included if the learner is concerned for infection.
* Surgery is consulted.

**Debriefing Materials:**

**Evaluation of a vomiting infant:**

An emergency clinician must have a systematic approach in evaluation of any patient. This patient is an infant, so it is important to get a thorough history from the parents. The history of the vomiting in this case is the key to the diagnosis. This patient is the age range for a child with pyloric stenosis and he is male, meaning that he is four times more likely to have a diagnosis of pyloric stenosis than a female infant that is vomiting. The vomiting is explained as non-bilious, non-bloody and projectile in nature, occurring after every meal, which is consistent with an obstructive cause, such that happens with a thickened pyloric sphincter.

Other important diagnoses within the differential for a vomiting infant include malrotation with volvulus, gastroenteritis and gastro-esophageal reflux. The history is not consistent with malrotation with volvulus, as this would present with bilious vomit. Gastroenteritis should be included in the differential for this severely dehydrated child, but the vomit would generally not be projectile and the patient would usually also present with diarrhea. Gastro-esophageal reflux would present after every feeding and be non-projectile. The physical exam finding of an olive-like mass in the epigastrum of this male infant is also very significant to help the learner diagnose pyloric stenosis rather than other causes of vomiting in the infant.

A chemistry panel will show a hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. Diagnosis is of pyloric stenosis is made via ultrasound, showing a thickened pyloric muscle greater than 3mm or a long pyloric muscle greater than 1.4cm. When the diagnosis is made, the learner should consult surgery for evaluation and treatment.

**Pyloric Stenosis:**

Incidence: 1 in 250

4x as likely in males

Significance: #1 cause of obstruction in infants

Cause: Hypertrophy & hyperplasia of circular antrum and pyloric musculature causing gastric outlet obstruction

Effect: Hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis from vomiting

Symptoms: Non-bilious emesis in 2wk to 6 wk infants at end of feeding

Exam: Palpable “olive” mass just to the right of midline above the umbilicus

Diagnosis: US: Thickened pylorus greater than 0.3 cm

Elongated pylorus greater than 1.4 cm

97-100% sensitive, 99-100% specific

Xray: Imaging shows dilated gas-filled stomach, although this is not necessarily ordered to diagnose pyloric stenosis

**Evaluation of the severely dehydrated infant:**

It is essential that the learner pay attention to the physical exam findings of this patient. In simulation cases, it is more difficult to appreciate “sick” versus “not sick” based on appearance, since we are dealing with mannequin models. This patient’s vitals show that he is hypotensive and tachycardic from vomiting. His fontanelle is sunken in, further cluing the learner of his severe dehydration. While a UA is not necessarily obtained, it can help clue the learner that the patient is dehydrated with a high specific gravity. The patient’s parents also explain a change in behavior to include the patient being less interactive. The learner should take these clues and address dehydration early in the case, given the patient’s vital signs.

IV access is often difficult to obtain in a dehydrated patient, and would be expected to be difficult in this dehydrated infant. Intraosseous access should be obtained after 2-3 failed attempts of IV if vital signs include hypotension, indicating severe dehydration. The 20cc/kg bolus of fluids is essential in helping improve this patient’s vital signs.

**Intraosseous access:**

Access to the bone marrow is indicated when there is an emergency need for vascular access, and other central or peripheral sites are not easily cannulated. A case series of pediatric resuscitations demonstrated the mean time to manual IO placement was 4.7 minutes with a success rate of 83% compared with an average time to intravascular access of 7.9 minutes. More recent studies using newer devices have demonstrated insertion times of only seconds with success rates of 94%. Various insertion devices include simple hand-twist needles, hand-held drills, and spring-loaded devices.

Contraindications include: overlying infection, exposed bone, underlying fracture and/or structural bone disorders (e.g. osteogenesis imperfecta).

**Assessing aspiration:**

Patients who have a witnessed aspiration from regurgitation of a large volume of gastric contents should have prompt suctioning of the upper airway. Endotracheal tube placement should be considered, as should placement of an orogastric or nasogastric tube for gastric decompression. Patients who aspirate large volumes of solid material or tenacious material may require suctioning of the tracheobronchial tree or bronchoalveolar lavage to clear the airway. Bronchodilators may be required for aspiration-induced bronchospasm. Prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended, and there is no evidence that corticosteroids prevent lung injury.

Patients who appear stable but have risk factors for worsening or aggressive disease should be admitted to a hospital or an observation unit. Oxygen and, possibly, antibiotics should be administered, and the patient should be observed carefully for 12 to 24 hours.

While CXR can be ordered in the event of an aspiration, the resulting image will generally appear normal for several days after the aspiration has occurred, unless a copious amount of fluid/vomit has been aspirated. This case demonstrates a normal image, which would be expected immediately after aspiration.
