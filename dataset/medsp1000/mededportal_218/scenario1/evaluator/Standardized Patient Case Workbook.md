**Appropriate Learners:**         Upper level medical students or primary care residents

**The Purpose of the Case and Primary Learning Issue:**  

Primary care physicians need to learn about the epidemiology, phenomenology, relationship with other illnesses, and treatment of somatoform disorders.  These disorders are best thought of as “unexplained symptoms,” a class of illnesses that have changed in their presumed etiology over time.  Expressing general distress bodily is the norm worldwide, whereas psychologization of general distress is a variant born of western intellectual commitments to mind-body dualism.  Somatoform disorders in primary care are common, ranging from 0.8% to 14% of all visits.  More than half of the most common symptoms in primary care are never adequately explained by a biomedical paradigm alone.  Somatoform patients have abnormal body sensations and authentically come for help to understand and manage them.  They also have characteristics that are different from non-somatoform patients.  For example, hypochondriacal patients in primary care present with more pain and higher levels of psychological distress, have less consistent health maintenance, and more often present due to anxiety or fear than non-hypochondriacal patients.  They worry about their health and their emotional state, but they usually only share the somatic complaints with their doctor.  This is often assumed by doctors to be because of the patient’s somatization and lack of psychological mindedness, but a high number of patients report that they wish their doctor would talk with them about emotional and spiritual issues.  In primary somatoform disorders, the somatoform disorder is either the only condition of concern or, when there is co-morbidity, the somatoform disorder precedes the other disorder and is responsible for the unexplained physical symptoms.  In secondary forms, another disorder precedes the onset of the somatoform disorder and the unexplained physical symptoms are thought to be due to, or secondary to, the other illness.  Somatoform disorders may be secondary to other psychiatric or other medical illness.  For example, unexplained somatic symptoms are highly prevalent in anxiety and affective disorders and diminish significantly with adequate treatment of the anxiety or depressive disorder.  The presence of medical illness makes it more likely that a patient will develop a somatoform disorder.  Finally, somatoform disorders are often confused with factitious and malingering disorders.  Good pharmacological and psychosocial treatments exist for somatoform disorders.

**Formative feedback to learner:**

1. Recognition of primary and secondary forms of somatization disorders

2. Development of a helpful management approach for both patient and provider.

3. Understanding the differences between somatization disorder, factitious disorder, and malingering.

**Lessons Learned from our experience with this case:** 

We used this case primarily as a demonstration by a faculty member interacting with the SP and having learners observe the interview and exam process. We believe that the case is easily adapted for participation by the advanced medical student or resident to perform the history, examination, and create a management and treatment plan. Summative and formative evaluations are possible, using points from checklists that may be utilized for learner assessment.

**Case Scenario and Summary:**

A 35year old married patient with asthma presents to the office with complaints of dizziness, difficulty swallowing, nausea, bloating, back and leg pain, right shoulder pain, and headache. All of these complaints are not new and have been present since he was about 26 years of age with some waxing and waning. He was sickly as a child. Patient is worried that something is seriously wrong with him. He is willing to have the provider talk to family members. There is no litigation pending.
