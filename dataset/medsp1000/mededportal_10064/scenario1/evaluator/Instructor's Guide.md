**Case Instructor Guide: A Pediatric Death from Non-Accidental Trauma**

**Title:**  A Pediatric Death from Non-Accidental Trauma

(*Alternatively known as Kobayashi Maru)*

**Target Audience:** This case targets 3rd and 4th year medical students, emergency medicine residents, faculty, emergency medicine nurses, and adjunct EM staff.

**Purpose:**

All learners:

The purpose of this case is to expose residents to experience a rare but important emergency department scenario: a pediatric death. Being an extremely uncommon scenario, the main goal of this case is to provide a forum where learners can practice the skills needed to navigate through such an emotionally charged event. Beyond the death, the case has several dimensions, and the instructor may choose to emphasize any individual dimension.

As is commonplace in emergency department encounters, considerable tension exists between the medical management and family expectations in the case. This is deliberate. Instructors should emphasize the medical management pearls for more junior learners (medical students, junior residents). For advanced learners (senior residents and faculty), instructors are encouraged to emphasize the management of the family's emotions and expectations.

Essential to the management of the case is the identification of the critically ill child. The SIM participants must identify a hypovolemic critically ill child and begin timely resuscitative efforts. Opportunities to observe their knowledge of Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) and Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) are present in the case. Additionally, interdisciplinary communication, team-based communication, and patient-centered care can be rehearsed as well.

Advanced learners:

Advanced learners should be encouraged to develop their sense of when further resuscitation attempts are futile, and how to navigate the difficult decision to end resuscitative efforts in a child. There are many compelling reasons for physicians to continue CPR in a child: physician beneficence towards a young patient, family encouragement, fear of legal consequences, among others. While there is no correct answer, the case provides learners with an opportunity to define their threshold of medical futility.

Participants in this case are given the opportunity to manage the emotionally charged parent reactions to the death of their child. Learners are prompted to develop their knowledge about the options available to engage parents in the resuscitation of a child. The option of having parents at bedside during the code is not universally trusted or practiced, but may be an important means of providing information and closure to the parents of the patient.

The recognition of child abuse is another important skill to practice. The diagnosis relies on the presence and possible absence of historical element combined with physical exam findings. In this case, advanced learners are provided the opportunity to synthesize this differential diagnostic possibility in this case.

**Educational Objectives:**

1. Organize a patient care team.
2. Recognize a critically-ill child and begin timely resuscitative efforts.
3. Identify suspected signs of child abuse.
4. Treat and manage a critically ill child using ATLS/PALS guidelines (hemothorax, pelvic fracture, epidural hematoma, pulseless electrical activity).
5. Understand the options to engage parents in the resuscitation of a child.
6. Develop recognition of when further resuscitation attempts are futile.
7. Manage social dynamics resulting from the death of a child.

**Conceptual Background**:This case was created as part of our Emergency Medicine Resident Simulation Curriculum. The case is based upon a composite of cases seen in the ED: pediatric trauma cases, child abuse presentations, and the most difficult of all - pediatric deaths in the ED. The primary objective of this simulation case is to expose learners to this (thankfully) rare patient encounter: the death of a pediatric patient.

During residency training, it is uncommon for any resident to encounter this patient event. However, over our residents' career, this is a scenario that most of them will likely experience. The case was developed to address the need to train our learners in the nuanced skills needed to navigate this difficult patient encounter within a safe, non-medico-legal, inconsequential environment.

As written, the case attributes the traumatic injuries to child abuse by the step-father. Recognition of abuse is an important skill, and we included it to provide advanced learners an opportunity to interpret the history, and physical exam findings, and synthesize the possibility of child abuse. This particular element can be omitted for teams composed of more junior learners, where the instructor would want to simply emphasize the resuscitation attempt and pediatric death. The injury mechanism can be replaced by a fall out of a tree (or other desired mechanism). However, the cost of doing this is that the patient will not have as vague a presentation, and the nature and severity of the patient's injuries will be more recognizable. This will work for junior learners, but may not create the same buy-in from more senior learners.

Beyond these points, this case provides a forum where learners are expected to identify a critically ill patient, and practice resuscitation using ACLS and ATLS protocols. These are essential skills that we expect our residents to know well, regardless of level of training. Early identification of gaps in knowledge can be identified here, and specific feedback given.

In summation, the simulation creates an environment where learners are able to navigate number of complex psycho-social issues encounter during emergency department shifts. The case is robust enough to allow for the adjustment of its complexity to align with the learner level.

**Implementation:**

1. Case Length - approximately 15-20 minutes.
2. Case Debriefing should be approximately 30 - 40 minutes to allow participants and observers to express their thoughts and opinions.
3. Case debriefing suggested as follows
4. Initial reactions?
   1. This simple question dissipates the tension that is created by emotional simulation cases.
   2. This will be an important part of the debriefing session, as there are few simulations with issues as charged as a pediatric death.
   3. Let the group express their concerns and frustration, paving the way to a rational discussion of the issues.
5. Give a 2-3 minute synopsis of case to bring everyone to the same page.
6. Use "I saw...", "I heard...", "I noticed..." to preface concrete observations, followed by an inquiry about the observations noted.
7. Ask the learner their intentions by performing the observed action.
8. Allow further discussion about the objective stimulus observed and the learner's intent.
9. Specific questions related to this case can be found in the Kobayashi Maru case write up on page 7-8 in Section VII, "Debriefing Plan".

**Deployment:** This case was run several times as part of our simulation education training during our resident conferences. Teams are selected beforehand, and know that they will be performing in a simulation on the assigned day. No prior information about the case was divulged to team members or the audience before the case begins.

Prior to implementing the case, the faculty, academic chief residents, and technician rehearsed the case several times. In these rehearsal sessions, we played out a variety of pathways driven by potential participant choices. Each team member had a printout of the Kobayashi Maru case flow diagram (Page 5). The day of the case, we recruited two medical students to play the mother and grandmother. They were coached in terms of their grief reactions.

The teams were comprised of four trainees (PGY-3, PGY-2, PGY-1, MS-4, with the team being lead by the upper level resident. One faculty member had the sole responsibility of observing the training scenario. One faculty and one academic chief resident played roles in the case (Nurse, EMS), and also served as backup to our technician and stimulus presenter. Our second academic chief ran the PowerPoint slides that projected the case stimuli and displayed the decorticate posturing during the case. Our technician controlled the Laerdal SimMan(R).

We recommend at minimum one faculty member have the sole role of observer with no technical responsibilities to capture the nuances of the team's medical and ethical choices. The debrief session should be led by the faculty with knowledge of the case construct and without responsibilities during the case enactment.

**Limitations:**

The case is complex. It requires rehearsal and preparation beforehand, and is not easily implemented on a whim. At minimum it requires 3 confederates, a technician, someone to project the stimuli, and a faculty observer. Still, given the paucity of real pediatric death experiences that the residents are expected to be part of, we believe this an important enough simulation to invest the resources required.

The case is highly charged and, on occasion, may provoke some emotional reactions in response to the pediatric death. During the debrief session, we find it essential to allow the participants and audience to vent their concerns about the case. We have seen varying reactions that are often tied to individual beliefs, and it is important to bring those reactions to the discussion. If this reaction discussion extends beyond a reasonable time limit of 5-10 minutes without a movement towards the issues the case was designed to address, we offer to "move on" after recognizing and explicitly stating the disparity of perspectives.

There are case nuances - trainees may focus on the historical aspects of the case to try to sleuth out the cause of the injuries. It is important to know that the case can be moved forward through a decline in patient vitals. We have our technician put the vitals on a timeline of decline to ensure the case moves forward appropriately.
