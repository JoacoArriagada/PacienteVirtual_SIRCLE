**Case: Dementia**

**(Geriatric Content: Caregiver, Family)**

**ACGME Competencies: Pt. Care, Medical Knowledge, Systems Based Practice, Communication**

Writer: Foley (October 2010)

Mr. V. is an 84 year old man who has been referred for a “dementia consult”. He is accompanied by his 86 year old wife who has been his sole caregiver since he began to experience memory decline 4 years ago. Mr. V.’s initial symptoms were frequent repeating and not being able to remember recent events and conversations. Over the past year, he stopped managing the couple’s finances – on his wife’s urging - due to mistakes that he made with paying bills. His wife must remind him to change his clothes and shave; otherwise, he is independent in ADL’s. He continues to drive and has told his family that he is “not going to stop”, even though he hit a neighbor’s mailbox and ran a stop sign one month ago. The couple has three children, two of whom live out of state. A daughter lives 10 miles from their home and visits them once a week. Mrs. V. admits to being under “some” stress in her role as a caregiver and she is reluctant to ask her daughter for help because she does not want to be a burden to her. Mr. V.’s primary care physician started him on Aricept and Namenda 6 months ago following an in-office MMSE on which he scored 22/30 points. A CMP, CBC, TSH, B12 level, RPR, and non-contrast CT were performed and all results were normal with exception of atrophy on the CT scan. No diagnosis other than “probable dementia” was rendered. Mr. V. was not told why the consultation was requested by his PCP, nor did his wife tell him the reason for the appointment. Before entering the exam room, Mrs. V. passed a handwritten note to the medical assistant that reads, “Doctor: I don’t know how much longer I can care for my husband. I need to know what is going on with his memory and what to expect.”

**Premise:** There are multiple issues to address during the appointment. Specifically, the clinician may need to: 1) create a priority list and plan as to how each issue or problem will be further evaluated and managed, 2) determine how to spend the time allotted for the consultation, which should include a cognitive evaluation and a neurological examination and 3) appropriately use members from the interdisciplinary team (medical social work/nursing). At the conclusion of the visit, a comprehensive plan should be outlined for Mrs. V.

**Ideally, the fellow should address**: 1) the dementia diagnosis, 2) the treatment regimen, 3) the wife’s needs as a caregiver, 4) community resources that would benefit Mr. V., 5) Mr. V.’s current living situation and alternatives, 6) Mr. V.’s driving, 7) Communication with the children, 8) Follow-up plan. The fellow will need to effectively communicate with the patient, who does not know why he is there, and demonstrate the ability to direct interdisciplinary team members.

**Examples of Abnormal Responses to Mental Status Exam Questions**

* Current President – “Carter”
  + Before him? – “not sure”
* # States in USA – “48”
* Add pocket change (quarter + dime + nickel + penny) = “40”
* Similarities of common objects – start off providing similarities, then progress to describe differences
  + Orange and tennis ball – “round”
  + Pine tree and pencil – “straight” or “wood”
  + Tricycle and airplane – “one flies, but the other doesn’t”
* Days of week in reverse order
  + Hesitate in response, then “Friday, Thursday, Wednesday. . . Friday, Saturday, Sunday, right?”
* Months of year in reverse order
  + Hesitate in response, then “December, November. . . this is silly.”
