**School Difficulties: Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorders, Learning Disorders, and Anxiety**

***Facilitator Guide***

**Learning Objectives:**

1. To describe the importance of identifying school difficulties in children
2. To differentiate among common Attention Deficit Hyperactive Disorder (ADHD), Learning Disorder (LD), and Anxiety symptoms in children
3. To discuss the screeners that can be easily used in primary care and their scoring
4. To provide appropriate recommendations for families of children with school difficulties

**Main Points:**

1. Attentional, emotional, and behavioral difficulties in school are important to identify early
   1. Mitigate child and family stress
   2. Reduce likelihood of co-morbid health issues
      * Children with ADHD are 2x as likely as children without ADHD to have another mental disorder
   3. Academic achievement, social development, self-esteem
2. Primary care pediatricians are uniquely situated to help, given their ongoing relationships and frequent contact with children and families
   1. Screening and diagnostic clarification
   2. Initiating treatment for simple, straightforward ADHD
   3. Recommendations and assistance securing additional services
      * Educating and empowering parents
      * School-based support: psycho-educational testing, IEP, 504 plan
      * Refer for mental health treatment

**Case 1:** *Ask a resident to read the case out loud*

Jaden is a 7 year-old boy who presents to the clinic with his mother. She explains she’s been getting calls almost every day from his teacher complaining he is not completing his work, is running around the classroom, and “talking too much.” His mother states at home he has a lot of energy, but she doesn’t think he has trouble focusing because he can play on the iPad for hours. She does admit she has to ask him up to 5 times to follow a simple direction and that moves constantly from one activity to another. Lastly, his mother is exhausted and claims it can take Jaden up to 3 hours to fall asleep at night and is restless throughout the night, so no one in the family is getting enough rest.

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What further information is helpful to obtain?” If no one knows, prompt the residents to think about medical etiologies of his symptoms, as well as external stressors.*

**A**: 1. How long has he had the symptoms of trouble focusing or increased activity?

2. Did he ever have elevated lead?

3. What is his sleep schedule? History of sleep dysregulation or snoring?

4. Any recent stress at home?

5. Did the mother receive similar reports from his previous teachers?

6. Does the mother believe he understands and is capable of doing the school work?

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What screening tools would be helpful in this situation?”*

**A**: Screening tool (SNAP or Vanderbilt for mother and teacher)

*These are ADHD-specific screening tools. SNAP stands for the Swanson, Nolan and Pelham scale and is comprised of 18 questions (9 regarding inattention and 9 regarding hyperactivity/impulsivity).*

*Pull up SNAP or Vanderbilt tool for residents to view, use SNAP or Vanderbilt scoring tool*

**Q:** *Ask the residents: “Who fills out the SNAP or the Vanderbilt?*”

**A:** The main teacher (or teachers if they have multiple types of classes) and the parent

* + Encourage the mother to send in the screener and request it back before the next appointment, and to get any additional information from the teacher she can (report card, progress notes, how Jaden socializes, etc.)

*Ask a resident to read this additional information below out loud*

Jaden and his mother return 2 weeks later. At the end of the last visit she explained she’s gotten reports from teachers about his silly and sometimes disruptive behavior since preschool, but she believes in the past they “worked with Jaden.” She also said she knows he is capable of doing the work, because when she sits with him one-on-one and goes through each question of his homework, he answers the questions correctly. She says he has lots of friends but gets very frustrated when things don’t go exactly how he wants when they play. Today, she presents her and the teacher’s SNAP with the following scores:

Teacher SNAP: Inattention = 2.67, Hyper/Impulsive = 1.88, Combined = 2.28

Parent SNAP: Inattention = 1.88, Hyper/Impulsive = 1.67, Combined = 1.78

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What is an appropriate diagnosis for Jaden?”*

**A**: ADHD, Combined Type

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What are the differences between ADHD, combined type; ADHD, inattentive type,*

*and ADHD, impulsive/hyperactive type?*

**A**: ADHD, combined type is the most common type of ADHD. The person will show impulsive and hyperactive behavior, as well as getting distracted easily and struggling to maintain attention.

People with ADHD, predominantly inattentive type will get distracted easily and find it difficult to pay attention.

ADHD, predominantly impulsive/hyperactive is the least common type. People with this will show signs of hyperactivity and the need to move constantly, as well as displaying impulsive behavior.

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “How many different settings must ADHD symptoms be noticed in for it to be diagnosed?”*

**A:** Two or more

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What treatment options would you recommend?” If no one knows, prompt the resident to think about both medication and therapy options.*

**A**: A stimulant medication trial +/- a referral for parent management training, behavior therapy, and/or social skills training

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What can be done in school whether or not a parent is open to medication?”*

**A**: If academic impairments are noted then a request for IEP (individualized education program)

evaluation should be requested

**Anticipatory Guidance**:

1. Simple ADHD can be managed in primary care
   1. Behavioral changes at home may be helpful—good sleep hygiene, adequate physical activity, structure and routines, and providing clear, simple directions for child to follow
   2. Stimulant medication can be very effective, and often symptoms can be stabilized with no or minimal side effects
2. If children are having functional impairments in academic, social, or emotional functioning, treatment is indicated

**Additional Points:** *these can be integrated into above discussion points or provided later*

1. ADHD prevalence rates range from 2-14% of children, with boys being 3-4x more likely to be diagnosed
   1. Both genetic and environmental factors are implicated
   2. Neurochemical (Dopamine) abnormalities in the pre-frontal cortex
2. If untreated, ADHD can lead to academic failure, social isolation, and antisocial behavior
   1. Co-morbid anxiety and depressive disorders
   2. Substance abuse disorders
3. Treatment options
   1. Psychoeducation regarding chronicity of ADHD, common developmental challenges, and general advice to promote improved academic and behavioral functioning
   2. Psychosocial interventions for mild ADHD, families opposed to medication treatment, or as an adjunct to medication treatment
      * Parent management training
      * Behavior therapy
      * Parent child interactive therapy
      * Incredible years group therapy
      * Social skills training
   3. Psychopharmacologic interventions
      * Should be considered for children >/= 5 years with impaired functioning
      * 75% effective, and most children can be stabilized without side effects
      * Often enable child to benefit from psychosocial interventions
      * Prescription stimulant use continues to be the first-line psychopharmacological treatment for childhood ADHD in clinical guidelines. In a meta-analysis looking at the comparative efficacy and tolerability of oral medications for ADHD, based on efficacy and safety, methylphenidate is most strongly supported in children and adolescents (Cortese et al, 2018; Myer et al, 2018)
      * Some of the most common side effects to be aware of from psychopharmacology are decreased appetite, impacted sleep, and/or irritability (Stahl, 2019).
