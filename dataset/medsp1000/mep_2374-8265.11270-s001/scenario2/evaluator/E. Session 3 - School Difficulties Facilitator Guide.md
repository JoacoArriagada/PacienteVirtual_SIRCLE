**School Difficulties: Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorders, Learning Disorders, and Anxiety**

***Facilitator Guide***

**Learning Objectives:**

1. To describe the importance of identifying school difficulties in children
2. To differentiate among common Attention Deficit Hyperactive Disorder (ADHD), Learning Disorder (LD), and Anxiety symptoms in children
3. To discuss the screeners that can be easily used in primary care and their scoring
4. To provide appropriate recommendations for families of children with school difficulties

**Main Points:**

1. Attentional, emotional, and behavioral difficulties in school are important to identify early
   1. Mitigate child and family stress
   2. Reduce likelihood of co-morbid health issues
      * Children with ADHD are 2x as likely as children without ADHD to have another mental disorder
   3. Academic achievement, social development, self-esteem
2. Primary care pediatricians are uniquely situated to help, given their ongoing relationships and frequent contact with children and families
   1. Screening and diagnostic clarification
   2. Initiating treatment for simple, straightforward ADHD
   3. Recommendations and assistance securing additional services
      * Educating and empowering parents
      * School-based support: psycho-educational testing, IEP, 504 plan
      * Refer for mental health treatment

**Case 2:** *Ask a resident to read the case out loud*

Matthew is an 8 y/o boy presenting to the clinic with his mother who expresses concern that he is not doing well in school this year. His mother explains that he is acting out more and wants him to be evaluated for ADHD before he is held back. When you ask how Matthew has done in school prior to this year, his mother tells you that Matthew has always been a little behind his classmates but now his behavior is more of a problem.

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What further information is helpful to obtain during this visit?”*

**A**: 1. Educational history (psycho-educational testing in school, past or present IEP, grade

retentions, attendance)

1. ADHD symptoms (parent interview, SNAP)
2. Developmental history (language, early schooling and learning)
3. Home environment (structure of homework time, bedtime, recent stressors)
4. Recent changes (any recent external changes before this started)
5. Family history: LD, ADHD, parents’ education
6. History of bullying
7. Prior IEP (Individualized education program) evaluation and services like speech therapy in school.

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What additional information would be helpful to obtain before/at the next visit?”*

**A**: 1. Collateral from school: report card, SNAP/Vanderbilt (teacher)

2. Rule out hearing or vision impairment

3. What interventions have been effective or ineffective?

*Ask the resident to read the additional information out loud.*

Hearing and vision screens are WNL. The teacher’s progress notes describe disruptive behavior, mostly when students are assigned independent reading. She describes concerns re: Matthew’s reading level, noting regular grammatical/spelling errors and switching words around. His teacher says he does best with 1:1 support and recommends more 1:1 reading practice at home. Otherwise, the teacher has no other behavioral concerns and her SNAP rating form is not significant. He is said to get along with his peers.

**Report card:**
Reading: 1s Science: 2s and 3s
Writing: 1s Social Studies: 1s and 2s
Mathematics: 3s Phys Ed: 3s

Arts: 4’s

Matthew is said to have had a speech delay, but he didn’t receive early intervention services. His mother remembers he had trouble with saying and writing his ABC’s. She doesn’t recall when he started reading.

Regarding school, Matthew’s mother explains he was almost retained last year but his teacher spent 1:1 time with him and “somehow they let him pass.” She is not sure what his grades are now but his teacher has expressed concern. She explains homework time is a struggle, especially reading, about which he says “he already did it” or he puts up a fight. Matthew is observed as compliant with his mother’s directions during the visit. Parent interview re: ADHD symptoms denotes inattentive behavior, though her examples suggest impairment only in the context of schoolwork.

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What is a likely diagnosis for Matthew?***”** *If the residents are unsure, prompt them by asking what they are most concerned about from the stem.* **A**: Learning Disorder (LD) – Reading

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What interventions would you implement at this time?” If no one knows, prompt residents by asking what they typically do for children at this age with concern for developmental delay or learning difficulty?*

**A:** 1. Educate parents on their role in advocating for their child, LD, educational rights
2. Assist parent in drafting letter requesting psychoeducational testing through the Committee for Special Education (CSE)

3. Write letter of support for CSE testing which parent can submit with the parent letter
4. F/U within 6 weeks + SW referral for families that may need additional support

**Q:** *Ask residents how they would give the information to the parents and what they would do next?*

**A:** We can support you, but parents are the best advocates for their children

Bring the letter requesting a CSE evaluation in person to the school

* + 1. Encourage parent to get receipt from school when submitting letter to school
    2. Follow-up on the request if you have not received information within one month
    3. While awaiting the evaluation, encourage and help your child with his or her work but try to minimize the frustration and conflict at home

**Additional Points:** *these can be integrated into above discussion points or provided later*

1. Lifetime prevalence of reported specific LD in children (US): 9.7% (likely higher than this)
   1. Disorders of reading are most common (80% of LD identified individuals)
   2. Higher incidence of LD in males
   3. Prevalence of LD higher among children at or below poverty line
   4. LD is twice as common in children with chronic health conditions
2. Early identification by pediatrician can improve long-term outcomes
   1. Risk factors: prematurity, low birth weight, chronic medical condition, poverty, family history of LD/language disorder, parents who do not read
   2. Track developmental milestones: language acquisition, phonological skills, early learning skills
   3. Early intervention programs (<age 5) shown as a strong predictor of future health status
3. Intervention: Education and Advocacy
   1. Reinforce importance of parent’s role in child’s learning and education
      * Encourage parents to read to their children every day starting at birth
      * De-stigmatize special education services
      * Empower family to identify diagnosis and access appropriate services
   2. Collaborate with the school or connect family with an educational advocate
4. High comorbidity of ADHD/LD
   1. LD may present as inattentive behavior because of limited ability, frustration, or disinterest
   2. Inattention identified in individuals with LD without ADHD is not impairing in other domains
   3. If evident ADHD, treat first – this may also allow for more accurate educational testing
