**School Difficulties: Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorders, Learning Disorders, and Anxiety**

***Facilitator Guide***

**Learning Objectives:**

1. To describe the importance of identifying school difficulties in children
2. To differentiate among common Attention Deficit Hyperactive Disorder (ADHD), Learning Disorder (LD), and Anxiety symptoms in children
3. To discuss the screeners that can be easily used in primary care and their scoring
4. To provide appropriate recommendations for families of children with school difficulties

**Main Points:**

1. Attentional, emotional, and behavioral difficulties in school are important to identify early
   1. Mitigate child and family stress
   2. Reduce likelihood of co-morbid health issues
      * Children with ADHD are 2x as likely as children without ADHD to have another mental disorder
   3. Academic achievement, social development, self-esteem
2. Primary care pediatricians are uniquely situated to help, given their ongoing relationships and frequent contact with children and families
   1. Screening and diagnostic clarification
   2. Initiating treatment for simple, straightforward ADHD
   3. Recommendations and assistance securing additional services
      * Educating and empowering parents
      * School-based support: psycho-educational testing, IEP, 504 plan
      * Refer for mental health treatment

**Case 3:** *Ask a resident to read the case out loud* **Q**: *Ask the residents: “What further information is helpful to obtain?”*

Ms. V brings 10 y/o Maya in for her WCC. Ms V. shares with you that Maya’s grades are dropping and she’s “not focusing.” Maya is in 5th grade in a regular education setting. When you ask Maya about school, she looks to her mother to answer. Her mother explains her teacher is concerned that she is avoiding doing work, frequently complaining of a stomachache and asking to go to the bathroom or the nurse. Her teacher also notes that Maya is taking a long time with her assignments, often erasing and rewriting her answers. At home, Maya avoids starting her HW and when prompted by her parents, she argues with them or “shuts down.” Ms. V says she and Mr. V have started sitting with Maya or reviewing her work when she is done; if they attempt to help her or correct her work, Maya will yell or cry. Ms. V is also concerned because Maya is now saying she isn’t smart. During this conversation, Maya is quiet and looks down at the floor.

**A**: 1. When did you first start noticing these changes?

2. Have there been any changes in Maya’s sleep or appetite?
3. Have there been any other changes at home or in school?

4. Family history: Anxiety, ADHD; family education history

5. Academic functioning: Current grades vs. past grades – is there a decline?

**Q:** *Ask the residents: “What screener would be useful with this patient?”*

**A:** The SCARED for parent and child

*Pull up attached SCARED tool to view with residents*

* SCARED score of greater than 25 may indicate an anxiety disorder
* SCARED questionnaire assesses panic disorder, somatic symptoms, generalized anxiety disorder, separation anxiety, social anxiety, and school avoidance

*Ask a resident to read the following information out loud*

Ms V. reports Maya has generally done well in school, but her grades have declined this year. Ms V. acknowledges Maya is getting more work and has started to talk about the end of the year tests, what middle school she’ll go to, and even what high school she’ll go to. Ms. V goes on to explain how important these tests are and presents as anxious herself. She shares how excited they are that Maya will be the first in their family to go to college. When prompted re: mood, Maya reports she feels “good.” Ms V. denies Maya appearing sad or depressed but acknowledges that she is more irritable and cries more often, noting “she was a happier kid this summer.” Ms. V also states Maya is having difficulty falling asleep and seems very tired in the morning. She has also seen Maya keep to herself more and spend less time with her friends, which the teacher noticed as well. Ms V. can’t think of any other major changes in the last year at home/school. The SCARED is scored a 30.

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “With this information, what primary diagnosis might you consider for Maya?”*

**A:** The parent SCARED form is significant for anxiety since it is above 25, so anxiety (Unspecified anxiety disorder, with need further evaluation)

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What interventions/treatment options would you recommend?” If no one knows the answer, prompt the residents by having them consider both medications and therapy.*
 **A**: 1. Consider referral for further evaluation and therapy

2. Identify support for the child in school (counselor, teacher)

3. Consider SSRI trial, in collaboration with or while awaiting additional treatment

**Q:** *Ask the residents: “When should anxiety be evaluated/treated?*” *If no one knows, prompt the residents by asking about reasons for treatment of other similar diagnoses?*

**A:** Anxiety is common and can be episodic in children, but warrants further evaluation and treatment if impairing

**Q:** *Ask the residents: “How can anxiety present in children in a physical way?*”

**A:** Physical manifestations of anxiety are very common in children, including sleep problems, headaches, stomach-aches, and other somatic symptoms

**Q:** *Ask the residents: “What are some common SSRIs used for anxiety?”*

**A**: Escitalopram (Lexapro); sertraline (Zoloft); fluoxetine (Prozac); fluvoxamine (Luvox); and citalopram (Celexa); and paroxetine (Paxil)

**Q**: *Ask the residents: “What is the black box warning for SSRIs and how soon after initiation should you follow-up with a patient?”*

**A**: Suicidality and follow-up should be within 2 weeks of initiation.

**Additional Points:** *these can be integrated into above discussion points or provided later*

1. Evaluation of anxiety in school age children
   1. Early childhood/chronic stress: exposure to domestic violence, poverty, food or housing insecurity
   2. Parental anxiety – observed, learned through modeling
2. Parent Education
   1. Educate parents regarding normative worries in childhood vs. clinically-significant anxiety
      * Level of functional impairment guides treatment recommendations
   2. Emphasize importance of modeling effective coping skills
   3. Provide guidance re: parental management of the child’s anxiety
      * Parent to support child in implementing anxiety management strategies
      * Parent to avoid rewarding or reinforcing fears and avoidant behavior
        1. Provide reassurance, and continue all regular activities/schedule if possible
        2. Collaborate with school re: management of behavioral symptoms (school refusal)
   4. Optimistic but realistic expectations to promote success
      * Gradually increase independence and empower parent to empower child
   5. Sleep hygiene
      * Bedtime routine, regular bed and wake times, turn off screens
3. Recommended treatment
   1. Psychotherapy—individual, group, and/or family
   2. Medication (appendix)
      * Consider SSRI trial if moderate-severe functional impairment
      * Treatment with SSRI may help to reduce physical symptoms, in order for child to better participate in and benefit from therapeutic work

**Appendix**

Antidepressants: Common SSRIs

**Note:** Do not work right away, therapeutic effect may take 4-8 weeks

**fluoxetine (Prozac)**

***Indications:*** depression\* (age 8+), OCD\* (age 7+), panic disorder, anxiety, bulimia, PTSD

***Dosing:*** Initial dose 10mg/day, after 2-3 weeks can increase by 10mg weekly; max 80mg/day

* Titrate slower to decrease severity of side effects

**Notes:**

* Long half-life (~24h), may be better for patients with hx of poor med adherence
* More activating of the SSRIs, may be beneficial for patients with fatigue and low energy or atypical depression (hypersomnia, mood reactivity, increased appetite), may avoid in patients with agitation, insomnia or anorexia

**escitalopram (Lexapro)**

***Indications:*** depression\* (age 12+), anxiety, panic disorder, OCD, PTSD

***Dosing:*** Initial dose 5mg/day in children, initial dose 10mg/day in adolescents; can increase by 5mg weekly; max 20mg/day

* Little evidence that more therapeutic effects are seen at doses higher than 10mg; Ensure adequate trial of 10mg prior to increasing to 20mg
* Taper dose by 50% every 3 days if stopping

***Notes:***

* Generally, more effective for depression than anxiety
* May be better tolerated than other SSRIs
* Cleanest of the SSRIs (least interactions with other meds)

**sertraline (Zoloft)**

***Indications:*** OCD\* (age 6+), anxiety, depression, panic disorder, PTSD

***Dosing:*** Initial dose 20mg/day in children, initial dose 50mg/day in adolescents; increase by 25-50mg weekly; max 200mg/day

* Generally, higher doses are needed in OCD compared to other conditions
* Taper dose by 50% every 3 days if stopping

***Notes:***

* Generally, more effective for anxiety than depression
* May not be as effective for depression as other SSRIs
* May be beneficial for patients with intrusive thoughts
* Can have more GI side effects

**ALL SSRIs**

***Common side effects:*** insomnia, GI upset, headache, dry mouth, sexual dysfunction

If side effects occur, wait and monitor, they may go away in a few days-weeks

***Warnings:*** Risk of serotonin syndrome if dose is titrated too rapidly or when SSRIs are combined with MAO inhibitors

***Notes:***

* Titrate slower to decrease likelihood/severity of side effects

\* = FDA approved for this indication in children and adolescents
