| **Overview**  The majority of patients with acute sore throat have a **self-limited illness** that only needs treatment for symptomatic relief. However, some patients have infection with **Group A Streptococcus (GAS)** and will need an antibiotic (although the measurable benefit is actually modest). In addition, a few patients with acute sore throat have a **serious condition** that requires immediate intervention. Therefore, the physician’s top priority is to exclude these rare but serious conditions, and the second priority is to determine if GAS infection is present.  **History of Present illness**  Start with an open-ended question such as;  *“I see here that you have a sore throat. Tell me about your symptoms.”*  As you listen to the patient’s narrative, listen for specific points.   * Does the patient have young **children** in the home? (Increases the likelihood of GAS) * Does the patient mention a **fever** (also increases the likelihood of GAS) * Does the patient mention having a **cough** (this *decreases* the chances of GAS, and increases the chance of a respiratory virus) * Does the patient say that **symptoms** have been present for **>2 weeks** (decreases the likelihood of GAS infection; mononucleosis is more likely.) * Does the patient mention any **swollen, tender “glands**” in the neck (increases the likelihood of GAS infection). * Does the patient mention a **runny nose** (more likely to be viral)   If the patient does not mention one or more of these points, then ask specifically about them.  **Exclude Serious Conditions**  Check for “**red flag**” symptoms   * **Drooling** (from retro-pharyngeal abscess partially blocking entry into the esophagus) * **Shortness of breath** (from submandibular abscess partially closing airway) * **Inability to open the mouth** (trismus) (from peritonsillar abscess involving the palatopharyngeus muscles). | | |  | | --- | | **Basic tasks** of the clinician in acute sore throat   1. | **Past Medical History**  The PMH is the patient’s background medical information. In a patient with sore throat, we will not take a full PMH. Instead, we will focus on information that helps with clinical decision-making.  **Risk Behaviors**   * Tobacco smoking (increases the risk of oral cancer)   **Medication List**   * *Always ask if the patient has had a past drug reaction*   **Sexual History** as appropriate (use your judgment). Warn the patient that a personal question will follow, and then ask the question in a neutral and non-judgmental manner.   * Any new sexual partners? (gonorrhea, herpes) * If so, then has there been oral-genital contact?   **Review of Symptoms**  The ROS checks to make sure that no important points have been overlooked. It is a quick series of Yes/No questions. For sore throat, we will focus the ROS on several key organ systems.  **State of hydration**   * Ask about fluid intake, thirst, or decreased urine output (checking for dehydration).   **Respiratory**   * Ask about shortness of breath, coughing, wheezing (bronchospasm, asthma, pneumonia)   **Cardiac**   * Ask about chest discomfort (pleurisy, pneumonia) * Ask about prior history of **rheumatic fever** (rare, but it is important not to miss this)   **Endocrine**   * Ask about diabetes (blood sugars go up with any stress) | ![](data:image/png;base64...) | **Physical Examination:** We will be doing a focused examination today, examining only the throat and neck. Before the exam **wash your hands** or **use hand sanitizer**.  **Vital signs:** review the vital signs, focusing on the **temperature**.  **General: as** you observe the patient, look for “red flag” signs.   * Muffled voice (suggests epiglottitis – a potentially fatal condition) * Stiff neck (can be due to infection extending to the muscles of the neck) * Swelling and redness on the neck (extension of infection to the neck) * Also, is the voice hoarse? (suggests likely viral infection – unlikely with GAS)   **Throat exam:**   * Ask the patient to open their mouth.   + Do not ask them to protrude the tongue. This makes it harder to see.   + If the patient cannot lower their tongue, use a tongue blade.   + Place the tongue blade in the center of the tongue, not the back (as this will cause gagging and potentially even vomiting). * With a flashlight, inspect the throat for redness, exudates, and swelling.   **Neck:**   * Look at the neck for areas of swelling and redness. * Palpate the anterior and posterior cervical areas for tender, swollen lymph nodes.   + Use the pads of three fingers moving together.   + Move the hand in a circular motion (“skin circles”).   + Press lightly at first, then gradually increasing the firmness. | | ![](data:image/jpeg;base64...)  **Throat examination**  **![](data:image/jpeg;base64...)**  **Palpation for cervical adenopathy**  **MIRS Item 21:** Closure |

| **Communication Skills**  Provide **closure** at the end of the encounter, which would include:   * **Summary:** Summarize your findings to the patient. * **Next steps**: Depending on the findings, this might be:   + a rapid strep test   + a prescription for an antibiotic   + a recommendation that no antibiotic be prescribed * **Follow up:** Advicein case of unanticipated events. Examples:   + If an antibiotic is prescribed, advice regarding what to do if a rash from an allergic reaction develops.   + Advice what to do if the sore throat gets worse or does not get better after a reasonable period of time. | |
