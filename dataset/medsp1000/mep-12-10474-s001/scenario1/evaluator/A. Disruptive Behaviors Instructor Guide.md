**Small Group # 1**

**A child with disruptive behavior**

During today’s small group, we will take a look at the case using a progressive disclosure format to highlight and discuss aspects of the evaluation (data gathering), assessment (fine-tuning the differential) and treatment planning for a child patient.

**Learning objectives**

1. Determine what tools can be utilized in the assessment of disruptive behavior in a child.

2. Identify what associated patient symptoms a physician is trying to elicit to create a differential diagnosis list.

3. Discuss the relationship between ADHD and ODD and how that may affect a treatment plan.

4. Discuss treatment options for diagnoses that present with disruptive behavior in a child.

1. The case will be presented in progressive-disclosure format.
2. At each place where disclosure stops, your task is to **engage in group discussion** about your process for determining what **information to gather next** and then, what **intervention should occur next**.

| ***Case Vignette Part 1 -- Presenting Situation:*** |

First Outpatient Clinic Visit:

Mrs. Blake brings her 12 year old son, Tony to the outpatient family practice clinic. You have been seeing Tony since he was 4 years old. Today, you have a 30 minute appointment with Mrs. Blake and Tony for the following concern: Mrs. Blake read about ADHD online and is convinced that Tony has ADHD and is requesting a referral to a child psychiatrist so that Tony can be prescribed Adderall XR.

Mrs. Blake is worried that Tony is falling behind in academic work. Tony used to be an all A student up until this grade. However, since starting grade 7, Tony’s grades have been falling to Bs and Cs. Although Tony is also having behavior problems in school, Mrs. Blake is most worried about his academic decline. The family has limited financial resources since it is just Mrs. Blake, Tony and his 2 younger brothers in the home. Mrs. Blake has always expected that Tony’s academic success would fund his college career.

Before pursuing a referral, you’d like to find out more about what’s been going on with Tony.

**![](data:image/jpeg;base64...)**

**Questions for discussion at stopping point #1:**

1. Mrs. Blake feels that ADHD is the problem. What further information would you need at this time to make the diagnosis?
2. What are other diagnoses that may present in a similar manner – with similar symptoms, in this environment, at this developmental stage of life?

|  |
| --- |
| ***Directions for Facilitator*** |

1. Have students review the DSM 5 criteria for ADHD.
   1. Encourage students to discuss the presentation of ADHD inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, and combined presentations.
   2. Encourage discussion about gender differences in presentation types; typical age of diagnosis with ADHD inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive, and combined presentations.
2. A very broad differential diagnosis list should be generated at this point. Some diagnoses should include:
   1. Oppositional Defiant Disorder / Conduct Disorder (core feature: “bad” behavior)
   2. Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (core feature: persistent irritable mood)
   3. Adjustment Disorders (recent bullying, abuse, physical body changes) (core feature: proximal precipitating event)
   4. Depressive Disorders (core feature: in kids irritability, also sad mood)
   5. Anxiety Disorders (core feature: worry, also somatic complaints)
   6. Substance Related and Addictive Disorders (core feature: drug use)
   7. Intermittent Explosive Disorder (core feature: normal most of time, explosive outbursts)
   8. Specific Learning Disorders (core feature: difficulty in an academic environment)

Have students identify the **core symptoms** of the above diagnoses on the differential. Encourage students to think of using a review of symptoms to aid with ruling in or ruling out diagnoses on the differential list.

Have students identify some**criteria** of the differential disorders: precipitating events, duration of symptoms, etc. This is help them to identify what questions they would next ask in order to rule in or rule out the differential list.

Encourage students to formulate the questions that elicit important/core DSM -5 symptoms and criteria of each of the differential diagnoses.

| ***Case Vignette – Part 2, Continuation*** |

Over the next 10 minutes of the appointment, you learn the following information:

Tony is a very intelligent boy and because of his academic achievement in primary school, he was selected for Academically Gifted (AG) classes for middle school. Seventh grade is the first grade of this middle school and the school is much larger than his previous school – he is expected to change classes! His grades have declined as he has been noted to not turn in homework assignments. Tony says that he does the homework, but either forgets it at home, or loses it on the way to school. Mother says she can never tell him to do something once – she always has to repeat herself. She recalls that he was not always this way. In fact, up until he was about 8 or nine years old, he was a “model child” in obedience, behavior and temperament.

His behavioral problems began about 3 years ago. He began talking back to mother and refusing to do his chores in the home. Behavior problems began in school about 10 months after. Because of his behavior, he received several in-school suspensions in 5th grade. By 6th grade he received a 2 day out-of-school suspension (mother let him stay at home by himself on those 2 days). So far for 7th grade, he has only had 5 in-school suspensions but no out-of-school suspensions. Mother hasn’t been called by the school except for the out-of-school suspension last year and she feels like she can manage his behavior at home by just not asking him to do things that make him mad. There isn’t much of a punishment system in place in the home – if Tony refuses to do chores, mother simply asks his brothers or does it herself.

Mrs. Blake reports that Tony’s relationship with his brothers is very poor. Brothers say that Tony bosses them around, make them do his chores and act like “he’s a daddy.” Mrs. Blake admits that Tony’s brothers are scared of him because he once locked the door to the bedroom that all the boys share and set fire to the curtains while all three boys were in the room.

**![](data:image/jpeg;base64...)**

**Questions for discussion at stopping point #2:**

1. What are some assessment tools that can be utilized to gain further information?
2. Discuss the need to assess the patient’s strengths and the development of a strength-based treatment plan.
3. In the meantime, what advice would you provide for mother while you obtain collateral from teachers about behavior in the school setting?
4. Who are other professionals that may need to be involved in the assessment and treatment of this child?
5. Is it time now to refer to a specialist?

| ***Directions for Facilitator*** |

During the stopping point, encourage group discussion about how their case conceptualization is changing with this new information

Encourage them to describe their comfort as a PRIMARY CARE PHYSICIAN with treating this case in their office.

The following are some suggested questions you might ask to generate discussion:

* How do we feel about getting this amount of information and responding to it within a 30 minute outpatient session?
* Although there is much more information given now, the information presented remains vague and requires even further investigation. Students should be questioning the value of this information in culling the differential diagnosis list.
  + For example, the behavior that resulted in school suspensions should be questioned. As with any system, the school system can have nuances depending on location. In Tony’s school, what behaviors can lead to in school or out of school suspension? What were Tony’s specific behaviors that led to his suspensions?
  + Another example of vague information is regarding Tony’s fire-setting. While clearly this one instance would not lead to a Conduct Disorder diagnosis (remind students of criteria for CD), it is also unclear the intentions of his behavior. This is an opportunity to review normal childhood behavior – fire curiosity, etc. It is an opportunity to discuss the child’s developmental stage when considering the intentions behind his behavior – try to have students “think like a 12 year old.” Encourage students to formulate ways of asking Tony about his intentions/behaviors – remember to avoid using “why” in your questions.

FURTHER INFORMATION FOR THIS CASE REGARDING THE FIRE:

* For the purpose of this case, when students ask further about the fire, you can inform that this is the only incident of Tony with fire. It occurred 2 years ago when he was 10 years old. Brothers continue to recall this isolated event because of the extreme response of mother at that time 🡪 it was the only time Tony had ever been spanked. When Tony is asked about this fire incident, he describes being home alone with his brothers and they were all playing in the bedroom. When he heard mother return home, he quickly locked the bedroom door in an attempt to out the fire before mother discovered his actions. In his haste to out the fire, the curtains accidentally caught fire. He denied homicidal ideations. He expresses remorse about the incident. Mother had no further episodes of safety concerns and has already removed all weapons from the home.

1. Some examples of strengths and weaknesses could include:

| STRENGTHS | WEAKNESSES |

1. Here’s the opportunity to stress that treatment should include biopsychosocial considerations. Discuss behavior management ideas for mother to use at home: identifying developmentally appropriate expectations, consistent consequences, rewarding appropriate behavior, etc.
2. Still not time for a child psychiatrist – many options for treatment interventions remain in the primary care setting.

| ***Case Vignette – Part 3, Continuation*** |

Telephone Encounter 1 week later:

Mrs. Blake calls to report that Tony has gotten a 3 day out-of-school suspension for behavior towards the teacher. She is angry that you didn’t refer him to the child and adolescent psychiatrist who she feels would have already started medication. She does not feel comfortable leaving Tony at home for 3 days and doesn’t know what to do. Two days ago, you receive a faxed copy of rating scales completed by 2 of Tony’s teachers. The results of the Vanderbilt suggest that ODD is a more likely diagnosis. Mother’s Vanderbilt score suggests problems in every area.

**![](data:image/jpeg;base64...)**

**Questions for discussion at stopping point #3:**

1. How would you approach the angry caretaker?
2. It is now confirmed that the diagnosis is ODD and not ADHD:
   1. What is the historical information that supports ODD vs ADHD in this case?
   2. What treatment plan would be appropriate at this stage? (Consider biological, psychological and social approaches)?

|  |
| --- |
| ***Directions for Facilitator*** |

1. The approach to the angry patient: Listen, Empathize, Problem solve.
   1. Remind students that there are several steps in empathizing with patients - recognition, reflection, attuning to emotional messages, attending to nonverbal communication, accepting negative feedback.
   2. Discuss the expectation of medication (parents expect a miracle drug, what symptoms, and to what degree do symptoms resolve with stimulants, with alpha-adrenergics, etc., are meds going to work equally on all symptoms, etc.)
2. a. Have students review the case facts (DSM-5 criteria) that point to the diagnosis of ODD.

b. Encourage group discussion about treatment plans

* + Biological – Are there medications that are FDA approved for ODD? Continue to monitor for comorbidities that would require pharmacological treatment.
  + Psychological – Parent management Training (PMT); Parent Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT) for up to 7 year olds. Individual therapies (cognitively based) that would be focused on anger management skills, problem-solving ability, techniques for delaying impulsive responses and improve social interactions.
  + Social – what supports can be put in place for mother? Church, family, structured day care for “respite” time, etc. Consider finding male role models for this child who is the oldest male in his household. Involvement in strength based activities.

| ***Case Vignette – Part 4, Conclusion*** |

Second outpatient Clinic Visit

Tony and his mother return for a follow up visit 3 months later. Mother states that there has been only minimal improvement in his behavior after they enrolled in parent management training classes 6 weeks ago. Mother has several questions for you.

**Questions for discussion at stopping point #4:**

1. Mother is frustrated that Tony is not getting better. She wants to know how long it will take for the therapy to work.
2. Mother wants to know if Tony will develop conduct disorder and then become a psychopath. What counseling can you provide for mother?

![](data:image/jpeg;base64...)

|  |
| --- |
| ***Directions for Facilitator*** |

Instructor:

1. Discuss the typical expectations of psychotherapy, including
   1. things get worse before they get better – extinction burst
   2. behavior therapy requires active participation of the parent and frequent adjustments to rewards
   3. typical duration depends on severity of symptoms, adherence to recommendations, etc
   4. behavioral training therapy is considered a brief therapy (12-16 sessions)
2. Discuss the difference between the diagnosis of ODD and the diagnosis of CD.
   1. Review the most current statistics on patients with CD going onto being diagnosed with Antisocial Personality Disorder
