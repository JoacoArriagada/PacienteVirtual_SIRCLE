# Case	Overview

Carol	Carter	is	a	32-year-old ${ \mathsf { G } } 2 { \mathsf { P } } 1 0 0 1 ^ { 1 }$ at	33	weeks	gestation	presenting	to	Labor	and	Delivery with	contraction-like	pains,	which	began	the	previous	night	and	had initially	occurred	at irregular	intervals	every	couple	of	hours.	The	contractions	have	progressed	in	frequency	to every	10-15	minutes	that	morning.	Her	husband,	Allen	Carter,	accompanies	her	in	the	hospital room.

Carol’s	contractions	are	concerning	for	preterm	labor,	although	she	has	not	broken	her	water and	denies	any	vaginal	bleeding.	The	patient	is	anxious	and	concerned	about	the	well	being	of her	child,	but	remains	cooperative	throughout	the	encounter.	However, Mr.	Carter	is	frustrated that	a	medical	student	has	been	sent	to	interview	his	wife	and	feels	that	it	is	indicative	of	the hospital	and	staff	not	taking	their	situation	seriously.	He	is	verbally	aggressive	to	the	student, offers	multiple	disruptions	to	the	encounter,	and	becomes	increasingly	hostile	as	the	interview continues.

# How	to	Appear	During	the	Encounter

General	appearance/	grooming:

Healthy	appearing	female,	noticeably	pregnant	(pregnancy	belly	prop) Appears	physically	uncomfortable	from	the	occasional	contractions Well-groomed,	clean	but	not	styled	with	minimal	make-up Accompanied	by	her	husband:	male,	healthy-appearing	and	well-groomed

Dress:		Hospital	Gown		(Carol) Button	down	shirt	and	pants	(Allen)

Description	of	Affect	and	Behavior:

Carol	Carter:	Carol	sits	on	the	exam	table,	clearly	worried	her	pains	may	be	contractions signaling	the	onset	of	preterm	labor.	Despite	her	worry,	she	is	generally	calm	and	cooperative with	the	student’s	medical	interview.	Her	husband,	Allen,	accompanies	her.	He	is	unpleasant toward	the	student and	impatient	with	their	presence.	Although	Carol	does	not	convey	the same	attitude	toward	the	student,	she	does	not	take	great	pains	to	stop	her	husband	from escalating	during	the	encounter.	She	may	initially	intercede	on	the	student’s	behalf,	assuring her	husband	that	the	student merely	needs	to	collect	some	information	before	the	physician sees	them.	As	Allen	becomes	increasingly	agitated,	Carol	may	place	her	hand	on	her	forehead, shake	her	head,	and	sigh	in	resignation	to	her husband.	Carol	is	not	the	source	of	difficult communication	and	should	give	information	to	the	student as	requested.

Approximately	halfway	through	the	encounter,	or	if	the	encounter	is	becoming	particularly heated	between	Allen	and	the	student,	Carol	should	experience	a	contraction	and	may	have two	to	three	throughout	the	entire	encounter.	At	this	point,	she	closes	her	eyes	in	pain,	takes	a few	quick	breaths,	and	places	her	hand	on	her	abdomen.	Each	contraction	may	last	thirty seconds	to	a	minute.

Allen	Carter:	Allen	either	stands	next	to	the	examination	bed	or	sits	in	a	chair	drawn	closely	to his	wife.	He	may	hold	her	hand	or	place	a	hand	on	her	knee	or	shoulder.	His	body	remains oriented	toward	Carol	out	of	concern	and	attentiveness.	He	is	at	first	happy	to	see	the	student, thinking	he	or	she	is	the	physician.	However,	upon	receiving	the	student’s	introduction,	Allen becomes	frustrated	and	expresses	that	he	wants	his	wife	to	be	seen	by	a	“real	doctor	– not someone	in	training”.	The	student may	attempt	to explain	the	role	of	the	medical	student	in	a teaching	hospital.	Allen	is	not	satisfied	with	this	explanation,	but	when	his	wife	intercedes	and asks	Allen	to	allow	the	student to	interview	her,	he	will	begrudgingly	submit.

Allen	remains	hostile	throughout	the	encounter,	but	will	allow	the	medical	interview	to progress	if	the	student takes	the	time	to	address	the	partner’s	frustrations,	partnering	with	the couple,	and	assures	them	that	the	hospital	will	work	to	care	for	his	wife	and	child.	The	student establishes	rapport	with	Allen	by	appealing	to	a	common	goal	of	caring	for	his	wife.	Overall,	it	is important	to	remember	that	Allen	is	not	unpleasant	by	nature,	but	merely	acting	out	of	fear and	concern	for	his	wife’s	health	and	the	well	being of	their	child.	These	emotions	in	the	setting of	feeling	neglected	and	unfamiliar	with	the	workings	of	a	teaching	hospital,	where	many people	at	different	educational	levels	are	involved	in	patient	care,	make	for	a	difficult encounter.	The	student would	do	well	to	address	these	issues	and	will	be	met	with	increasing hostility	and	interruptions	if	ignored.

In	particular,	certain	aspects	of	the	encounter	or	the	student that	Allen	is	apt	to	attend	to	or criticize	may	include:

If	the	student does	not	write	details	of	the	interview	on	a	piece	of	paper,	Allen	should ask	whether	the	student is	memorizing	his	wife’s	medical	history. If	the	student spends	a	disproportionate	amount	of	his	attention	to	writing,	Allen	may point	out	that	it	is	only	polite	to	look	at	the	person	you	are	speaking	with. Allen	may	ask	whether	the	physician	who	will	eventually	see	them	will	ask	his	wife	all	of the	same	questions. Allen	may	inquire	as	to	what	year	the	medical	student	is	in	and	how	long	they	have	been working	with	obstetric	patients. If	told	the	academic	hierarchy	of	medical	student,	resident	physician,	and	attending, Allen	wants	to	know	whether	they	will	be	expected	to	relay	the	same	information	to	all three	individuals	and	who,	ultimately,	is	responsible	for	caring	for	his	wife. How	long	will	it	take	before	a	“real	doctor”	sees	them?

These	are	merely	examples	of	Allen’s	possible	complaints,	but	he	is	ready	to	point	out	any shortcoming	he	perceives	in	the	student.	He	may	do	so	less	frequently	if	the	student addresses his	concerns	in	a	respectful	manner.
