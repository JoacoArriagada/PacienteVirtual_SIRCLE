**Resolution of the Clinical Scenario:** (read out loud to participants after the end of the simulation and prior to the debriefing portion)

The patient was taken emergently for exploratory laparotomy by the pediatric surgery team after successful stabilization by the primary team of caregivers. In the OR, the surgeon found copious amounts of formula and purulent material throughout the peritoneal cavity and noted the false tract created by the GT. The patient’s abdomen was irrigated, GT revised and broad-spectrum antibiotics were continued upon transfer to the PICU.

**SCENARIO I: MEDICALLY COMPLEX CHILD WITH ACUTE ABDOMEN**

**Relevant Clinical and Management Information for Debriefing Session**

(For distribution to Participants during Debriefing Session)

TW is a 2 y.o. male with history of TBI due to MVA s/p VPS with severe neurologic impairment, oromotor dysfunction, seizure disorder, tracheostomy-dependence and severe spasticity, who was admitted for failure to thrive. You became involved in his care due to acute onset of fussiness and vomiting.

In this case, the primary etiology of his acute abdomen was related to his gastrostomy tube (GT). In the course of events, as it became apparent, this patient’s newly-placed GT had been accidentally dislodged and then replaced with re-initiation of feeds. As more and more surgeons are placing low-profile buttons upon initial GT insertion, it may become more difficult for practitioners to clearly identify the GT that has been recently placed/manipulated versus the GT that has been present for a long time. It is therefore imperative that as a practitioner, you obtain the relevant history to determine the management pathway to follow for tube dislodgement. In addition, it is important to ensure that all members of the health care team are aware of the initial date of GT placement. Some institutions have specific guidelines that allow for easy identification of the newly-placed GT and/or a management algorithm for dislodged GTs.

Perioperative GT dislodgement is managed differently than remote dislodgment—this relates to how stable the stoma is. Various sources approximate different timeframes for complete healing, anywhere from 7 days (in adults) to 8 weeks. There is no consistent guideline for the pediatric population at this time.

Peritonitis is a rare but potentially fatal complication of gastrostomy tubes. Peritonitis can be associated with the following: peritoneal placement of the tube, tube migration with perforation, early tube dislodgement(as was the case in this scenario), leakage of gastric contents into the peritoneum, and perforation of other abdominal structures during placement.

|  |
| --- |
| ***Management of Patient with Suspected Peritonitis due to GT Malplacement*** |
| * *Stabilize airway, breathing and circulation* * *Stop GT feeds, full NPO; convert medications to alternate routes* * *Place NG tube for decompression and gastric drainage* * *Provide fluid resuscitation and maintenance* * *If suspected, urgent consultation with radiologist, interventionalist and/or surgeon is mandatory* * *Radiologic investigation: Check GT placement (optimally with fluoroscopic dye study, but can also attempt to assess with plain film); may require US or CT if intrabdominal fluid collection is suspected vs. exploratory laparotomy (patient dependent)* |
