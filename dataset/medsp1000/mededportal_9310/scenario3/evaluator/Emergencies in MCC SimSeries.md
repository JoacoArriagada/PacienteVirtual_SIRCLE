**SCENARIO 3: Relevant Clinical and Management Information for Debriefing Session**

(For distribution to Participants during Debriefing Session)

RS is a 9 year old male who presents to the emergency room at a community hospital with a

three day history of emesis which is worse in the morning, low grade temperature x 2 days, and

increased sleepiness x 2 days. He has had a 1 month history of decreased participation and interest in his favorite school activities. His mother is concerned that he may be in pain and with direct questioning ("Has this ever happened in the past?"), she tells you that these symptoms remind her of his previous shunt malfunction.

Morning vomiting suggests that he has increased intracranial pressure. The one month history of decreased participation and interest in school activities suggests that his shunt malfunction may have slowly evolved initially.

While his sister has the "stomach flu", it is important to note that she has fever, vomiting AND diarrhea while RS only has fever and emesis. While gastrointestinal illnesses are easily shared between family members, the LACK of diarrhea means that the index of suspicion for an alternate etiology for his symptoms must be higher.

In this case, the primary etiology of RS's vomiting and altered mental status is related to his

ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) failure and infection. In infants, signs of VPS failure may include a full and tense fontanel (soft spot), bulging of the scalp veins, swelling or redness along the shunt tract as well as vomiting, irritability, sleepiness and decreased interest in eating. Infants may demonstrate downward deviation of the eyes ("sun setting sign") and fever. Children may also show loss of motor milestones.

When a child is older and the cranial sutures are fused, symptoms may include headaches, vomiting, irritability and fatigue. Children may report visual changes such as diplopia or demonstrate loss of conjugate gaze on exam. Patients and their families may also report difficulty waking up and staying awake, personality changes and loss of coordination. In the event of an abrupt malfunction, a patient may develop symptoms very rapidly, in a matter of hours. Without treatment, coma, permanent brain damage, and even death, may result. Hypertension, bradycardia, and irregular respirations (Cushing's triad) are late findings which may be seen in patients with impending herniation. All three elements of the triad are not always present.

Common causes of VPS failure in children include infection, obstruction (due to buildup of excess protein or tumor in cerebrospinal fluid), overdrainage (extra-axial fluid collection may cause brain compression, subdural hematomas), and intraventricular hemorrhage. Shunt malfunction can occur with both partial and complete blockage of the shunt. Also, the components of the shunt can become broken, disconnected or relocate from their original positions.

| ***Recommended Management Summary:***  ***Patient with increased ICP and impending herniation due to VPS failure*** |
| * Decrease ICP by hyperventilation ONLY if signs of impending herniation * If concerned about herniation, bag-mask ventilate at a fast rate or bag through ETT if intubated * Ventilate to maintain CO2 in range of 35-40. * Promote venous drainage by keeping head midline and HOB at 30 degrees. * Push hyperosmolar agents (mannitol 0.25 to 0.5 g/kg) or (3% saline 5 ml/kg) to goal serum osmolality around 320. * Call Neurosurgery for decompressive craniotomy or drain placement. * Intubate (use Thiopental or Etomidate as neither will increase ICP, avoid Ketamine, Consider lidocaine to blunt ICP spike). * Deep sedation and seizure prophylaxis. * Maintain cardiovascular support to maintain blood pressure (consider 20 ml/kg isotonic fluid boluses, or possibly dopamine or phenylephrine for alpha effect). * Maintain serum sodium goal of 145-155. |
