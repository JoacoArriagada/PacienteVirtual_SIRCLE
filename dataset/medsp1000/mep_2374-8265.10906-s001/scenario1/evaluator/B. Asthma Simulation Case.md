| **Brief narrative description of case** | A 2 year old boy “Eli” with history of eczema and reactive airway disease presents to the emergency department with respiratory distress. The patient’s signs and symptoms are consistent with lower airway obstruction. The team will evaluate the patient, develop a differential diagnosis and proceed with treatment of an acute reactive airway exacerbation. The team will be required to provide multiple medications in order to stabilize the child. |
| **Primary Learning Objectives** | After participation in this simulation and debrief (Scenario 1), learners will be able to:   * Demonstrate ability to assess and emergently manage airway, breathing and circulation * Identify lower airway obstruction and respiratory distress * Formulate a list of possible diagnosis and prioritize elements of evaluation * Manage respiratory distress from presumed reactive airway disease * Construct a disposition plan after stabilization in the emergency department * Demonstrate constructive team dynamics and direct, closed-loop communication with the medical team. |
| **Critical Actions** | * Perform the primary survey throughout the simulation case and interpret the findings to develop a differential diagnosis. * Implement a treatment plan for reactive airway disease including administration of bronchodilators, steroids and (optionally) magnesium sulfate. * Reassess the patient after interventions and address the need for further intervention. * Develop contingency plans should the initial interventions not produce the expected results. |
| **Learner Preparation** | Learners should have pre-requisite knowledge about:   * How to perform the primary survey (assessment of airway, breathing, circulation and disability) * How to stabilize a child with respiratory distress from lower airway obstruction * Management of reactive airway disease in the acute setting * Teamwork in the emergency setting * Communication with families to provide accurate and timely information about ongoing evaluations and therapies in the emergency room setting. |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

The learners enter the room and find the child in respiratory distress. They immediately place him on monitors and perform a primary exam. They identify that he has signs of increased work of breathing and evidence of lower airway obstruction concerning for reactive airway disease/asthma exacerbation. After obtaining a pertinent history they identify a lower airway obstruction and begin treating the patient with albuterol and provide steroid therapy. After the first appropriately dosed albuterol treatment, the patient demonstrates only minimal improvement. They must therefore start a second albuterol treatment and may consider additional treatment with magnesium sulfate. After either of these interventions the patient has improvement in his symptoms and a disposition plan including continued albuterol and steroids must be developed.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

*.*

1. Failure to recognize the signs of reactive airway disease: We found that medical students do not always identify wheezing and signs of air trapping on exam. Sometimes they misinterpret wheeze as stridor and ask for racemic epinephrine to be given. Other times they decide the patient has pneumonia or bronchiolitis. Additionally, students often interpret minimal or no wheezing as not asthma when in fact the exam is limited as the patient is moving minimal air. If students do not order albuterol, a facilitator may ask “What sounds are you hearing?” If the team does not correct itself a facilitator may ask the parent, “Is this similar to other times he has needed albuterol?”
2. Desire to intubate the patient: We found that medical students sometimes want to jump to intubation when a patient is demonstrating respiratory distress. The facilitator may ask “Is that going to make his underlying problem better?” If the team does pursue intubation, this may be discussed during the debrief.
3. Failure to give medications other than a beta-agonist: Some students do not order additional medications besides albuterol. The facilitator may ask, “Is there anything else we could be giving to help right now?”
