**Appendix D: Asthma Simulation (Scenario 1) Debriefing Materials**

**Debriefing Overview**

*Simulation creates the opportunity to debrief. We believe that the focus of each simulation should be the DEBRIEF. Simulation creates the opportunity to examine our medical management, technical skills and teamwork and communication skills. It facilitates discussion about challenges in a safe environment in order to improve the quality of patient care. As a general rule of thumb, the debrief should be one to two times the length of the scenario. Debriefing is a skill that often requires practice and feedback. It is crucial that the debriefer is about to critically observe with simulation and not be distracted by other tasks such as filling a role, when possible.*

**Framework for debriefing:**

Each debrief should consist of 4 components:

* + - Introduction
    - Discussion of emotions
    - Discussion of medical management and technical skills
    - Discussion of teamwork and communication skills

*There is often overlap between medical management and teamwork issues. Debriefing may not follow a linear progression of all four of these components.*

General Debriefing Goals:

* + - Try to facilitate the team’s discussion (avoid dominating the conversation)
    - Ensure a safe learning environment
  + Discuss the team performance (not the individual)
  + Reiterate the formative, not summative nature of the simulation/debrief
    - Ask open ended questions (avoid yes/no questions)

1. **Introduction**

This “sets the stage” for debriefing and creates expectations.

What you might say:

* "This is an opportunity to reflect and learn, improve our medical care, teamwork, and communication."
* "Everyone should be able to ask questions and share their thoughts."
* "Once you leave this session, we encourage open discussion of the concepts, but ask you to not to discuss individual performance."
* Remind the group of the ground rules (treat everyone with respect, maintain confidentiality).

1. **Emotional experience discussion**

There are a couple of camps regarding discussing emotions. One perspective is that until emotions are dealt with, it’s difficult for adult learners to “move on”: switching gears to process thoughts, actions and opportunities for improvement. Another perspective is that adult learners should process their emotions independently.

Our perspective is the first. If a group or team member is emotionally charged (sad, mad or frustrated) regarding something that did or didn’t happen in a scenario, it’s usually difficult for the individual or the group to be actively engaged, receptive to feedback and able to promote learning, until the emotions are addressed.

Validate the learners’ reactions/emotions, and if learners do not volunteer their reactions/emotions, suggest some common ones such as:

* “These situations can be stressful/intimidating… “

An example: a medication error occurs. One team member may think it is all his/her fault. S/he may feel embarrassed, judged, etc. If he/she can verbalize this, other team members may offer different perspectives, which enable the team to process the error together, potentially identifying contributing systems issues. If the emotions aren’t addressed- 3 separate people may feel embarrassed, responsible and not engage in a discussion, failing to identify systems issues which led to the error.

What you might say:

* "How did that feel?"
* "Can you tell me more? Why?"

1. **Medical management and technical skills**

This portion of the discussion focuses on the medical aspects of the scenario. It’s usually more comfortable to begin with these “facts”.

What you might say:

* + - "Let’s begin by discussing medical management."
    - "What did you think was wrong with the patient? Can someone briefly summarize what happened in this scenario?"
    - "How did you reach those conclusions?"

1. **Teamwork and communication (a. k. a. crew resource management, non-technical skills)**

This portion of the discussion focuses on how the team worked together. It can be emotionally charged and difficult to discuss without feeling personal. The challenge is to try to generalize specifics into themes.

What you might say:

* + - "Let’s talk about how you functioned as a team."
    - "What did your team do well?"
    - "What could your team do differently next time?"
    - "That is something I see often. Has anyone else experienced that? How have you seen that handled?"

### 5) Summarizing

* This is your opportunity to ensure the key learning points are highlighted
* Try to identify approximately three take-home points
* You may ask the participants’ to identify take home points or call them out yourself.

*Medical management/technical skills examples:*

* + - * 1. "This was a scenario of a child with a history of asthma presenting in respiratory distress."
        2. "The differential diagnosis respiratory distress is large. Focusing on the primary survey and having a systematic work up are critical for effective resuscitation. Evaluation and management of respiratory distress include physical examination, IV resuscitation as necessary, identification of likely etiology and medical management when possible, supporting oxygenation and ventilation, as needed."

*Teamwork/ communication examples:*

* + - * 1. "It is important to recognize need for a full resuscitation team when a patient is in severe respiratory distress."
        2. "It is crucial to designate leadership and team member roles to ensure coordinated team functioning."

Role assignment to specific individuals to avoid duplication/omission of tasks

Respect toward all team members is key to enable empowerment to speak up if patient safety issues arise.

* + - * 1. "Use brief or huddle to create a shared mental model for the working diagnosis and management plan."
        2. "Closed-loop communication is of utmost importance to ensure safe and adequate communication."

**Instructions for using included debriefing tools**

We have included 2 options to be used by facilitators during the simulation and debrief. These forms should be reviewed prior to the simulation in order to orient the users to their content. Both allow facilitators to take notes related to performance and achievement of learning objectives by participants. It is difficult to address more than 4-6 items per debriefing session and both objectives related to medical management and communication skills should be addressed.

1. **Debriefing Guide**

This is a more comprehensive tool that provides a specific script the facilitator may use to address specific learning objectives during the debrief. It includes brief synopses of relevant material related to the objectives. The right most column provides a space for facilitators to record their observations during the simulation to help guide discussion pertinent to the objectives. This form may be useful for novice facilitators who require more directed support and scripting during the debrief.

1. **Evaluation/Debriefing Form**

This is a brief tool that provides some general questions to guide the facilitator through the debrief while addressing specific learning objectives. There is space to record notes during the session as well as a prompt to provide a global assessment of performance during the simulation. This form may be useful to facilitators with experience in debriefing who do not desire to use a specific script but would benefit from a tool to organize observations and key points to address.
