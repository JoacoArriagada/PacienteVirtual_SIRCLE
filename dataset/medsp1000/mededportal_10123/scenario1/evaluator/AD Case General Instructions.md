I. Rehab Emergencies for Post-Graduate Year 2 (PGY-2) Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (PM&R) Residents: Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD)

II. Target Audience: PGY-2 PM&R Residents

III. Learning Objectives or Assessment Objectives

1. Formative: At the end of this session, the student will:

a. Know which population is at risk for developing Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD)

b. Recognize the common presenting signs and symptoms of AD

c. Recognize AD as a medical emergency

d. Know successful interventions for preventing and reversing AD

e. Distinguish patient needs in the case where a causative factor cannot be established and the patient continues to be symptomatic

2. Summative: At the end of this session, the student will

a. Demonstrate competence in performing a focused physical exam by:

i. Obtaining vital signs

ii. Performing a focused assessment of the situation

iii. Performing a focused neurologic exam:

1. Assessment of the cranial nerves

2. Deep tendon reflexes

3. Gross strength/sensation

4. Range of motion to ascertain tone

5. Testing for dural tension

iv. Ascertaining the cause of this patient’s AD and reverse it effectively

v. Recognizing the kinked foley as the causative factor of AD

b. Communicate and counsel a patient effectively in prevention of AD

IV. Environment

1. Station signs

2. Hospital bed

3. Blood pressure cuff

4. Stethoscope

5. Foley catheter with leg bag (empty)

6. Adhesive or means to fasten foley to thigh/groin

7. Bed sheets

8. Hospital gown and socks

9. Note cards

V. Actors

1.Standardized patient, male, 27 years of age, in good physical health, dressed in hospital gown in bed

VI. Case Narrative (describes what the learner will experience)

Opening Line: "Please stop my headache, doc, I beg you."

Standardized patient HPI: Your name is Steve Spiner, age 27. You were in good health, working construction, and had a young daughter named Hannah at home, who just turned 1 years old. You have a wife, whom you’ve been married to for 2 years. You were fixing a burst pipe in your home and were trying to access the a ceiling panel in your basement on a ladder. Unfortunately, you pulled a ceiling panel a little too hard and fell backwards, landing on your back, which resulted in a fracture of your 4th thoracic (T4) vertebra. You were taken to the hospital, where you underwent surgery by Dr. X to stabilize the spine and were fused from T2-6.

After a brief recovery at a large teaching hospital, you were transferred to a free standing Rehabilitation Hospital, where you have spent the past 3 weeks learning to function independently with your disability. You are an American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) A, meaning that you have a complete injury, no volitional movement of your lower extremities, and some difficulty with trunk stability. Additionally, you are working toward intermittent catheterization, but still have a foley catheter due to some difficulty with void trials.

Aside from the voiding difficulty, you are doing well. You did not have any blood clots during your acute care stay, and you did not develop any pneumonias. The one issue that is worth mentioning is that you had a urinary tract infection several weeks ago, but you were successfully treated with Cipro.

This evening you went to bed at a normal time, and had a good day of therapy. You were able to fall asleep quickly, and did not have a headache at that time. Your shoulders are a bit sore from transfer training, but otherwise you had felt well prior to sleep. You were awoken from sleep about 15 minutes ago with a headache. This headache is pounding and located in the front of your head on both sides. It feels like someone is squeezing your head together, you rate the pain as 10/10. There is no numbness, tingling, or sensory changes in your hands or anywhere else in the body. You do not have any flashes, floaters or see halos around lights. You do not feel nauseous, and have not vomited. The light in the room is not making your headache worse. Additionally, you feel sweaty and flushed in the face, and your vision is somewhat blurred, but you attribute this to the intense pain you are having. You have goosebumps on your legs and nasal congestion. You think that perhaps this is a really bad “head cold.” You are now upset that the nurse has not given you anything for pain and you want something done about the pain.

* 1. **Life situation**: You are newly married with a young child at home. Your wife was out of work to care for the child, and you worked in construction to pay the bills.
  2. **Character description**:
     1. Objective: To alleviate this headache
     2. Obstacles: You are upset that no one has adequately addressed the headache yet
     3. Tactics:
        1. You are initially confrontational and impatient, wanting something to be done. If the resident is sympathetic, then you calm down a little. If not, then you continue to be angry.
        2. If the resident does not sit you up and take your vital signs within 3 minutes of the encounter, you feign that the pain is worsening, and start to pass out. You then will direct them to a simulator to run a code.
  3. **Personality**: You are a risk taker and tend to be a little hot-headed. In this scenario, since you are in quite a bit of pain, you are impatient and demanding of the physician.
  4. **Past medical history**: Aside from the recent spinal cord injury 5 weeks ago, you are in good health.
  5. **Family history**: Your mother is alive and well, with high blood pressure. Your father suffered a heart attack at age 50, but is currently doing well after heart surgery. There is no family history of headache.
  6. **Medications**: Docusate, senna, bisacodyl suppository, lioresal, tizanidine, diazepam at night, oxycodone immediate release every 4 hours as needed (PRN), acetaminophen PRN
  7. **Allergies:** None
  8. **Encounter:**
     1. When the resident knocks and enters the room, you are laying supine, flat, in bed moaning in pain and grabbing at your head in the dark. You are upset, and ask if the resident “can help with the pain. I’ve been waiting here for 15 minutes in excruciating pain and no one has done anything.” You are testy and demand something be done about the pain.
     2. The resident should turn on the lights and sit you up. While asking you questions, he/she should take vitals. If he/she does not do this within a few minutes, then you will degrade clinically and pass out, causing a code, whereby you direct them to a simulator.
     3. If the resident does at least sit you up, you can continue to answer questions about the headache. He/she should be asking questions relevant to onset, provocation/palliation, radiation, quality of the pain, etc. With respect to your current state:
        1. Pain- “10/10”
        2. Quality- “Throbbing, intense”
        3. Location- “both sides in the front above the eyes”
        4. Radiation- “No”
        5. Palliation/provocation- “I haven’t gotten anything for the pain yet, Doc, give me something and stop asking these questions.”
        6. Visual disturbances (flashes, floaters, halos around light) - “No.”
        7. Blurred vision- “Yes.”
     4. Associated symptoms: Offer the fact that you are feeling really warm, and are sweating an awful lot, and you feel like your face is flushed. You feel like this all might be related to a head cold, because your nose is runny too, and you feel congested.
     5. The resident may ask you when you moved your bowels last. If this is the case, then you had your bowel routine around 7PM with a good amount of stool evacuated. The resident may also ask about skin breakdown. If this is the case, you can tell him/her that you do not have a pressure ulcer, and that the nurses faithfully turn you, and that you perform weight shifts in the wheelchair every 30 minutes.
     6. At some point during the physical exam, the resident should get your vitals. If he/she does not do this and finishes his/her assessment,, say “Doc my head feels like it’s going to explode!” Subsequently you will pass out and direct them to a simulator to run a code.
     7. If they do obtain your vitals, they will see that your blood pressure is extremely elevated. They should immediately start to search for a cause of the pressure elevation, take your socks off, and ask to look in your groin. If they see you have a foley (bag should be empty, no urine flow), they will simply need to unkink the catheter, which will be easily located on the thigh.
     8. If they do not attempt to look at the feet, legs, and groin, and continue to examine the head/neck/ or eyes, then you say “Doc my head feels like it’s going to explode!” If they start to test your muscles or sensation, pass out and direct them to a simulator to run a code.
     9. If the resident recognizes that the foley catheter is the problem, and unkinks it, then you should ask him/her about why this happened. He/she should explain to you about AD.
  9. **Timing:** There are a lot of places where the Standardized Patient (SP) can decompensate. If the resident acts appropriately during the encounter, and the SP “survives” without decompensation, then the SP may ask about prevention with any time left over, or what to do if you can’t find a reason for the dysreflexia.

VII. Debriefing Plan

There were several facets to the debriefing of this module. The first part was a "pre-briefing" overview of what residents could reasonably expect to encounter, general times, and schedule-specific information. After the encounter, we opted to provide debriefing *en masse*. This way, we could generate a discussion about resident performance, constructive criticism regarding the construction of the case, and what residents may have learned from the encounter. Standardized patients were also invited to the discussion, and although they did not isolate a specific resident or "finger point," they were able to generate specific feedback to the group. Videos of each clinical encounter were recorded, and the link to each video provided to the residents so that they could review their own performances. Facilitator(s) watched each resident's individual performance in real time, and thus were also able to comment on specific aspects of the case that they observed.

Each resident completed a pre- and post- encounter form (see attached), which served not only to ascribe data and feedback to the encounter, but also to serve as a platform to generate discussion. These questions include, but are not limited to, the resident's comfort level with making a diagnosis, management of autonomic dysreflexia, and counseling a patient on preventative strategies. These questions reflected the course objectives in that they are a subjective attestation of the resident's comfort level in both the formative and summative objectives. Both the summative and formative objectives of the course rely on effective diagnosis, management, counseling, and prevention of AD, which is precisely what the pre-/post-encounter forms ask. Debriefing materials included only encounter forms, pens/pencils, notebook paper, and a location where a group could meet comfortably.

After the debriefing, residents moved to a room with a projector and computer, and a didactic presentation was provided by the facilitator (see attached). This didactic presentation was a concise review of the signs and symptoms, diagnosis, management, and prevention strategies in autonomic dysreflexia for a patient with spinal cord injury. The didactic portion was calculated to last approximately 15 minutes, and by situating it at the end of the session residents were able to relate what they saw clinically to the information provided.

IX. Pilot Testing and Revisions

A. Numbers of participants

-8 PGY-2 residents at the beginning of their rehabilitation training, with no formalized training in rehabilitation emergencies, 2 standardized patients trained for the case

B. Performance expectations, anticipated management mistakes:

-Residents were expected to be able to recognize the situation as autonomic dysreflexia, and manage the patient accordingly in a manner that reflected an "on-call situation." They were also expected to be able to communicate effectively and counsel the patient after the acute event.

-Since residents are used to having vitals handed to them, one expected management mistake anticipated was failing to obtain vitals.

- Residents might also fail to recognize the severity of the situation and speak to the patient without taking action (loosening clothing, sitting patient up, looking for causal factor). Failure to multi-task in this manner might be expected of even experienced medical professionals, but would lead to SP decompensation.

-If the SP does decompensate, they should be directed to the simulation mannikin, but the SP need not interact with the simulator. They simply direct the resident to the simulator stating that they now need to run a code.

- Residents might also fail to properly manage time and subsequently allow the patient to decompensate before realizing that something has to be done.

-Breakdown in communication might be expected to occur if the resident has trouble figuring out the problem.

-The resident might also say they are going to do something, such as "I'm going to have the nurse change the foley," but due to the mock nature of the activity, not actually discover the problem (a kinked foley), because they feel they are addressing the problem by verbalizing it. This confuses the situation and leads to disruptions of flow and execution of the activity.

C. Evaluation form for participants: see attached

-Participants were given pre- and post-encounter forms with which to fill out (see attached). These forms reflected comfort level with the tested material and thus was a subjective attestation from the learners regarding their progress.

-At the post encounter, all 8 residents in the pilot program responded with either "agree" or "strongly agree" when asked about the diagnosis, management, risk factors, and counseling of patients with AD. In the pre-encounter form, residents were mixed, but generally responded that they were either "neutral" or "agree" that they were comfortable with the diagnosis, management, risk factors, and counseling of patients with AD. Please see attached for specific data concerning resident responses.
