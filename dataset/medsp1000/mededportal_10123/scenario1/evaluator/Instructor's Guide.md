**Describe the overall purpose of the resource**

This rehabilitation related emergency situation in a spinal cord injured patient is a resource that was designed for medical residents beginning their rehabilitation residency. Although the encounter was designed for rehabilitation residents, it could be applied to any number of medical professionals that encounter patients outside of the rehabilitation setting. With minimal modifications, this resource could be applied for use in a family practice setting, emergency department, or skilled nursing facility. As such, family practice residents, emergency medicine residents, physician's assistants, and nurse practitioners could conceivably participate in this exercise.

**Describe the conceptual background (i.e., why and how it was created)**

This pilot program was designed to expose the rehabilitation resident to potential emergencies that might be encountered in the clinical setting of a rehabilitation facility, particularly when on call. Since most residents are transitioning from internal medicine, surgery, or traditional rotating internships, many have not encountered rehabilitation specific emergency situations, and thus might be ill-suited to manage an impending situation alone. In an effort to prevent this uncertainty and potential pitfall in patient care, we are attempting to build a program that includes important complications, events, and diagnoses that are specific to Rehabilitation patients. By using a simulated patient, we can mimic the emergency situation without the stress of dealing with an actual emergency, and thus allow the resident to practice in a safe environment.

The program was modeled after similar cases observed during 3rd year objective structured clinical exams (OSCEs).1 After having seen the way these cases were modeled for medical students, the format was adapted for use in residency by adjusting the summative and formative objectives, as well as the expectations from the learner. The case itself was constructed using model systems statistical population data. For example, spinal cord injury (SCI) is more common in males, and thus we created the patient's character to reflect some of these demographics.2 The scenario was based on characteristics of our associated free standing rehabilitation facility, with a hospital bed, bedside blood pressure cuff, 24 hour nursing staff, etc. Finally, the timing of the scenario was specific to reflect a nighttime challenge to the resident to simulate an "on-call" scenario.

The didactic piece, which was implemented after the scenario and the residents had received feedback, was designed to supplement the experiential learning. The sequencing was purposeful. Theoretically, by providing didactic material after the residents explored the diagnosis in a real time setting, they would be able to contextualize the topics that were discussed in the presentation. The thought was that the material might "stick" better if the residents were able to associate their experiences with supplemental board relevant data. Thus, their overall learning would be enhanced by the combination of both experiential learning and didactic presentation.

**Provide practical implementation advice (e.g., materials needed, length of session, faculty/facilitator needs, preparation needs, tips for success, common pitfalls, etc.)**

The materials needed for this session are relatively few. A hospital bed, foley set (which can be left empty, due to problems with its drainage), a means of fastening the foley to the groin (tape, tucking into tight undergarments), blood pressure cuff mounted to the wall or bedside, bed sheets, pen and paper (for writing blood pressure readings). Standardized patients should wear underwear, hospital socks, and a hospital gown. A clipboard and typed schedule for faculty should be prepared, and headphones and observation/recording equipment present if available. Printouts of the schedule, surveys, and resident evaluations should also be present.

Individual behavioral goals are based on the learning objectives outlined in the general instructions. Residents specifically should be able to recognize AD as a potential emergency situation. They should be able to recognize the common presenting signs and symptoms of AD, know which population is at risk, and the interventions by which to reverse the process. They must be able to demonstrate the pertinent physical exam maneuvers to arrive at a diagnosis, by obtaining vital signs, performing a focused assessment of the situation, and perform the correct sequence of techniques to reverse the process. A focused neurologic exam should include assessment of the cranial nerves, reflexes, gross strength/sensation, range of motion to ascertain tone, and testing for dural tension. A critical element is obtaining vital signs, as residents should know that dangerously high blood pressures can result in adverse patient outcomes. Learners must ascertain the cause of this patient’s autonomic dysreflexia and reverse it effectively. Residents must finally be able to communicate and counsel the patient effectively in self-intervention and prevention strategies. Since this case has a clear cause, a discussion generated after the encounter should include what the resident is to do if no cause can be determined and the patient continues to be symptomatic. This is covered in the didactic presentation given after the encounter (see attached).

Since the scene was to reflect a potential emergency situation requiring quick decisions and resolution, we limited the encounter to 15 minutes. This ultimately proved an adequate length for most residents. Some of them were able to complete the exercise within minutes, others took the entire duration. In terms of overall timeframe, we had residents arrive at 7:30AM for the "pre-briefing" and set up. The encounter started at 8AM and took exactly 2 hours to execute, being that each encounter lasted 15 minutes total. At 10AM, everyone (staff, SPs, residents) met for the de-briefing, which lasted about 45 minutes. After a short break, the didactic presentation was given to residents (11AM-11:30AM), including time for questions. Thirty additional minutes were provided for clean up, and everything ended at 12PM.

We were lucky to have both clinical and non-clinical faculty that helped to facilitate discussions during the "pre-briefing" and de-briefing. The physical medicine and rehabilitation attending (MM) and chief resident (BS) had received no formal training in debriefing after such an event, but we did have the help of one of the SP trainers that has been running simulation cases with medical students for many years. He ensured not only that the case ran smoothly, but also facilitated some of the interactions between the learners and the teachers (both the SPs and the faculty). The de-briefing was modeled after a plus/delta classroom assessment technique.3 In relation to the exercise, we asked residents to focus on what seemed to work to advance their learning, think about their responsibility, and what they should continue to do to learn from the course (plus). We then asked what changes are needed in the course to improve learning for them and what they might need to change to improve their learning in the exercise (delta). The feedback loop was closed with the residents by directly discussing their thoughts during the de-briefing.

Ideally residents should be at the beginning of their PGY-2 year, when they are relatively naive to rehabilitation related diagnoses and serve to benefit most from the educational session. Having the standardized patient prepare blood pressure cards ahead of time is helpful to hand to the resident when they attempt to take the patient's blood pressure. The standardized patient should also be trained in creating a realistic paraplegic patient, and should not take liberties that might jeopardize the validity of the encounter. Such liberties include, but are not limited to, mimicking spasticity, performing activities that they were not trained to do such as transferring, or talking about symptoms that are not written in the case.We attempted to ensureresidents' psychological safety by making feedback anonymous. Rather than singling out individuals, SPs and faculty were instructed to generalize their commentary. Instead of saying "X did not examine the patient properly. When doing an abdominal exam X should have auscultated before percussing the abdomen," faculty was instructed to say "we observed that some residents did not perform the abdominal assessment appropriately. It is necessary to auscultate the abdomen before percussing." Residents were able to anonymously comment on the activity afterward, and were encourage but never forced to speak during the de-briefings. We also mentioned in the pre-briefing that if residents felt uncomfortable engaging in this activity, to speak with one of the faculty privately.

SPs were recruited through the University's simulation center and were actors familiar with medical acting. SPs were trained by the author (BS, a clinician), as familiarity with SCI in the instructor is critical in being able to guide actors in realism. They were trained in one 3 hour session, and were distributed written materials one week prior to the session. During the training session, SPs read through the case, asked questions about content, and then were involved with a set of mock simulations with the author portraying several iterations of students. It is critical that SCI specific verbiage be explained to the SPs, as most SCI patients understand certain aspects of their injury such as "level of injury," completeness of their injury. Extra care should be taken by the instructor to explain these idiosyncrasies that are specific to SCI. SPs had the opportunity to work through issues, ask questions, and optimize their portrayal prior to the actual program. The most challenging aspect for the SPs to master was their ability to decompensate. The progression from headache to unconscious requires a finesse that the SPs ultimately performed very well, but it took several tries to master the spectrum. Neither of the two hired SPs had any emotional issues with the case, however, this is conceivably a concern. We did make sure to ask whether or not the SP would be comfortable with residents examining the groin area (even though they would be covered), and with portraying a serious and disabling injury such as SCI before hiring them for this particular role. We advise making sure that SPs are comfortable with both of these factors prior to training them.

**Identify limitations of the submission and the author's ideas for expanding or improving the submission**

This particular case was an exploratory pilot program, and thus we identified several areas for expansion and improvement for future use. Some specific areas of concern are discussed subsequently. One of the most troublesome factors that played out in the pilot was residents' lack of initiation. For example, a couple of residents said they would have the nurse "change the foley," but took no action to look at the foley themselves. The standardized patients admitted afterward that they were confused by the situation, since no nurse was present to change the foley, and they were instructed to decompensate if the foley was not addressed in a timely manner. They acted appropriately, and acted as if the foley had not been changed, attempting to prompt the resident to take action themselves. In future, since we can anticipate this potential reaction, SPs can be prepared to subvert resident inactivity by telling them something like "well, the nurse isn't here right now, is she? Just help me out doc!" or something to this nature. One resident also had trouble with diagnosis and more or less "gave up" when the SP passed out. This resident subsequently left the encounter to dial "911," which would be an appropriate course of action at a free-standing rehabilitation hospital without acute care, but his/her reaction did not test his/her ability to problem solve. In future we should explain that it is the responsibility of the resident to attempt to find and reverse the problem as best they can, and that in the case of a "code" it would be assumed that the proper authorities would be notified.

In relation to codes, unfortunately we were not able to utilize a simulator (we have the Sim 3G® model) as we had initially written in the instructions. For the purposes of a first introduction to this type of activity, we simplified the encounter. In future, we would like residents to be able to run a code and anticipate the use of a simulator such as the Sim 3G® in lieu of the SP simply "passing out" and becoming unresponsive, as occurred in this trial. The justification in using such a simulator would be that PM&R residents need to stay current with emergency management, as it is not a common occurrence in most rehabilitation settings, but necessary for all physicians to know. We envisioned the computerized simulator to act solely for residents to practice codes, and thus the SP should be counseled when to direct residents to the simulator, but does not need to interact with the simulator in any way.

Faculty and SP observations reinforced the positive feedback from the residents. Some pertinent observations are discussed subsequently. The observing faculty felt that residents generally either knew what to do right away, or struggled with diagnosis: there was generally no "middle ground." Those residents that quickly ascertained the diagnosis were able to effectively manage the patient. Those that could not figure out what was going on usually stumbled through the entire encounter. Most residents struggled with counseling patients in prevention of AD, either because they did not know themselves, or were uncomfortable with the knowledge that they had. Most residents were able to follow the standard exam "paradigm" of a focused history and physical examination¸ and had good physical examination technique. Some of the residents played into the fantasy elements of the encounter (e.g. that there was a nurse outside the room), while others were concrete. The SPs thought that the residents overall did a good job in reacting to their pain and being empathetic. Further, even if residents could not figure out what to do, they generally reacted in a human way and treated the SPs fairly. The SPs also agreed that the residents' physical examination skills were proficient and that their history questions were usually on target.

Another unavoidable limitation was the lack of resident activity while waiting for their turns to participate. In future, we hope to bolster more cases that could be running simultaneously so that residents would be involved in multiple cases at once, and not have to wait 1 hour to participate, and then wait until the end to review everything. Otherwise, hiring more SPs to accommodate the number of residents would remedy this idleness. Additionally, there were only 8 residents that were used in the program. Due to this small number of participants, we cannot adequately appraise the overall efficacy of the course.

In this pilot program, the SP simply "passed out" when the resident did not perform the requisite countermeasures, however, this does not adequately test a resident's response to an emergency situation. Being able to subsequently run a code would be an effective adjunct to this exercise, and the case was originally written to encompass the use of simulation technology as such (see above). Unfortunately, due to the complexity of planning, it was foregone on this occasion. In future, it would be helpful to add this facet of the case.

Literature similar to this current study is lacking. In one 1998 study assessing knowledge about women with tetraplegia of internal medicine (IM) and obstetric and gynecology (OB/GYN) residents found that only 7% of IM residents and 14% of OB/GYN residents knew to ask about autonomic dysreflexia in a female tetraplegic's history.4 Another study looking at simulation training for rehabilitation nurses found "evidence that high fidelity simulation versus traditional problem based learning provides a greater sustained effect on confidence, competence and comfort levels among experienced rehabilitation nurses."5 AD was a subject area studied. No simulation training for PM&R resident physicians specific to autonomic dysreflexia has been reported in the literature, however, there is a simulation exercise published on MedEd Portal for anesthesia trainees designed to expose the anesthesia trainee to AD during uterine manipulation or ankle fracture.6 Its design and purpose differ strongly from our own, as anesthesiologists do not routinely work with patients with SCI.

Overall this exercise ran smoothly, and all residents thought it was a good use of their time. In their assessments of the activity in the post-quiz, as well as during the debriefing, residents were positive and all would participate in this type of activity again. With the promise of expansion, residents felt this would be a strong adjunct to their orientation into a Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation residency. The ultimate goal is to incorporate additional "rehabilitation emergency" cases to be performed concomitantly with this AD case to offer the new resident a variety of training exercises at the same time. As aforementioned, this program is easily adaptable to a number of residency programs, most notably internal medicine, emergency medicine, general surgery, neurosurgery and family practice. But this program may also be expanded into non-physician medical training, for example, rehabilitation nursing, physical and occupational therapy training, etc. We feel that it is a versatile exercise that has the potential to be a powerful illustration of a frequently mismanaged subpopulation.
