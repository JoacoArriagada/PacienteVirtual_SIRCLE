**Describe the conceptual background (i.e., why and how it was created)**

This pilot program was designed to expose the rehabilitation resident to potential emergencies that might be encountered in the clinical setting of a rehabilitation facility, particularly when on call.

The scenario was based on characteristics of our associated free standing rehabilitation facility, with a hospital bed, bedside blood pressure cuff, 24 hour nursing staff, etc. Finally, the timing of the scenario was specific to reflect a nighttime challenge to the resident to simulate an "on-call" scenario.

**Provide practical implementation advice (e.g., materials needed, length of session, faculty/facilitator needs, preparation needs, tips for success, common pitfalls, etc.)**

The materials needed for this session are relatively few. A hospital bed, foley set (which can be left empty, due to problems with its drainage), a means of fastening the foley to the groin (tape, tucking into tight undergarments), blood pressure cuff mounted to the wall or bedside, bed sheets, pen and paper (for writing blood pressure readings). Standardized patients should wear underwear, hospital socks, and a hospital gown. A clipboard and typed schedule for faculty should be prepared, and headphones and observation/recording equipment present if available. Printouts of the schedule, surveys, and resident evaluations should also be present.

Since the scene was to reflect a potential emergency situation requiring quick decisions and resolution, we limited the encounter to 15 minutes. This ultimately proved an adequate length for most residents.

Ideally residents should be at the beginning of their PGY-2 year, when they are relatively naive to rehabilitation related diagnoses and serve to benefit most from the educational session. Having the standardized patient prepare blood pressure cards ahead of time is helpful to hand to the resident when they attempt to take the patient's blood pressure. The standardized patient should also be trained in creating a realistic paraplegic patient, and should not take liberties that might jeopardize the validity of the encounter. Such liberties include, but are not limited to, mimicking spasticity, performing activities that they were not trained to do such as transferring, or talking about symptoms that are not written in the case.

SPs were recruited through the University's simulation center and were actors familiar with medical acting. SPs were trained by the author (BS, a clinician), as familiarity with SCI in the instructor is critical in being able to guide actors in realism. They were trained in one 3 hour session, and were distributed written materials one week prior to the session. During the training session, SPs read through the case, asked questions about content, and then were involved with a set of mock simulations with the author portraying several iterations of students. It is critical that SCI specific verbiage be explained to the SPs, as most SCI patients understand certain aspects of their injury such as "level of injury," completeness of their injury. Extra care should be taken by the instructor to explain these idiosyncrasies that are specific to SCI. SPs had the opportunity to work through issues, ask questions, and optimize their portrayal prior to the actual program. The most challenging aspect for the SPs to master was their ability to decompensate. The progression from headache to unconscious requires a finesse that the SPs ultimately performed very well, but it took several tries to master the spectrum.

**Identify limitations of the submission and the author's ideas for expanding or improving the submission**

One of the most troublesome factors that played out in the pilot was residents' lack of initiation. For example, a couple of residents said they would have the nurse "change the foley," but took no action to look at the foley themselves. The standardized patients admitted afterward that they were confused by the situation, since no nurse was present to change the foley, and they were instructed to decompensate if the foley was not addressed in a timely manner. They acted appropriately, and acted as if the foley had not been changed, attempting to prompt the resident to take action themselves. In future, since we can anticipate this potential reaction, SPs can be prepared to subvert resident inactivity by telling them something like "well, the nurse isn't here right now, is she? Just help me out doc!" or something to this nature.

In relation to codes, unfortunately we were not able to utilize a simulator (we have the Sim 3G® model) as we had initially written in the instructions. For the purposes of a first introduction to this type of activity, we simplified the encounter. In future, we would like residents to be able to run a code and anticipate the use of a simulator such as the Sim 3G® in lieu of the SP simply "passing out" and becoming unresponsive, as occurred in this trial. The justification in using such a simulator would be that PM&R residents need to stay current with emergency management, as it is not a common occurrence in most rehabilitation settings, but necessary for all physicians to know. We envisioned the computerized simulator to act solely for residents to practice codes, and thus the SP should be counseled when to direct residents to the simulator, but does not need to interact with the simulator in any way.
