# PURPOSE:

The following standardized patient case was developed to assess patient centered counseling skills for long acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) methods, low health literacy communication skills, and appropriate use of the Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use.(1,2) This author could not identify any published standardized patient in English for evaluation of contraceptive counseling skills.\* A few unpublished tools focused only on communicating the risks and benefits of oral contraceptives.

The APGO Medical Student Educational Objectives, 9th Edition, specify that third year medical students should attain a “shows how” level of competency for counseling regarding the risks, benefits and use of each method of contraception.(3) Accordingly, methods of evaluation include standardized patients, direct observation and simulation. Both domestic and international family planning policy makers and experts assert that increasing the evidence based use of long acting reversible contraception is necessary for reducing unintended pregnancy and abortion domestically and maternal morbidity and mortality internationally. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends that “…long-acting reversible methods should be recommended as first line contraceptive methods and encouraged for most women.”(4)

Among the barriers to increasing LARC use is provider misinformation.(4) The WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria, and now the companion CDC version adapted for use in the US,(1,2) provide shorthand continuously updated evidence based clinical practice guidelines freely available through the internet. Their appropriate use is easily taught to third year medical students, residents and practicing physicians in any specialty. As physicians in any specialty serving reproductive aged women and couples will provide contraceptive counseling and advice, provider familiarity with this resource during training can improve care.

As the burden of unintended pregnancy and abortion in the United States now falls disproportionately to poor and uninsured women, appropriate contraceptive counseling requires strong limited health literacy communication skills, assessment of which is included in this checklist.

# Development:

The case and checklists were written by the first author, and reviewed by 5 national colleagues working in family planning and reproductive health, including Dr. Eve Espey who serves on the APGO Undergraduate Medical Education Committee and the ACOG Long-Acting Reversible Contraception Working Group. Other reviewers were Dr. Andrew Kaunitz (University of Florida), Dr. Christopher Estes (University of Miami), Dr. Justine Wu (UMDMJ Medical School), and Dr. Steven Sondheimmer (University of Pennsylvania). All reviewers found the standardized patient realistic and appropriately detailed. Their recommended changes were for greater detail in risks, benefits and efficacy for the methods and reassignment of some competencies to a higher trainee level. A table with more detail on the risks, benefits and efficacy specifying resident vs. student competence accompanies these materials.

The case was used to assess medical students at the end of third year comprehensive OSCE assessment at the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine in June 2010. The ob/gyn clerkship curriculum for these students included specific instruction and practice in low health literacy communication skills evaluated in a separate required clerkship OSCE, the use of the WHO MEC, and an unobserved contraceptive counseling encounter with an actual patient followed by discussion and feedback with an attending or resident.

The standardized patients were trained to play the patient and provide feedback with a faculty member (the first author) playing first a competent student examinee and then an incompetent examinee. A faculty member was present; feedback was given immediately after the encounter by the faculty and the standardized patient together.

The OSCE was conducted in actual examination rooms with the patient sitting in a chair. The examiner pointed out to students upon entering the room the props available which included standard industry models of the LngIUS, the CopperT, the etonorgestrel implant, a package of birth control pills, a vaginal contraceptive ring, and the contraceptive patch. Also available was the WHO MEC “wheel”. Given the recent issue from the CDC of the Medical Eligibility Criteria for the US, the better alternative would now be the one page version freely available in both color and black/ white versions at: http://www.cdc.gov/reproductivehealth/UnintendedPregnancy/USMEC.htm.
