# Case 3

**HPI: A 65-year-old man was recently hospitalized after his first generalized tonic-clonic seizure. He presents to the ER 2 weeks after discharge with fever, jaundice and a rash. He reports that he has been taking all of his medications as prescribed. He has not been around any sick contacts. He denies abdominal pain.**

**PMHx:** New diagnosis of seizure disorder, otherwise none

**PSHx:** none

**Family history:** noncontributory

**Social history:** No alcohol, tobacco, or illicit drugs

**Medications:** He was discharged on several new medications, including carvedilol, simvastatin, phenytoin, a multivitamin, and levothyroxine.

**Exam:**

**General -** he appears well.

**Vitals -** Temperature 38.1 C, HR 85, RR 20, BP 125/80.

**HEENT -** His sclerae are icteric, mucous membranes are moist.

**CV –** normal

**Chest –** clear to auscultation bilaterally

**Abdomen -** soft, normal bowel sounds, nontender, no organomegaly.

**Skin -** His skin is faintly jaundiced with an erythematous macular rash.

**Laboratory testing reveals a white blood cell count of 15,000 cells/mcL (15% eosinophils), AST 750 IU/L, ALT 800 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase 400 IU/L, total bilirubin 5 mg/dL. INR was 1.5.**

**An ultrasound of the right upper quadrant revealed no bile duct abnormalities.**

**Question 1: Use the website** [**https://livertox.nlm.nih.gov**](https://livertox.nlm.nih.gov) **to search his new medications. For each medication, Livertox describes the types of liver injury that have been established. Which one is most likely responsible for this presentation?**

Phenytoin is the typical drug that causes this presentation.

This patient likely has DRESS syndrome, which is an acronym for “Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms.” This syndrome was originally described in patients taking phenytoin. The syndrome involves fever, morbilliform rash, and variable hematologic, hepatic, pulmonary, cardiac, and renal manifestations.1 The rash can involve mucous membranes and can be associated with significant edema. Hepatic involvement can range from a mild acute hepatitis to hepatic necrosis, which is a major contribution to the 10% mortality rate in DRESS. Treatment involves stopping the offending agent and supportive care. DRESS is similar to Stevens-Johnson Syndrome/Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (SJS/TEN). However, SJS/TEN tends to have more mucosal involvement and does not have eosinophilia.2

**Question 2: Search LiverTox for “anticonvulsants.” What drug might you recommend as a replacement for phenytoin that would be unlikely to produce the same reaction but that treats generalized tonic-clonic seizures?**

1. **Levetiracetam**
2. **Lamotrigine**
3. **Carbamazepine**
4. **Phenobarbital**

Searching livertox for “anticonvulsants” brings up a nice summary, with a table showing other anticonvulsants and their likelihood scores. Key examples would be:

* Likely to have a similar effect – Carbamazepine, phenobarbital, fosphenytoin
* Unlikely to have a similar effect: levetiracetam
* Unlikely to have a similar effect but may not be effective in this patient: Benzodiazepines (usually use as an adjunctive therapy, limited by tolerance and side effects), gabapentin
* May or may not have a similar effect – Lamotrigine, topiramate (class B&C)
* Valproic acid is not an aromatic anticonvulsant, but it commonly causes several other forms of liver injury. These include acute, severe hepatitis and a Reye-like syndrome. Therefore, it would not be a good choice.
