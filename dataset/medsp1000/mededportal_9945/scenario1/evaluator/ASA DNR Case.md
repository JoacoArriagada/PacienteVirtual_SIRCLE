1. **Acute Intentional Overdose in the Setting of a Signed Do Not Resuscitate Order**
2. Target Audience: residents, nurses and medical students
3. Learning Objectives
4. Primary Objective
   1. Recognize the signs and symptoms of a salicylate overdose
   2. Perform resuscitative actions to stabilize a patient
   3. Understand the treatment and management regarding aspirin overdose
   4. Understand the clinical and ethical concerns surrounding regarding Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) orders
   5. Understand the clinical and ethical concerns regarding the resuscitation of a patient who has attempted suicide.
5. Secondary Objectives
   1. Develop team organization in a crisis situation
   2. Develop team-centered communication skills toward management of a crisis
   3. Develop individual resuscitative skills
   4. Identify the indications for emergent resuscitation in a critically-ill patient
   5. Resolve conflicts with regard to family issues in the emergency department
6. Critical Actions
   1. Organize a medical team with clearly defined roles for all participants
   2. Place the patient on the cardiac monitor and pulse oximeter
   3. Recognize and treat hypotension, e.g., IV fluid bolus and aggressive fluid resuscitation.
   4. Recognition of signs and symptoms of a decompensating patient including vital signs and physical exam findings.
   5. Recognition of signs and symptoms of a salicylate poisoning and manage case based on accepted standards – Fluids, alkalinazation of urine, ICU
   6. Follow proper ACLS protocol
   7. Admit patient to ICU and convey appropriate information to ICU physician.
   8. Articulate thoughts with regard to addressing DNR order in concert with an attempted suicide
   9. Address family concerns and update them on patient status
7. Environment
   1. Lab set up – Emergency Department resuscitation bay
   2. Mannequin Set Up – LaerdalSimMan® or SimMan 3G® placed on a stretcher, lying down with IV access and in a hospital gown.
   3. Props – EKG showing QT prolongation, equipment needed for intubation, EKG showing ventricular fibrillation, pre intubation CXR, IV access, ACLS medications including sodium bicarbonate, Code Cart, laboratory studies.
   4. Distractors –the patient’s adult children have conflicting opinions about the resuscitation: one sibling wants “everything done” whereas the other wants the DNR order honored because “it’s her time”.
   5. This case may be run without a simulator if run similar to an oral board-type case where the scenario leader verbally or through visual aids (say a power point type presentation) articulates the dynamics of the patient’s condition response to treatment.
8. Actors
   1. Paramedic to transition patient to awaiting medical team
   2. Medical team consists of at least three residents and a nurse
   3. Two adult children of the patient
   4. The Patient. The simulation will transition from the patient to the simulation manikin once the case is initiated.
9. Case Narrative:
   1. Scenario background. The patient is a 67 yo Caucasian women and a retired school principle who is brought in by EMS for altered mental status. The patient was diagnosed with metastatic colon cancer a year ago and signed a Do Not Resuscitate/Do Not Intubate (DNR/DNI) order at that time. The patient had been experiencing depression, chronic pain issues and insomnia resulting from her diagnosis. Her depression was treated with duloxetine with added benefit in treating her chronic-pain and zolpidem for her insomnia. For the past few months the patient has been in constant pain, unaddressed by narcotics. Today, she has ingested an unknown quantity of 10 mg tablets of zolpidem and 60 mg tablets of duloxetine. The act of intentional overdose and possible suicide attempt will becomes apparent when the paramedic during his report presents empty pill bottles of duloxetine and zolpidem and an empty bottle that once contained 81 mg aspirin tablets. All bottles were found on a table next to the patient. The patient’s adult children arrive soon thereafter with an active DNR/DNI order.
   2. Patient Background:
      1. ROS – unable to obtain a clear review of systems due to patient condition. The patient’s children can supply some details of the patient’s history.
      2. Past medical history – significant for hypertension and colon cancer.
      3. Surgical history – significant for colon biopsy which showed colon cancer.
      4. Medications –metoprolol, oxycodone/acetaminophen and aspirin as needed for pain, duloxetine and zolpidem.
      5. Allergies – sulfa drugs.
      6. Social History – no history smoking, does not drink, recently retired and lives alone.
      7. Family History – two healthy children who live in town, her husband died last year.
   3. Initial Presentation. The patient is wheeled in by stretcher. EMS was called by the patient’s children who found patient obtunded on the floor at her home. The patient arrives somnolent and poorly responsive. Initially she can answer yes/no questions but is obviously altered (GCS of 11, E2-V4-M5). The paramedic states her blood glucose was 68 mg/dL at the scene and they were able to obtain IV access. After a few minutes into the scenario (say, while attempting to perform an initial history and physical exam and getting the patient on the monitor) the patient decompensates with GCS of 8 (E2-V2-M4).
   4. After the paramedic exits, the patient’s children arrive. One child insists on complete resuscitation and calls the pill bottles to the attention of medical team. The second child is in hold of a signed and up-to-date DNR/DNI order and does not want the patient to be resuscitated. The conflict between the siblings is one of “do everything” versus “it’s her time”.
   5. Second stage of case. Treatment of the patient’s hypotension and tachycardia will lead to worsening hypoxia. The patient becomes obtunded and requires interventions including intubation and aggressive fluid resuscitation using ACLS guidelines and a team-centered approach. A sodium bicarbonate infusion should be initiated when the team understands salicylate poisoning is contributing to her condition. If the patient is intubated and bicarbonate is initiated without too much delay the patient is stabilized (situation 4 in table below). Otherwise, the patient will go into VT and eventually PEA arrest if not treated. Here, the team must also discuss issues of DNR with family and articulate why the patient needs treatment (attempted suicide and as well as a potentially reversible cause of patient’s condition).
   6. Scenario Branch Points. The patient is going to require ACLS-driven management. Specific branch points are outlined in the following table.

1. Instructors Notes
   1. Case Flow Details
      1. The patient will progressively become more tachycardic and hypotensive and hypoxic. If missed or delayed, have a confederate point at the worsening vital signs on cardiac monitor.
      2. Failure to place the patient on continuous cardiac monitor and pulse oximetry should result in a cue by having nurse ask if the team wants to the patient on supplemental oxygen.
      3. If team does not respond to prompts, the patient will become more tachycardic and hypoxic and decompensate (situations 4 and 5 in the above table).
      4. If the team does not ensure two large bore IV’s and resuscitate with IV fluid, cue them by having the nurse mention placement of a small caliber IV by EMS.
      5. If the team does not address the DNR order, have the patient’s children start to get more vocal about why the patient needs resuscitation. One family member could note the DNR is not valid if the patient attempts to commit suicide.

* 1. Move case forward by having patient become hypotensive and progressively more tachycardic, unstable and altered. The patient’s heart rhythm can progress from sinus tachycardia to ventricular tachycardia and eventually PEA arrest as needed. If the case progresses to PEA arrest, allow team to perform two rounds of cardiopulmonary resuscitations (following current ACLS guidelines) prior to return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The patient should recover. Once a call is made to the ICU, they can inform the team they will call appropriate consults including nephrology for possible dialysis.

8. The ethical dilemma: A dilemma exists between the health care providers desire to act for the good of their patient and the individuals expressed wishes not to be resuscitated. The health care providers cannot satisfy both of these ethical principles as any action to satisfy one, runs counter to satisfying the other. Ultimately, there is no correct answer.
   1. Case for intervention: Overiding DNR orders in the setting of a patient’s suicide is based upon the belief the autonomy of the suicide attempter is not legitimate. Opponents of not following DNR orders in this scenario argue that the majority of suicide attempts are irrational acts at a time when the individual does not possess clear decision-making capacity. They argue the person is suffering a treatable mental illness that has temporarily impaired their judgment. Indeed, 90% of suicides are found to be associated with mental illness on postmortem psychological review (Geppert, 2011). By intervening and treating the patient in this SIM, participants inherently assume the mental illness is treatable and temporary and that after recovery (from both the overdose and depression) the patient will no longer want to hurt themselves.
      1. What do the participants think about this in the setting of metastatic colon cancer and chronic pain?
      2. Is this patient’s depression treatable?
   2. Case for maintaining DNR: The principles of autonomy and non-malficence (do-no-harm) are important to this discussion. A DNR order is signed when the individual has a poor quality of life presently, anticipates no medical benefit from CPR, and expects the persistence of a poor quality of life after any resuscitation. The individual’s decision to forgo CPR is based upon an individual’s value system. In this light, the burden of resuscitation may contribute significantly to a patient’s suffering, without a meaningful change in quality of life. The best outcome in this case is a return to a life facing metastatic colon cancer and chronic pain.
      1. Is this the best outcome for our patient?
      2. Will any harm have been done by providing CPR?
      3. Is the emergency department encounter the appropriate place to decide what to do? (Shouldn’t the ICU or a palliative care team sort this out?)
   3. Legal Aspects: Generally physicians have not faced significant legal ramifications when they choose to not comply with DNR wishes. Lynch et al (2008) performed a comprehensive review of such cases and found an absence of substantive legal remedies in either torts of battery and negligence or wrongful living suits. Conversely, numerous successful malpractice lawsuits have been lodged against physicians who choose to abide by a DNR order in the context of a suicide attempt.
      1. Does guidance exist regarding DNR orders in the context of suicide attempts locally?
      2. Do you screen for suicidal ideation prior to issuing a DNR order?
   4. Overall, there are no clear issues as evidenced by the medical-legal literature and existing cases. The dilemma of resolving such issues rests in making joint –decisions with the family, palliative care teams, medical ethics panels and honoring the patient’s wishes as best as possible.
9. The role of health care power of attorney and the general hierarchy of a surrogate decision- maker canbe discussed here. The hierarchy may vary from state to state but generally starts with the patients spouse and ends with an ethics board or court appointee.
