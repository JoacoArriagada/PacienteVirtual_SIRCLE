### NURSE Framework

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | Example | Description |
| Naming | “It sounds like you were really frightened when you got that news.” | State the patient’s emotion |
| Understanding | “I understand how this decision could be very challenging.”  Active listening without interruption.  Follow-up questions to show understanding. | Empathize with/legitimize the emotion |
| Respecting | “The medical team has noted how you are at bedside day and night, caring for your loved one." | Praise the patient for strength |
| Supporting | “It sounds like we have the same primary goal in mind, which is…” | Show support for the patient and their circumstances |
| Exploring | “Is there anything else that is troubling you about this situation?” | Ask the patient to elaborate on the emotion |

CASE 4

**Learning Objectives:**

In this case students will...

**Communication and Interpersonal Skills**

1. Practice NURSE statements and nonverbal communication strategies to de-escalate an angry family member.
2. Elicit and address underlying reasons for a family member’s anger.

**Professionalism:**

1. Empathetically communicate the reasons for avoiding unnecessary testing.
2. Avoid making false promises regarding patient care.
3. Clarify their role on the care team in a respectful, professional manner.

**Student Prompt:**

“You are the intern that admitted a 14-year-old boy with acute right lower quadrant pain. He was evaluated with abdominal ultrasound which showed some trace pelvic free fluid.  This patient was staffed by the pediatric surgeon on call; the child’s plan overnight will be to continue IV fluids and serial abdominal exams, calling the surgical team if the patient acutely worsens.  At 6:45 PM, before you check out, you get a call from the bedside nurse that the family would like to discuss the plan of care.”

Debrief/Common Pitfalls:

* Ask: “Who believes a scenario like this will happen during intern year?”
  + Share any personal examples of patients or family members citing second opinions from medical or peri-medical acquaintances.
  + The involvement of family members with medical (or peri-medical) degrees creates a difficult communication challenge, especially when those individuals are not present to evaluate the patient themselves. When you are a new doctor, it is common to feel flustered when a confident third party disagrees with your plan of care. This is confounded by the fact that families and patients typically trust these third parties’ opinions due to their personal relationships.
  + To be clear, a CT scan would expose this child to unnecessary radiation and agreeing to provide it is against best practice guidelines. We should not agree to substandard care for the sake of de-escalating someone’s anger.
  + You can develop trust with the family member by using NURSE statements to redirect the conversation and unearth their unspoken concerns. (In this person’s case, guilt about overlooking the severity of their child’s symptoms and fear that things will get worse).
* Ask: “How did you handle the family member calling you a student/commenting on your age?”
  + When patients or family members misstate your role (intentionally or unintentionally), it is natural to feel defensive or even offended. In this situation, the goal is to recognize those emotions, put them to the side, then turn the conversation back toward the family’s concerns.
    - Example: “I am Dr. \_\_\_\_\_, a member of the physician team that is responsible for your child’s care. Can you tell me more about your concerns so I can find out how to better help you?”
  + Comments about age can feel particularly intimidating for those still in training. Common strategies for managing ageist remarks include:
    - Ignoring/Restating role: “I am the resident doctor who is responsible for your child’s care. How can I help you?”
    - Humor: “Thanks, I have a great skincare routine,” or “Would you believe I’m in 20th grade?”
    - Joint experience: “Fortunately your child is receiving care from a whole team of doctors, including me, who have years of collective pediatric experience.”
    - The truth (with a neutral, professional tone): “I’m 26. How can I help?”
* Ask: “At what point did you realize that it might be helpful to ask for backup?”
  + Especially during your intern year, it is often helpful to ask your attendings for support, especially if you have tried to reassure families/patients on your own without success.
