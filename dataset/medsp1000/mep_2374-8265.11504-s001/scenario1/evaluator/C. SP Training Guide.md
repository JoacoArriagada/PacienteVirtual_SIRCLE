# CASE 1:

**Learning Objectives:**
In this case students will...

**Communication and Interpersonal Skills**

1. Practice NURSE statements and nonverbal communication strategies to understand and de-escalate a patient’s anger.
2. Avoid inappropriate use of the unqualified phrase “I understand” in patient communication.
3. Formulate a truthful, straightforward response to a patient who requests services that are not available (e.g. a meeting between consultants who are no longer on call).

**Professionalism:**

1. Not discredit members of the team who disagree with each other or with the student’s own clinical assessment.

**Student Prompt:**

“You are the intern taking care of a patient who presented with septic shock secondary to *C. difficile* colitis who is now post-op day #3 following total colectomy.  The patient’s hemodynamics have markedly improved.  Unfortunately, the patient’s renal function has slowly declined from baseline (sCr 1.8). You consult the nephrologist who declares the patient is in end stage renal disease and recommends tunneled dialysis catheter placement and initiation of hemodialysis during this admission. The family understands and agrees to these plans.

Today, a new nephrologist is on service who recommends holding hemodialysis for now in hopes of renal recovery. The new nephrologist tells the patient and spouse this plan. (In terms of objective data, nothing has changed from yesterday. sCr is still 4.5-4.8, potassium is stable, and the patient has low – but still present – urine output).

You walk in the room to update the patient/family about your (the primary team’s) daily plan of care.”

| THE PATIENT’S STORY: | **Treatment Course**: I came to the hospital last week with severe nausea, vomiting, and fatigue. Doctors made the diagnosis of “septic shock from a bacterial colon infection.” I had part of my colon removed as a result. I am feeling much better and have been moved out of the ICU, but the doctors say my kidney numbers are now getting worse. The kidney doctor who came to see me over the weekend recommended I have a line placed in my neck to start dialysis because my kidneys were too damaged to recover.  **Initial Story**(to be used after student responds to your initial statement): *“Today, a new kidney doctor came to see me. She told me we aren’t starting dialysis because she thinks my kidneys might recover. Instead, she said I should stay in the hospital to “watch and wait.” I don’t understand what changed! I feel the same as I did yesterday when the plan was totally different. Then the doctor hurried out of the room before I could collect my thoughts.*  *I feel back to normal. Clearly no one can tell what’s happening with my kidneys anyway. I want to go home right now. Maybe my spouse can bring me back to the hospital tomorrow once you are all on the same page.”*  [Pause for student response]  **If the student successfully uses a NURSE statement**, possible responses include:   * *“I am so confused by all these different doctors coming in and giving plans; it is hard to tell all their names, roles, and specialties apart – especially when they keep changing. It seems like the doctors are contradicting themselves and I am not able to trust the system.”* * *“I had a friend who went on dialysis and died 6 months after. I am afraid the same thing will happen to me. I’m also scared that ‘watching and waiting’ now will only make me sicker later.”*   **If the student says, “I understand,” after you describe how you feel,** react strongly and negatively, e.g., *“How could you possibly know what it is like to be living a normal life, then suddenly be stuck in a hospital, dying from kidney failure, about to leave behind your spouse of 32 years?”* If the student has a real-life experience close to the one in the scenario and thus relates to your situation, highlight the differences between their experience and the one you are going through now.  In contrast, responses like, ***“That is understandable,”***or qualified statements like,***“I understand how this could be overwhelming for you,”***have a different meaning from “I understand” and typically do not elicit a negative response. |

| PROFESSIONALISM ISSUES OR CHALLENGES | If applicable, ask the student, *“Can you set up a care meeting between me and the two kidney doctors?”* (They might even offer this themselves). The student should recognize this is not something in their power to accomplish and should instead reply honestly, stating clearly that this will not be possible. They should recover by redirecting the conversation back to your underlying emotions and fears. **If they do make a false promise to arrange the meeting**, pressure them for more details, asking, *“When will you schedule the meeting? What time should I come back this afternoon?”*  **If the student discredits either of the nephrologists**, ask, *“How am I supposed to trust you as a doctor if you don’t trust your own advisors?”* Avoiding discrediting consultants is important; students should maintain team integrity by respecting differences in clinical decision-making, even when it is uncomfortable to communicate this to patients. |
