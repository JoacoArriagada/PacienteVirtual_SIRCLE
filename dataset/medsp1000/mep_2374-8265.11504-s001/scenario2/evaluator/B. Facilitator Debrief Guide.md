### NURSE Framework

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | Example | Description |
| Naming | “It sounds like you were really frightened when you got that news.” | State the patient’s emotion |
| Understanding | “I understand how this decision could be very challenging.”  Active listening without interruption.  Follow-up questions to show understanding. | Empathize with/legitimize the emotion |
| Respecting | “The medical team has noted how you are at bedside day and night, caring for your loved one." | Praise the patient for strength |
| Supporting | “It sounds like we have the same primary goal in mind, which is…” | Show support for the patient and their circumstances |
| Exploring | “Is there anything else that is troubling you about this situation?” | Ask the patient to elaborate on the emotion |

## CASE 2

**Learning Objectives:**

In this case students will...

**Communication and Interpersonal Skills**

1. Practice NURSE statements and nonverbal communication strategies to understand and de-escalate a family member’s anger.
2. Name and validate the underlying emotions that commonly lead patients or family members to make “all or nothing” statements.

**Professionalism:**

1. Avoid false reassurance and tactfully disclose potential unfavorable outcomes of a pending diagnostic workup.

**Student Prompt:**

“You are the intern caring for a 13-year-old young man admitted for left unilateral hip pain. An MRI showed diffuse non-specific contrast enhancement in the affected femoral head. He underwent a bone biopsy of the affected region early in admission. Now he is being treated with antibiotics for possible chronic osteomyelitis. However, you inform the family that the bone culture and fluid is sterile and unlikely to be true infection.  On rounds today, your attending mentioned a growing fear of malignancy but these concerns have not been shared with the family.  The daily plan of care for the last 72 hours has been to wait until the final bone biopsy is read. You receive a page from the nurse that the mother would like an update on the plan of care.”

Debrief/Common Pitfalls:

* Ask: “How did you respond to the parent’s black-and-white comment that they have received ‘no answers’?”
  + In most cases it is best not to contradict or challenge “no answers” or other blanket statements. These phrases are often signals of underlying distress. People who make these statements are typically not receptive to an objective discussion about laboratory or other system logistics, even though those are daily realities of healthcare. It is better to redirect the conversation to the person’s underlying emotions using tools from the NURSE mnemonic. Examples include:
    - Naming the emotion: “It sounds like you feel afraid that your child still does not have a diagnosis.”
    - Understanding/legitimizing: “I can only imagine how difficult this has been for you to have your child admitted for days without having a final diagnosis.”
    - Respecting: “I want to recognize what an amazing advocate you have been for your child during this uncertain time.”
    - Supporting: "You and I both want to help him feel better as quickly as possible. One of the hardest parts of being in the hospital is the waiting game; we are working hard to complete the workup as soon as we can.”
    - Exploring: “Would you feel comfortable telling me what potential ‘answers’ worry you the most?”
* Ask: “Did anyone discover the family member’s concern about cancer? How did you handle that?”
  + It can be very challenging to communicate a differential diagnosis that includes cancer, especially as a trainee. However, lying about it is never the right answer since this diagnosis is on the differential for a reason: it might turn out to be the correct one.
  + An appropriate response to questions about cancer would be, “We are exploring all possible reasons that your child is so sick. To be thorough, our workup includes looking for cancer. We do not have a final answer yet, but I promise we will give you more information when we have it.”
  + Redirect the conversation to the family member’s emotions. Example: “’The unknown’ can be difficult for many patients and their families – how are you coping with this?” ￼
