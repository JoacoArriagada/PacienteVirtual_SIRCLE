# CASE 2:

**Learning Objectives:**

In this case students will...

**Communication and Interpersonal Skills**

1. Practice NURSE statements and nonverbal communication strategies to understand and de-escalate a family member’s anger.
2. Name and validate the underlying emotions that commonly lead patients or family members to make “all or nothing” statements.

**Professionalism:**

1. Avoid false reassurance and tactfully disclose potential unfavorable outcomes of a pending diagnostic workup.

**Student Prompt:**

“You are the intern caring for a 13-year-old young man admitted for left unilateral hip pain. An MRI showed diffuse non-specific contrast enhancement in the affected femoral head. He underwent a bone biopsy of the affected region early in admission. Now he is being treated with antibiotics for possible chronic osteomyelitis. However, you inform the family that the bone culture and fluid is sterile and unlikely to be true infection.  On rounds today, your attending mentioned a growing fear of malignancy but these concerns have not been shared with the family.  The daily plan of care for the last 72 hours has been to wait until the final bone biopsy is read.

You receive a page from the nurse that the mother would like an update on the plan of care.”

| THE PATIENT’S STORY: | **Treatment Course**: I brought my son Nick to the hospital last week because his hip was extremely painful. Since they admitted him, he has undergone many tests without any conclusive results. The doctors put him on antibiotics in case the bone was infected, but now they are saying the infection tests were negative. A doctor offhandedly mentioned “malignancy” this morning and that’s all I’ve been able to think about since. I want answers!  **Initial Story** (to be used after student responds to your initial statement): ***“****I brought Nick here over the weekend to get some answers about why his hip has been hurting. Since then, you all have told me to keep waiting and waiting, even though you’ve put him through about a hundred needle pokes and procedures. It is ridiculous that we’ve been here for 3 days, yet you all seem to still be running in circles. You said this morning that the infection tests were negative. If it’s not infection, what could it be? I’m sick of having no answers.”*  [Pause for student response]  **If the student successfully uses a NURSE statement,** possible responses include:   * *“I have been so scared for Nick. When I brought him in, I was sure that the hip pain was just some arthritis or something. But I now, I know arthritis would be strange for someone his age and I’m afraid it is something more serious.”* * *“I feel spread so thin – I thought being a single parent/grandparent was already difficult. Now I worry that Nick’s siblings feel forgotten because I’ve been here so much.”*   **If the student contradicts the “no answers” statement,** react negatively, either by dismissing the student, shutting down, or pushing harder. *“Do you have any clue what you’re doing at this point? My kid is getting worse, and you’re just sitting around doing nothing.”* The issue at stake here is the student’s failure to address your underlying emotions, which are anxiety and fear. |

| PROFESSIONALISM ISSUES OR CHALLENGES | **If the student offers false reassurance about cancer workup,** say “*I’m not an idiot – I have access to Nick’s chart. You wrote “possible malignancy” in his note this morning.”* Reasonably, students may feel it is inappropriate to share a possible cancer diagnosis without a completed workup. However, they should not mislead the family if asked directly about it.  An appropriate student response to questions about cancer would be, “We are exploring all possible reasons that your child is so sick. To be thorough, our workup includes looking for cancer. We do not have a definite answer yet. We will be sure to give you more information when we have it.” Some students with high self-efficacy in delivering serious news might even transition the conversation to relay the teams’ concerns about cancer. |
