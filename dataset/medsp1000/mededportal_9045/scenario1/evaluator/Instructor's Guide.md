**Pat Andares – Headache – Instructor’s Guide**

**Resource**: Pat Andares – Headache

**How to use the resource file**: The resource file contains information necessary for training standardized patients, presented in a table format. It describes the case, the way used, contains SP training notes and additional details.

**Purpose of the resource**: To enrich the sources of case scenarios available to standardized patient trainers and medical school faculty for objective structured clinical examinations.

**Educational Objectives**:

* Apply the principles of effective communication to facilitate relationship building, data gathering and patient education
* Use appropriate questioning and physical examination to determine the type and cause of headache
* Perform physical exam, discover and determine type of gait disturbance
* Perform focused neurologic exam
* Write a SOAP note to communicate in a written form the exam findings, differential diagnosis and plan

**Conceptual background**: This case was created for use with standardized patients, for teaching and/or evaluation purposes. The case is intended for teaching/examination of medical students, years 1 through 4 (depending on medical school’s type of curriculum), as well as resident and faculty development.

**Practical implementation advice**: All original materials/patient exam room needs are described in the case, users are free to change the original case to better suit their needs, as long as due credit is applied.

How long the SP training would take depends on the SP pool, how often each SP portrays different roles, and the longevity of their experiences. In my practice, I trained each SP cohort (all SPs to be used during the year) upfront at employee appointment, and additionally every year on the following:

* Policies and Procedures – 1 hr
* Normal PE – 2 hrs
* Abnormal PE – 3 hrs
* Giving feedback to students – 2 hrs

Gait abnormalities, muscle strength abnormalities, droopy mouth, DTR (increased, decreased), as well as Babinski are taught and practiced by the SPs during the “Abnormal PE” session, and little time is needed to remember how this is done during the training for this particular case.

For the first training in abnormal PE, it is best if a person with medical education (MD, DO or RN) demonstrates the presentations; for the case training, an experienced SP educator (with or without medical background) would be an excellent resource.

The SPs in my pool had continuous engagement in role-playing, with at least one 5-hour teaching period every month, often playing every week. This preserved their skills and decreased the needed number of SPs in the pool. In result, for each and every new case throughout the year, a training session of only 1 hour (with the group of all SPs portraying the same case) was necessary. I presented this new methodology (“the one-hour SP training approach”) as a workshop at the 2011 ASPE Conference. The workshop received excellent reviews.

Guidelines for neurologic examination – The SP training was guided by the Schwartz’s textbook; however, there are many textbooks in clinical examination, neurology examination and SP training that could help. For SP training, I would recommend the classic book “Training Standardized Patients to Have Physical Findings” by Howard S. Barrows (edition 1999).

Ability to implement the case to get reliable scores – The SPs enjoyed the training for this case and its complexity. They shared openly their satisfaction with the training, and on the “training feedback form,” including a series of questions about the quality and quantity of the training; there were no requests for additional training. On a continuous basis at the originating program, inter-rater reliability was assessed, and for 20 consecutive cases at the Center, it was above 80%. Additionally, a case study was completed to review the inter-rater reliability between SPs and faculty-observers in a multiple-station high-stakes OSCE examination; results indicated faculty and SPs matched in scoring; manuscript is under preparation.

Can SPs reliably portray the symptoms?– Some of the SP educators may hesitate given the complexity of the case. My answer is: yes, the SPs were able to and enjoyed portraying this case. No deviations were reported by the “peer reviewer” SP observing colleague’s portrayal of the case, nor from the randomized faculty review of cases for quality assurance.

Questions about items on the checklist may also arise; I am presenting several Q&A clarifications:

#1 – Does student have to do all three to get a yes or are those examples of what can be done? – *All three must be done.*

#3 – How does SP know if student develops a chronologic time-line of patient complaint? – *This is trained by teaching the SP to pay attention to student’s summary of history of present illness: when the complaint started, how it developed over time, and what precipitated the visit to the office, repeated back to the SP by the student.*

#4 – Do they need to ask just one of those to get credit? – *Yes, “or” indicates any of the listed.*

#13 – Walk back and forth? Is this testing normal gait only? – *If the student asks the patient to walk in the office, the patient will display their hemiplegic gait.*

Please, remember that the SP educators are invited to make some changes to the information or checklist, to be able to use it better for their own program’s educational needs.

**Use of section “Standardizing the Unknown”**: This section is intended for use when training multiple SPs for portraying the same case. Its purpose is two-prong:

1. It is very hard to predict and standardize the answers to each and every question learners might decide to ask of the SP. Having more details to the portrait makes the character more complete, and the SPs can easier associate with and think as the patient portrayed.
2. The details referred to in this section might be quite different for different states, cities and schools. These questions are intended for the group of SPs to decide on. This gives them the chance to partake in the creation of the portrayal, bring local detail to the role, and take ownership of the role. The SP trainers should feel free to add/delete questions in this section to make it most applicable to their local specific circumstances.

In my experience with adding this section to the SP case, it takes less than 10 minutes of allotted training time for the SP group to decide on the details, and on numerous occasions has meaningfully contributed to the case presentation. Usually, the last question related to “What else would you like to know about this person?” brings up any parts of the role that might have not been clear or well described during training.

The notion of training standardized patients in the context of a role, rather than for specific interrogative props is gaining momentum across the nation and is considered a possibility for SP training for the USMLE Step 2 CS examination. Karen Lewis, Board of Directors Member for the Association for Standardized Patient Educators (ASPE), ASPE Publications Committee member and Director of the SP Program at George Washington University, wrote in the September 2011 issue of “ASPE News”: “In my experience, simulated activities where the SP can perform within the confines of a behavior profile almost always accomplish better learning for our students, clearer educator understanding of what they have learned, and more informed ideas about the next steps.”

**How to use the “Example SOAP Note”**

The example written note is meant as a possible guide for completeness of information inclusion. While the parts of a SOAP note are standardized, the individual writing styles are varied, and we cannot stipulate that there is only one correct way to complete the SOAP note.

The “Example SOAP Note,” if used in a teaching context, should be provided to the students to compare with their own notes, and help them in organizing the note. When the SOAP note is used for evaluative purposes, there are a number of ways in which it could be utilized, depending on the faculty expectations, student learning level and desired complexity. Traditionally, the faculty designing the evaluative experience has the freedom and responsibility to decide on these details.

One simplified schedule for SOAP note evaluation I have used with novice learners includes:

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **#** | **Scoring Rubric** | **Yes** | **No** |
| 1 | Information content was recorded within the appropriate part of the note; i.e. subjective information was recorded in section “History” etc. |  |  |
| 2 | Information content was recorded correctly; i.e. all facts were correct, examinations not performed were not recorded, etc. |  |  |
| 3 | Differential diagnosis list logically followed (was supported by) the recorded information |  |  |
| 4 | Immediate diagnostic workup list logically supported the considered differential diagnosis list |  |  |
| 5 | The note was well organized, was written with clarity and without typos |  |  |

This scoring schedule could be the starting point for any faculty wishing to further personalize the assessment of SOAP notes within OSCE exams, especially for cases used with more advanced learners. Also, SP educators and clinical skills faculty are welcome to use the scoring rubric included at the end of this case.

**Recommended length of session:** 15 minutes for learner in patient room, 10 minutes for patient note, 30 min for debriefing with faculty-observer (for teaching purposes only).

The debriefing session may include:

* Discussion of what went well
* Discussion of what could be improved
* Review of questions that presented difficulty for the student(s)
* Specific recommendations on:
* Time management
* Patient examination
* Data gathering
* Closing of the encounter with provision of summary to the patient

In simulated environments, the most learning occurs during the debriefing of a scenario. In general, it is recommended that the length of the debriefing session is as long as or longer than the time devoted to patient encounter and note writing. Such debriefing may be purposefully omitted if the case is used for high-stakes exams.

Case has been successfully implemented with students at Paul Foster SOM.
