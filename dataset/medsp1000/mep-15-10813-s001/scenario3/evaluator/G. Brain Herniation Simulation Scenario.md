**Ideal Scenario Flow**

The Lead Fellow and Support Fellow enter the room. The ICU nurse provides a brief history of present illness, and the Lead Fellow asks the ICU intern to briefly review the patient’s Emergency Department presentation, labs, and clinical course. The Support Fellow is asked to examine the patient and reports the patient is comatose. The Lead Fellow recognizes the need for airway securement and asks the Support Fellow to prepare for intubation. The Lead Fellow also voices the differential diagnosis for acute coma, including concern for herniation in the setting of traumatic head injury, hypertension, and bradycardia. Neurosurgery assistance is requested and interventions to lower intracranial pressure are implemented, including raising the head of bed and administering hyperosmolar therapy.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. Uncertainty about doses of medications: Some learners may be uncomfortable with dosing of induction agents or dosing of hyperosmolar therapies. One option is to have the intern ask if they should call the pharmacist for recommendations, leave the room, and return shortly thereafter with the appropriate dosing information.
2. Lack of identification of herniation syndrome: Learners may not consider herniation and may instead respond to hypertension with anti-hypertensives, which would be anticipated to worsen this patient’s condition.
3. Failure to identify need for intubation: The learners may be intently focused on the neurologic decompensation of the patient and fail to recognize the patient is no longer protecting his airway nor ventilating adequately. By Act 3, the respiratory therapist should prompt the team to consider intubation if they have not yet discussed this.

Intubation Strategy

* Manipulation and stimulation of the upper airway can lead to reflexive increases in ICP
* The longer the laryngoscopic manipulation, the greater the sympathetic nervous system stimulation, so gentle technique and limitation of multiple laryngoscopy attempts is important
* Awake fiberoptic intubation is the gold standard for patients with elevated ICP and known or high suspicion for difficult airway
* Rapid sequence intubation (RSI) is the preferred management technique for routine intubations in patients with elevated ICP
  + Induction and neuromuscular blockade is important even for comatose patients, as the reflexive sympathetic response to airway stimulation can still occur
  + Fentanyl can be used prior to induction to blunt some of the reflexive sympathetic responses
  + Studies on pretreatment with intravenous lidocaine having conflicting results as to whether it prevents ICP increases during intubation
  + Etomidate is recommended as first-line induction agent for patients with elevated ICP, though ketamine is a reasonable alternative
  + Succinylcholine is recommended for neuromuscular blockade; there is no reliable evidence showing it causes an increase in ICP during the fasciculation phase
  + Non-depolorizing neuromuscular blocking agents, such as rocuronium, can be used but there is no evidence of superiority over depolarizing agents
* Patient positioning
  + The head and neck of the patient should be kept in a neutral position to optimize cerebral venous drainage
  + Limit the time spent flat to as briefly as possible, or consider intubating in the semi-upright position (30 degrees), to limit increases in ICP associated with positional changes

Other Interventions:

* Elevate the head of the bed above 30 degrees to facilitate cerebral venous drainage
* Hyperventilate (target PaCO2 30-35mmHg) – generally not recommended; produces transient effect only and causes vasoconstriction which can lead to further cerebral ischemia
* Decrease metabolic rate with sedation and/or barbiturates, and avoid hyperthermia
* Decompressive craniectomy benefit is unclear for diffuse traumatic brain injury, but helpful to evacuate mass lesions
