**Ideal Scenario Flow**

The Lead Fellow and Support Fellow enter the room. The ICU nurse provides a brief history of present illness, and the Lead Fellow asks the ICU intern to briefly review the patient’s Emergency Department presentation, labs and clinical course. The Support Fellow is asked to examine the patient and reports the patient is confused but otherwise has a non-focal neurologic exam. The Lead Fellow voices a differential diagnosis for acutely altered mental status, recognizing severe hyponatremia and signs of possible lung malignancy on the chest radiograph, increasing suspicion for SIADH as the underlying etiology. When the patient begins to seize, hypertonic saline therapy is requested. When there is a delay in hypertonic saline arrival and the patient continues to seize, sodium bicarbonate is administered and repeated when seizures continue. Once the patient becomes obtunded and hypoxemic, intubation is performed. When hypertonic saline arrives, it is administered at a sufficient dose and Neurology consultation is requested.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. Uncertainty about doses of medications: Some learners may be uncomfortable with dosing of induction agents or dosing of hypertonic saline. One option is to have the intern ask if they should call the pharmacist for recommendations, leave the room, and return shortly thereafter with the appropriate dosing information.
2. Lack of awareness that sodium bicarbonate is a hypertonic therapy: Many learners will be unaware that sodium bicarbonate, which is readily available in most ICUs, is a hyperosmolar solution that can be used to acutely increase the serum sodium. This is especially critical in the situation where significant time will elapse while pharmacy is preparing hypertonic saline solutions. If the learners are unaware of this use of sodium bicarbonate, then the nurse can mention that they have seen it used before to raise the sodium and suggest its use to the Lead Fellow.
3. Failure to recognize metabolic etiologies of seizure: Learners may not consider metabolic causes of seizure, such as electrolyte disarray, during the scenario. If this is the case, the nurse can prompt the lead fellow by asking if they would like a glucose and electrolyte panel checked.

Debriefing Materials: Hyponatremia

Metabolic causes of seizure should always be considered in refractory seizures and, when uncovered, treated aggressively.

* Hypo- or hypernatremia
* Hypo- or hyperglycemia
* Hypo- or hypercalcemia
* Hypomagnesemia
* Withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines
* Drug intoxication
* Hyperammonemia/acute liver failure

Seizures due to hyponatremia

* Usually due to a precipitous drop in serum sodium concentration; this patient with SIADH was given normal saline, which worsened his hyponatremia acutely.
* Emergent treatment:
  + 3% saline 100-150 mL infused over 10 minutes, can be repeated (total of 300 mL) OR continuous infusion at 0.5-2 mL/kg/hr
  + Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) 100mEq, which is two 50 ml ampules each containing 1mEq/ml NaHCO3, is equivalent to the osmolarity of 5.8% sodium chloride and can be given as a temporizing measure1
* Central venous access device administration is preferred over peripheral administration of 3% or higher concentration saline, and is to be used if available; however, in emergent situations peripheral IV administration can be used with relatively little risk of adverse effects 2
* Goal: raise serum sodium by 4-6 mEq/L initially, which typically relieves neurologic symptoms
* Additional correction of hyponatremia beyond the 24-hour goal of 6-8 mEq increases the risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome
* Long-term treatment will depend on the underlying cause of hyponatremia

Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)

* The brain can adapt to slow, chronic changes to serum tonicity through several mechanisms
* Osmotic demyelination syndrome (formally called central pontine myelinolysis) can occur when there is a rapid fall in serum tonicity, leading to water movement into the brain
* ODS can result in severe neurologic disability, including “locked in” syndrome and coma, that are often irreversible
* Major risk factors for ODS include presenting sodium ≤ 105 mEq/L, hyponatremia that develops over a few hours, and overly rapid rate of correction of ≥ 8 mEq/L
* Because of the risk of ODS, the rate of correction of hyponatremia should not exceed 6-8 mEq in a 24-hour period
