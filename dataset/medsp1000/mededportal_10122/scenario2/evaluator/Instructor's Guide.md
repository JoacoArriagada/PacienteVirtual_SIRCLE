**INSTRUCTOR’S GUIDE**

**“Do You Want Us to Do Everything?” Teaching Residents to Discuss Resuscitation with Hospitalized Patients**

**Educational Objectives**

While training in end-of-life communication has become increasingly common in both undergraduate and graduate medical education, there clearly remains a continued significant educational gap. Programs to teach residents end-of-life communication skills, including resuscitation status discussions, need to be further disseminated. We implemented a feasible 3-hour workshop designed to teach interns code status discussion skills which includes a didactic component, a suggested framework for conducting a CSD, role-play, and competency-based assessment and feedback.

**Role Plays:**

The role-play cases (Appendix A) were developed by three experienced faculty and are based on their own clinical experiences. Over time, based on the learners’ feedback, the instructors developed several modifications of the initial role-play in order to explore different aspects of difficult communication (for example, responding to patient/family emotions, dealing with conflict) and to prevent the role-play from becoming rote. While a particular role-play can be used repeatedly in the same workshop we have found that residents prefer varied scenarios.

The use of standardized patients, who are trained to produce a scripted clinical scenario with as little variability as possible, may create a more consistent experience for all residents during role-play simulation and debriefing and is an option for this workshop. While standardized patients have been shown to be effective for training communication skills, they are expensive educational tools. In our institution we found it more feasible to use two experienced faculty (in addition to the facilitator) to play the roles of the family members Sara and John.

Following the presentation, the faculty members demonstrate a code status discussion in role-model role play format **(10-15 minutes)**.

Learners then take turns playing the role of the physician with the faculty facilitator and the other learners acting as “observers.” The faculty facilitator completes the competency-based evaluation form (Appendix B) and provides it to the resident at the end of the session. In general we limit the role plays to approximately 10-15 minutes each with an additional 10 minutes for debriefing and feedback. Learners are encouraged to call a “time out” at any time if they feel they are having difficulties and would like to re-direct or be re-directed. Faculty may also call a “time out” and re-direct the learner if needed. **(20-25 minutes per learner).** Learners are provided a “Code Status Discussion Checklist” (Appendix E) to refer to during the role-plays.

**Debriefing:**

Debriefing, or “after-action review,” is the practice of reviewing an encounter or event with the goal of identifying lessons that might improve the next one. After participating in a simulated case, debriefing allows clinicians to reflect. The goals of the facilitator are to help participants understand, analyze, and synthesize what they thought, felt, and did during the simulation in order to improve future performance in similar situations. Debriefing with each learner after the CPR role play and before providing specific competency-based feedback is an essential component of the workshop as it allows the resident time to reflect, providing a framework to better incorporate feedback. Rudolph et. al. have published an excellent model of debriefing as formative assessment.

Basic questions to ask the participating learner in the debriefing process include:

* How did that go?
* What did you think went well?
* What was challenging? (include discussion of patient and physician emotions)
* What could you have done differently/what else could you try?
* Why did you choose to stop in those places?

Observers can answer the following questions after the learner reflects:

* What did you think went well?
* What did you think was challenging?
* What would you have done differently?

**Feedback**

The faculty facilitators and resident observers can also provide specific feedback using the competency-based checklist (Appendix E). The facilitating faculty member completes a competency-based evaluation form on each learner (Appendix B) and provides it to the resident at the end of the session.
