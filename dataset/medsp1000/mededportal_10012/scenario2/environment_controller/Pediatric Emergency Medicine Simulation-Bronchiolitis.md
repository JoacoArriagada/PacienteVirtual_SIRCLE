**Scenario Overview**

This scenario teaches learners to recognize and manage bronchiolitis in the emergency setting.

The scenario is simulation-based, designed for manikin-based simulation equipment, with an integrated team communication focus.

Learners will participate in a simulation scenario and be asked to identify the signs and symptoms of bronchiolitis and their management (See Appendix A).

Two versions of this scenario are presented. The first version presents respiratory distress secondary to bronchiolitis which meets criteria for the use of high flow nasal canula oxygen therapy or nasal CPAP. The second version of the scenario presents respiratory failure secondary to bronchiolitis which requires intubation. The selection of which version is used should be based on individual learning objectives and availability of different therapies.

At the end of training, the learners should be able to recognize the seriousness of the situation and manage a full resuscitation team. The primary complications for the team to recognize and manage are respiratory distress and impending respiratory failure.

**Educational Rationale on How the Scenario Generalizes to Real-Life Circumstances**

Bronchiolitis is a viral lower respiratory disease found in children less than 24 months old. The lower respiratory tract symptoms are typically preceded and accompanied by rhinorrhea and congestion. It is often due to infection by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) but can also be caused by other viral pathogens. Bronchiolitis is a significant cause of pediatric morbidity; in 2009 it resulted in an estimated 128,000 hospitalizations.1

A subset of patients with bronchiolitis experience severe respiratory symptoms requiring aggressive ventilatory support, with 7-8% of Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) patients with bronchiolitis having respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation.2-4 In order to reduce the need for mechanical ventilation, less invasive respiratory support modalities are increasingly being used. Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) delivers positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) that recruits collapsed airways, reducing air-trapping and lung hyperinflation.5 While the evidence supporting the use of CPAP is somewhat limited, one randomized controlled study showed significant improvement in pCO2 levels in patients versus controls.6

High flow nasal canula (HFNC) oxygen therapy delivers heated, humidified oxygen to patients. This allows higher flow rates of oxygen (1-8 L/min) to be delivered than with standard therapy. It is also thought to improve muco-ciliary clearance, decrease the energy expenditure required to heat and humidify inhaled gas, and provide some amount of CPAP.7, 8 Studies show consistent, clinically significant positive airway pressure at higher flow rates (usually 6-8L).9,10 Although no randomized clinical trials have been published looking at clinical outcomes with HFNC, a number of retrospective studies have shown decreased rates of intubation with the use of HFNC compared to historic controls.2,7 The ease of use and acceptability of HFNC has allowed it to be used outside the ICU on general pediatric wards in patients with bronchiolitis.8

**Duration of Training Session**: 1 hour

**Frequency of Scenario**: The goal is to have each learner experience this scenario approximately once-twice/year. Ideally it would be presented just before or during “bronchiolitis season” (November to March in much of the United States) in order to maximize its immediate applicability to patient care.

**Number of Trainees per Session:** 5 to 10. This scenario is most realistic and achieves maximal learning, if all participants are functioning in their normal roles, with the same number of participants as would typically be expected. E.g.: nurses perform nursing roles, physicians perform physician roles. If a more experienced physician would normally function as the team leader, s/he should play that role in the simulation. If a response team at your institution normally consists of ~ 7 respondents that should be the target number of trainees. If necessary, trainees or confederates can “act” any unfilled roles or those roles can be left unfilled. The instructor should be aware that realism will be compromised and learning objectives may be harder to achieve.

**Primary:** Pediatric, emergency medicine, family medicine, and internal medicine / pediatric medicine residents, pediatric emergency medicine fellows, emergency medicine and pediatric emergency medicine faculty and nurses, respiratory therapists, and pharmacists.

**Secondary:**  Medical students, pediatric hospitalists, pediatric critical care physicians

***Required background knowledge:***

* + Signs and symptoms of bronchiolitis
  + Management of bronchiolitis and respiratory distress and/or failure
  + Rapid sequence intubation medications and equipment
  + TeamSTEPPS11 communication terminology (See Appendix B)

***Required background skills expected in trainees prior to receiving training in the target scenario:***

* + Assessment of breathing and circulation
  + How to provide supplemental oxygen
  + Administration of CPAP or HFNC (In many settings these treatments are provided by respiratory therapists. Ideally the provider who would provide these therapies in the clinical setting would be included in simulation. If this is not available a “confederate” may be utilized to fill this roll.)
  + Endotracheal intubation

**Goal 1: Identify bronchiolitis and its complications**

The learner will identify that the patient’s clinical presentation is consistent with bronchiolitis. *(ACGME Competencies: Medical KnowledgeA, Patient CareB, Interpersonal and Communication SkillsC, ProfessionalismD, Systems-based PracticeE)*

**Objective 1a** *–* ***Demonstrate initial patient assessment* [A, B, C, D]**

The learner will perform an initial physical examination (primary survey e.g. airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure) and history (SAMPLE- Signs and symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, and Events) of any pediatric patient s/he is evaluating.

**Objective 1b– *Apply appropriate monitoring* [A, B, C]**

The learner will apply basic monitoring equipment for a critically ill patient (cardiorespiratory monitors, pulse oximetry, blood pressure, temperature, end tidal carbon dioxide monitoring if available). S/he should obtain a patient weight or accurate estimate (e.g. Broselow-Luten Tape).

**Objective 1c** *-* ***Identification of bronchiolitis* [A, B,C]**

The learner will identify signs of bronchiolitis based on patient history (patient age, antecedent URI, increasing work of breathing) and physical examination (tachypnea, nasal flaring, retractions, coarse breath sounds, wheezing/crackles, hypoxia). Bronchiolitis should be stated in the differential diagnosis (although other diagnoses may be considered).

**Objective 1d (optional) – *Identification of tension pneumothorax*[A, B, C, D]**

The learner will identify a change in clinical status in a previously stable patient. The learner should identify the presence of a tension pneumothorax based on patient history (initiation of positive pressure therapy) and physical exam findings (decreased breath sounds, tracheal deviation, hypoxia, hypotension).

**Goal 2: Manage bronchiolitis and its complications**

The learner will manage bronchiolitis in a safe and professional manner. *(ACGME Competencies: Medical KnowledgeA, Patient CareB, Interpersonal and Communication SkillsC, ProfessionalismD, Systems-based PracticeE)*

**Objective 2a – *Management of bronchiolitis with supplemental oxygen* [A,B]**

The learner will apply supplemental oxygen upon recognition of respiratory distress and/or hypoxia. The goal is to improve oxygen delivery to tissues.

**Objective 2b – *Management of bronchiolitis with suctioning*[A,B]**

The learner should perform nasal suctioning upon recognition of bronchiolitis. Goal is to ameliorate obstruction of nasal passages and decrease work of breathing.

**Objective 2c – *Understanding the role of additional therapies in the treatment of bronchiolitis*[A]**

The learner should consider the risks and benefits of the use of albuterol, racemic epinephrine, and/or nebulized hypertonic saline in this critically ill patient. If they are used, the learner should observe the patient for signs of clinical improvement after therapy, and make further therapeutic decisions based on these observations.

**Objective 2d – *Obtain parenteral access and provide fluid resuscitation.***

The learner should recognize the patient’s critical illness and prepare for advanced resuscitation by obtaining either intravenous or intraosseous access. The learner should provide fluid resuscitation to the patient with 20 mL/kg of normal saline. The learner should also consider the need for blood testing when IV access is obtained.

**Objective 2e – *Management of bronchiolitis with advanced respiratory support*[A,B]**

The learner should recognize the presence of inadequate ventilation and oxygenation and initiate advanced respiratory support. This may include CPAP, HFNC, or endotracheal intubation. Goal is to improve oxygen delivery and respiration.

**Objective 2f** – ***Recruit additional resources* [A, B, C]**

The learner should initiate recruitment of additional resources available at their institution or needed to transport the patient to definitive care. The learner should identify this early in the scenario and begin activation of personnel (e.g. respiratory therapy, anesthesia, radiology, intensive care, transport services, etc) and supplies.

**Objective 2g** – ***Airway management* [A, B, C]**

The team should physically initiate advance respiratory support (HFNC, CPAP, or endotracheal intubation). Ideally this should be done by the provider(s) who would perform these tasks in the clinical setting where the simulation is performed (i.e. RT initiating CPAP, MD + RT performing endotracheal intubation).

**Objective 2h (optional) – *Management of tension pneumothorax* [A, B, C]**

The team should perform immediate needle decompression of the affected lung when tension pneumothorax is recognized. Team member should assemble the equipment necessary for this procedure and perform it successfully on the manikin. The team should also recognize the need for eventual chest tube placement, although the procedure is beyond the scope of this simulation.

**Goal 3: Demonstrate effective teamwork and communication skills**

The learner will become more skilled in the management and leadership of emergency personnel including physicians, nurses, and ancillary personnel.

*(ACGME Competencies: Medical KnowledgeA, Patient CareB, Interpersonal and Communication SkillsC, ProfessionalismD, Systems-based PracticeE)*

**Objective 3a – *Team structure and leadership*[C, D]**

The learner will be exposed to a full-scale manikin-based simulation, in which the learners are faced with a life threatening emergency due to bronchiolitis. They will be expected to clearly identify and maintain a team leader and team member roles.

**Objective 3b – *Communication skills* [C, D]**

The learner assigned the team leader role will be required to direct available resources to manage respiratory distress/failure secondary to bronchiolitis. The team will be expected to *brief* at the beginning of the scenario and *huddle* during the scenario. The goal of *briefing* and *huddling* is to create a shared mental model, so that the team is on the same page regarding working diagnoses, management priorities and plan of care. The team leader will coordinate, direct and communicate with a resuscitation team using directed call-out and check-back.

1. **Environmental Set Up (See Section 10)**
   * Try to re-create the location, look, and feel of the participants’ work environment.
   * Place simulator in a gown, diaper, etc. in order to maximize realism.
2. **Pre-Simulation Introduction**

* Share a “learning contract” with participants. An example of some elements you may include: “We believe each of you is intelligent, well-trained, and doing your best” (adapted from the Center for Medical Simulation, Cambridge, MA).4 “We recognize this is a fictitious environment. We ask you to stretch your imagination, go beyond your comfort zone and help promote each others’ learning.”
* Share ground rules with participants (e.g. “Treat others with respect, maintain confidentiality”).
* Share the agenda (e.g. “We will begin with a 30 min simulation followed by a 30 min debrief”).
* Orient your participants (e.g. Review capabilities of simulator being used. Review location/availability of equipment/supplies. Identify facilitator to whom requests/questions should be directed during the simulation).
* Review safety issues (e.g. correct use of defibrillator).
* Review principles of teamwork and communication – TeamSTEPPS (See Appendix B). Review expectations of team leaders and members: take time to plan before a patient arrives (brief) and “get the team on the same page” (huddle).

1. **Scenario Notes**

* A “trigger” is a critical time or event that signifies the start or end of a Stage in the scenario. These are the critical steps that help the scenario progress. Please review these prior to conducting your session.
* Prompts. Sometimes learners get stuck- miss a physical exam finding, critical piece of history or don’t know/fail to implement a critical action that’s needed to help the team meet a learning objective or advance to the next stage. When this happens, the facilitator, who is actively monitoring the team’s progress, may choose to ask a question, state a cue or have a third party “drop a critical hint” to mobilize the team towards meeting the objectives. The instructor needs to balance “keeping the team from failing to meet the learning objectives” with the team’s opportunity to work through a problem. Careful not to shift it from self-discovery to a lecture!

1. **Debriefing (See Appendix C)**

* Remember: Try to have participants step away from the simulator, into a different space (e.g. chairs in a circle or separate room). This physical cue helps participants shift from “doing” - a clinical focus to “reflecting” - a learning focus.
* Remind participants that the debriefing time is intended to focus on the *group’s* performance.

1. **Learner considerations**

* Over time, we have discovered that each group of learners’ needs vary. Thus, we have included a range of potential objectives, which can be tailored to suit different learner groups. E.g. for our less experienced clinical providers, we focus more on the medical decision making goals. For our more advanced providers, we focus more on the team work and communication goals.
* If you are instructing more experienced learners, consider “titrating” the learning objectives. E.g. for learners that have limited medical knowledge, applying oxygen and performing deep suctioning could all that should be required to move from stage 1 directly to stage 3. For experienced providers, they will need to move through all stages of the simulation, including the recognition and treatment of a tension pneumothorax, before proceeding to stage 5.
* If you are instructing more experienced learners, consider “titrating” the scenario’s signal to noise ratio. E.g. for resident learner groups, we will run the scenario as written. For those with more experience, we might add more “noise”. This could be an actor playing the role of a parent who gives insufficient history, is crying or questioning the team (providing a distraction).
* Participants. The most realistic and richest learning experiences occur when all the participants are performing their “typical” roles. E.g. physician functions as physician, nurse as a nurse, respiratory therapist as a respiratory therapist. If your learner group does not contain the full spectrum of “normal team members”, you may have to either ask some participants to function in different roles or provide “actors” to fill the necessary roles. Recognize that realism is going to be lost and learning opportunities missed. E.g. If the group is all physicians, none of your learners may have drug measuring/administration experience. As an instructor, you will need to make decisions on how much you want them to do to “get credit” and be able to advance to the next Stage in the scenario. Is it enough to be able to order the drug? Do they need to find the vial? Draw it up? Administer it to the simulator? Your answer should be driven on helping your learners to achieve the learning objectives.
* Impact of the curriculum. This simulation was designed as an adjunct to the implementation of a new hospital-wide clinical pathway for initiation and use of high flow nasal canula oxygen therapy in bronchiolitis patients. When the simulation was performed in our institution, it served to familiarize participant and observers with both the pathway itself and the potential complications of HFNC therapy. Performing this simulation also allowed for the identification of barriers to successful implementation of the clinical pathway, which were forwarded to and addressed by the hospital’s bronchiolitis working group. We believe this simulation may be used in both a teaching and testing capacity at other institutions as well.

**Common Errors and Prevention Strategies:**

1. **Failure to recognize bronchiolitis.
   *Strategy:*** Review signs of bronchiolitis: history (patient age, seasonality, antecedent upper respiratory infection, and gradually progressive symptoms), physical exam (tachypnea, increased work of breathing – nasal flaring, retractions, copious rhinorrhea, wheezing/crackles, hypoxia).
2. **Failure to recognize illness severity
   *Strategy:*** Review indicators of persistent respiratory distress or impending respiratory failure (work of breathing, persistent hypoxia, significant tachypnea, lethargy, hypercarbia)
3. **Failure to adequately treat bronchiolitis
   *Strategy:*** Review management of bronchiolitis, including initial treatments (suctioning, supplemental oxygen) and indications for more aggressive measures (HFNC, CPAP, endotracheal intubation).
4. **(Optional) Failure to recognize tension pneumothorax**

**Strategy:** Review differential diagnosis for sudden decompensation in a stabilized patient requiring respiratory support (equipment failure, mucous plugging, pneumothorax, displace ETT if applicable). Review signs of tension pneumothorax (unilateral decreased breath sounds, tracheal deviation, hypoxia, and hypotension).

1. **(Optional) Failure to treat tension pneumothorax in a timely fashion**

***Strategy***: Review need to treat emergently, not waiting for radiographic confirmation, review indications, anatomy and technique of needle decompression of a tension pneumothorax.

1. **Inefficient teamwork**

***Strategy:*** Review need to *brief* (discuss team roles) prior to a critical situation and *huddle* (ad-hoc planning to re-establish situation awareness) during a crisis.

1. **Inefficient communication: lack of call-out
   *Strategy:*** Review importance of directed communication:

“Survey physician- What’s the circulatory status?”

1. **Inefficient communication: lack of check-back**

***Strategy:*** Review use of closed-loop communication:

Team Leader: “Bedside nurse –obtain a CBG.”

Bedside Nurse: “Drawing a CBG now.”

Team Leader: “Acknowledged.”

1. **Inefficient communication: lack of shared mental model**

***Strategy***: Review use/request for team summaries with the establishment of a working diagnosis, rhythm change, clinical change, or lack of response to intervention

Medication Administration Nurse: “Team leader, can you summarize?”

**General strategies to solve problems**

* Increase knowledge base: assigned reading, lectures, teamwork and communication training
* Debriefing focused to re-evaluate critical thinking and structure planning of actions
* Teaching points based on errors
  + Regular simulation training to avoid previous mistakes
  + If time allows, run simulation a second time after issues are discussed
  + Behavior change comes slowly, over time. We believe it’s critical to create opportunities for learners to develop skills over time.

**Key methods for delivering cognitive training include the following:**

* + Bronchiolitis Learner Handout (See Appendix D).
  + Handouts may be shared with team members anytime during the simulation training process. We recommend sharing these with your learners sometime BEFORE your simulation if you want them to have the information ready to apply. Handouts should be AVAILABLE DURING your simulation if you want them to have a “reference” to turn to. They should be made available AFTER your simulation if you want them to be able to compare their actions to a reference. When you share the handout with your learners should match your educational goals and philosophy: formative, summative, etc.

*We believe that the focus of each simulation should be the DEBRIEF.*

*Simulation creates the opportunity to examine our medical management, technical skills and teamwork and communication skills. It facilitates discussion about challenges in a safe environment in order to improve the quality of patient care.*

### Framework for debriefing:

Each debrief should consist of 4 components:

* + - Introduction
    - Discussion of emotions
    - Discussion of medical management and technical skills
    - Discussion of teamwork and communication skills

1. **Introduction**

This “sets the stage” for debriefing and creates expectations.

What you might say:

* This is an opportunity to reflect and learn, improve our medical care, team work, and communication.
* Everyone should be able to ask questions and share their thoughts.
* Once you leave this session, we encourage open discussion of the concepts, but ask you to not to discuss individual performance.

1. **Emotional experience discussion**

There are a couple of camps regarding discussing emotions. One perspective is that until emotions are dealt with, it’s difficult for adult learners to “move on”: switching gears to process thoughts, actions and opportunities for improvement. Another perspective is that adult learners should process their emotions independently.

Our perspective is the first. If a group or team member is emotionally charged (sad, mad or frustrated) regarding something that did or didn’t happen in a scenario, it’s usually difficult for the individual or the group to be actively engaged, receptive to feedback and able to promote learning, until the emotions are addressed.

An example: a medication error occurs. One team member may think it is all his/her fault. S/he may feel embarrassed, judged, etc. If he/she can verbalize this, other team members may offer different perspectives, which enable the team to process the error together, potentially identifying contributing systems issues. If the emotions aren’t addressed- 3 separate people may feel embarrassed, responsible and not engage in a discussion, failing to identify systems issues which led to the error.

What you might say:

* How did that feel?
* Can you tell me more? Why?

1. **Medical management and technical skills**

This portion of the discussion focuses on the medical aspects of the scenario. It’s usually more comfortable to begin with these “facts”.

What you might say:

* + - Let’s begin by discussing medical management.
    - What did you think was wrong with the patient? Can someone briefly summarize what happened in this scenario?
    - How did you reach those conclusions?

1. **Teamwork and communication** (a. k. a. crew resource management, non-technical skills, human factors)

This portion of the discussion focuses on how the team worked together. It can be emotionally charged and difficult to discuss without feeling personal. The challenge is to try to generalize specifics into themes.

What you might say:

* + - Let’s talk about how you functioned as a team.
    - What did your team do well?
    - What could your team do differently next time?
    - That is something I see often. Has anyone else experienced that? How have you seen that handled?

### 4) Summarizing

* *This is your opportunity to ensure the key learning points are highlighted*
* *Try to identify approximately three take-home points*

**Bronchiolitis Learning Objectives**

1. **Recognize bronchiolitis**
   1. History suggestive of bronchiolitis: Patient age, seasonality, antecedent URI, +/- fever, gradually increasing respiratory difficulty, difficulty feeding
   2. Physical signs: copious upper airway secretions, nasal flaring, grunting, retractions coarse breath sounds (“sounds like a washing machine”), +/- wheezing, +/- crackles
   3. Vital signs – tachypnea, hypoxia
   4. Differential diagnosis – pneumonia, reactive airway disease, sepsis, vascular anomaly (“rings and slings”), cardiac dysfunction (congenital heart disease, myocarditis), metabolic abnormality
   5. Risk factors for more severe disease: Prematurity, younger age, baseline cardiopulmonary disease, baseline neurologic disease
2. **Manage bronchiolitis**
   1. Mild to moderate bronchiolitis – supportive care; nasal suctioning, ensure adequate hydration, oxygen as needed to keep SaO2 >93%
   2. Bronchodilators (albuterol, racemic epinephrine) generally not effective (controversial, trial may be considered in severe bronchiolitis especially with risk factors for asthma)
   3. Nebulized hypertonic saline not shown to improve clinical status in the emergency department
   4. Steroids not indicated
   5. Chest X-ray generally not indicated – consider in severe disease, diagnostic uncertainty
   6. Indications for admission – hypoxia, poor oral intake, significant work of breathing, apneas
3. **Recognition of severe bronchiolitis**
   1. Severe respiratory distress (grunting, retractions, tachypnea) refractory to suctioning
   2. Lethargy, decreased responsiveness
   3. Persistent hypoxia on oxygen therapy
   4. Hypercarbia (CBG, VBG, or ABG)
4. **Management of critically ill patients with bronchiolitis**
   1. High flow nasal canula (HFNC)
      1. Delivers heated, humidified oxygen
      2. Flow rates 1-8 L/min
      3. Higher rate of oxygen flow, improved mucous-ciliary clearance, some amount of CPAP
      4. Small studies show decreased rate of intubation versus usual therapy
      5. Has been used on general medical wards
   2. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)
      1. Recruits collapsed airways, reducing air-trapping and hyperinflation
      2. Starting pressure 6 cmH2O
      3. Small RCT shows significant improvement in pCO2 versus controls; avoid intubation in case series
   3. Endotracheal Intubation
      1. Respiratory failure or failure to stabilize with non-invasive ventilation
   4. Monitor for complications of positive pressure ventilation, i.e. pneumothorax
