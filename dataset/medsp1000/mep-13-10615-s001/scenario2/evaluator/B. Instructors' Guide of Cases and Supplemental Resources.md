Section 3: Supporting Abe (50 minutes)

Instructors will next continue discussing case material for Abe by distributing case text "Supporting Abe, Part B" (Module 2, Handout 1 from Appendix C). This material provides more of Abe’s social history, as well as describes the progression of his visit. In addition to presenting case material, the instructors introduce the concept of safety nets.

*Note:* The social history provided for Abe (Appendix C, Module 2, Handout 1) should be adapted for the specific host institution and region. The current version contains placeholders for neighborhoods and school names that instructors should alter so that Abe’s experience can be situated in the immediate geographic context of the learners (rather than Boston, where the case was originally developed).

Prior to discussing the case material, instructors will distribute the Supports and Challenges Grid (Appendix F). Instructors will indicate that LGBT youth require safety nets, due to their exposure to minority stress and disproportionate risk for adverse health outcomes (reinforcing material covered in Section 1). Creating a safety net requires taking a thorough inventory of factors and agents that support and affirm, or that reject and challenge LGBT youth. It is also important to understand how these factors and agents interact with each other and other factors in a LGBT youth's life, or how contexts can be both supportive and rejecting. The grid provides a useful schematic for organizing factors that are external (contextual) or internal (located at the level of the individual) as well as invalidating or supporting. The instructor will then direct the learners to use the grid to map social history, medical, and other factors in Abe’s life (as they read case material) that may factor into his safety net. As the instructors and learners discuss the case material, they should return to the supports and challenges grid to see if the information they have mapped can help to inform strategies for supporting Abe in multiple contexts and in the face of diverse obstacles and challenges identified in the case.

Instructors will ask a learner to read the “Social History” portion of Handout 1. The instructors will then ask, “What assumptions are you making based off of Abe’s social history?”

Instructors will ask a different learner to read the “Progression of the visit” portion of Handout 1. The instructors will then ask, “What do you do next?”

*Section 3 (Supporting Abe, Part B) potential probe questions to be asked as learners discuss Abe’s social history and the progression of his visit*

* *What issues need to be addressed with this case?*
* *How do you respond to Abe’s sharing what is going on?*
* *What does Abe want? How might he want to accomplish this?*
* *Looking at this case from the prospective of different providers, what might be of greatest concern to a: medical provider (i.e., physician and nurse or nurse practitioner); mental health provider (i.e., social worker or psychologist); and a nutritionist?*
* *Do you assume Abe is sexually active?*
* *Are you obliged to inform the parents about passive SI?*
* *What further questions would you have to assess the scope of suicidality that was revealed in this clinical interaction?*
* *What aspects of the mom's narrative and behavior may be conveyed as either supportive or rejecting? How does Abe's and his mother's experience compare to that shared in the Family Acceptance Project video ("Always My Son")?*
* *What assumptions do we make when we hear about Abe's racial/ethnic and religious background? How might these assumptions impact how we approach issues around sexual orientation?*
* *How do you create a safety net? What risk and protective factors can you identify in Abe's life? How might you address these risk factors or leverage these protective factors to better support Abe?*
* *Based on the information you have mapped on the supports and challenges grid, how would you work with Abe, his parents, and other supportive adults and peers to identify and utilize supports in different contexts? How would you work with Abe to mitigate the influence of the obstacles and challenges he is currently facing?*
* *Why would this patient have difficulty establishing a therapeutic alliance with a therapist?*

*Section 3 (Supporting Abe, Part B) take home points (can be summarized into bullet points and read aloud, or displayed on a lecture slide)*

* Sexual orientation is but one facet of an individual's identity. In working with LGB youth, it is important to acknowledge how gender, age, race, ethnicity, religion, spirituality, and other facets of identity may contribute to their experience of being a sexual minority, as well as their capacity to access supports. Having multiple intersection identities can be both an asset as well as a challenge to LGB youth. How do the learners think Abe's cultural and religious background may impact how he experiences his sexual orientation identity? Providers should consider what assumptions they may make because of a LGB youth's multiple identities, using tools from the discussion on cultural humility to facilitate this process.
* Creating a safety net requires taking a thorough inventory of factors and agents that support and affirm, or that reject and challenge a LGB youth. It is also important to understand how these factors and agents interact with each other and other factors in a LGB youth's life, or how contexts can be both supportive and rejecting. It may be helpful to map these factors and agents along a continuum of validation and invalidation, and consider whether these factors and agents are internal or external to the youth in question. Instructors may use the grid provided in Appendix F, as developed by Stoddard and colleagues,10 to help visualize how and where factors and agents are located in a youth's life. For example, having a high school Gay-Straight Alliance is a potential support to Abe, but accessing the GSA may require coming out, which could expose Abe to school-based bullying and victimization. One parent might support Abe, while another parent rejects him. Abe's neighborhood may have a resource or drop-in center for LGB youth, but it might be in a part of the neighborhood that Abe does not feel comfortable going to, or it may predominantly serve individuals with whom Abe does not identify or get along. In creating a support network with a patient, providers can rely on a patient to judge the safety and the logistics of using different supports.

*Section 3, (Supporting Abe, Part C) take home points (can be summarized into bullet points and read aloud, or displayed on a lecture slide)*

* Supporting LGB youth is an ongoing process, and clinic is just one context in which LGB youth need support and affirmation. Adolescents spend significant time in school, and LGB adolescents are at risk for school victimization, truancy, and drop out.11 Involving LGB youth with Gay-Straight Alliances (when available) can connect youth with peers and adults who can provide valuable social support and outlets for advocacy which can work towards making school a safer space.12 Knowing of other community and faith-based resources to support LGB youth is also important for referrals for additional support or when school-based resources are not available. Resources also exist for parents and families (e.g., PFLAG), although it is important to consider first whether parents and families are receptive to such support.
* Social media are an additional source for information and support for LGB youth, particularly because Internet resources may overcome other barriers to support in a youth's daily life (e.g., lack of local resources or family support, being "in the closet" but needing support, etc.). However, it is also important to review the potential risks for social media, such as exposure to cyber bullying, exploitation, as well as overuse and Internet addiction.
