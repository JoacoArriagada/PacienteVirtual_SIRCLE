**Case 3 (Slide #6) -15 minutes: Atypical sibling rivalry related to childhood exposure to intimate partner violence. Slide Content: *“Sibling Rivalry?”*** *During a visit to the general pediatrician’s office, the mother of an 8 year-old boy states that the boy often hits and curses at his sister.” The mother is extremely concerned that he might have ADHD and would like a prescription. She also states that she hits her son as punishment for his hitting her sister. What are this boy’s risk factors for community violence victimization or perpetration? How would you approach this situation? What specific phrases would you use?*

**Objectives for case #3:**

The residents will be able to:

1. Recognize that chronic exposure to intimate partner violence is associated with aggressive behavior and subsequent injury from community violence
2. Appreciate that intimate partner violence is a common cause of atypical (excessively violent) sibling rivalry
3. Recognize that violent behavior is not a part of the formal criteria for attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder
4. Describe methods of screening for intimate partner violence in situations of atypical sibling rivalry

**Notes to Instructor:**

*After presenting the case, ask one resident to discuss their approach to the situation. Have the resident formulate questions and talking points to the mother as if it were a real patient encounter. Allow the resident to speak uninterrupted. Then allow the other resident to add in additional points.*

*You may want to use the following questions to stimulate discussion:*

*“So, what do you think is the most likely cause of this child’s behavior?”*

*“Do you think he has ADHD? “*

*“Do the DSM IV criteria for ADHD say anything about violence? “*

*“Let’s assume you suspect intimate partner violence, how would you go about asking this mother about it?”*

*“What concerns do you have about asking about intimate partner violence?”*

*“If she disclosed that she was being abused, what resources could you offer to this mother?”*

*“What are this boy’s risk factors for violent injury in the future?”*

*The key points to be covered in this conversation include:*

* *Identify risk factors for violent injury in this young boy.* Potential risk factors for increased violent behavior include:

1. *Use of corporal punishment as means of discipline*. As discussed earlier, this child is learning, at a very young age, to use violence to solve his problems.[7](#_ENREF_7)
2. *Undiagnosed mental health problems.* Many community violence victims have a long history of undiagnosed mental health conditions that can be traced back to the school-aged years.[11](#_ENREF_11) While this child might have ADHD, it is unlikely that all of his symptoms are ADHD related. ADHD is diagnosed when a child displays inattentiveness and hyperactivity in two different settings (e.g. school and home) beginning as early as 4-5years of age. Important for this case is the child’s display of violence and inappropriate language, which do not coincide with a diagnosis of ADHD. The resident must make it clear to the parent that these symptoms are not completely explained by ADHD. The resident should then ask the parent to list other factors that might be contributing to the child’s behavior. One approach is to start by saying, *“I see ADHD in the clinic every week. This is does not sound like ADHD.”* It is also possible that this child may have Oppositional Defiant Disorder or Conduct disorder, but again this does not explain all of his symptoms and such a diagnosis would require evaluation by a child psychiatrist.
3. *Intimate partner violence (IPV).* In many cases, boys who behave in this manner do so because they are living in violent homes.[12](#_ENREF_12) An effective approach might be to say, *“Children may pick up these kinds of violent behaviors from school, from their friends, from the television, or even from their homes. Sometimes if children see one parent hitting the other, they will replicate the same behaviors. Does this happen at your house?”* If the mother denies abuse you may try to challenge her with a gentle but direct statement, *“The last time that I saw a child like this it was because he witnessed his father hitting and cursing at his mother. Are you sure this has never happened to you?”* When parents disclose intimate partner violence to you it is important not to be judgmental. Recognize that there are many reasons for why a mother would choose to remain in an abusive relationship.[13](#_ENREF_13)

* *Resources for IPV victims*. Never tell an IPV victim that they should “*leave*” an abusive relationship. Leaving can be extremely dangerous for the victim and it must be coordinated with a skilled IPV counselor. You can call the national domestic violence hotline (1-800-799-SAFE).[14](#_ENREF_14)
* *Demonstrate empathy.* Again, by ‘*being on her side’* the resident is much more likely to have the mother disclose the abuse that has happened to her.[15](#_ENREF_15) She is also more likely to be open to discussing alternatives to corporal punishment.
* *Protective factors.* In this case, also recognize that the mother’s concern regarding her child’s behavior is considered a protective factor against future violent behavior. While the mother does behave violently at times, she does not condone that type of behavior from her son.[16](#_ENREF_16) As a result the son is less likely to agree that violence is an acceptable means of getting what he wants.
* *Motivational Interviewing*. Motivational Interviewing (MI) is a patient-centered method of counseling that seeks to elicit intrinsic motivation for changing behavior and encourages patients to understand and resolve their ambivalence to such change.[17](#_ENREF_17) In this case, MI may be helpful by making the mother aware of the detrimental effects of exposing her child to violence. This counseling may influence the mother to make positive changes in her behavior as well. A basic MI interview may look something like this:

*Doctor*- “What do you want for your son’s future?”

*Mother*- “I want him to be a respectful, nice boy.”

*Doctor*- “Does your son imitate and look up to you?”

*Mother*- “Yes, of course.”

*Doctor*- “If your son looks up to you and you hit him when he misbehaves, what do you think this says to him.”

*Mother*- “It gives him the false idea that hitting other people is good. If I keep hitting him, he will continue to hit his sister and maybe even other people. If he learns this behavior he will not be viewed as a nice and respectful child. I should stop hitting my son.”

The idea of MI is to have the patient tell you their motivations for participating in certain behaviors and to have them develop a plan for change.

* *Confidentiality-* This conversation should take place with the child outside of the room beginning at age 3.[18](#_ENREF_18) There is a concern that the child might repeat the content of your discussion with the perpetrator (father). Also, the mother may be less likely to disclose the details of her corporal punishment with the child in the room.
