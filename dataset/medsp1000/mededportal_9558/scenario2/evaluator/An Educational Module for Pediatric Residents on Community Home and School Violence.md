**Case 2(Slide #5) - 15 minutes: Family that is grieving the loss of their father due to homicide. Slide content: “The Grieving Mother”** *In your office you see a two and a half year-old child whose father was recently murdered. The child is accompanied by his mother. On physical examination, the child appears healthy. What are your concerns? What should you do? What specific phrases would you use?*

**Objectives for case #2:**

The resident will be able to:

1. List at least two resources for parents dealing with grief
2. Discuss the emotional and financial burdens that the loss of a father may place on a child and on the grieving mother
3. Appreciate that losing the father of a child puts the mother at risk for depression, financial instability, chronic stress, and fear of violent injury

**Note to Instructor:**

*After presenting the case, ask one resident to discuss their approach to the situation. Have the resident formulate questions and talking points to the mother as if it were a real patient encounter. Allow the resident to speak uninterrupted. Then allow the other resident to add in additional points. More so than with the other cases, it is likely that residents will successfully touch upon most of the teaching points.*

*To stimulate conversation you may ask questions like:*

*“How do you think the death of this father might affect the child? The mother?”*

*“Besides grief, what other ways might this family might be affected by his loss? Think very practically.”*

*“If the mother develops depression as a result of this loss, how do you think that might impact her ability to function as a parent?”*

*After residents have finished speaking you may contribute additional pearls from your clinical or academic experience.*

*The key points to be covered in this conversation include:*

* *Address the mother’s state of well-being.*[*9*](#_ENREF_9)It is important to ask the mother how she is coping with the recent loss. What support networks are in place for her to handle the stress of losing her husband and child’s father? Are their signs of anger and depression? Is this family in any way in danger from the same violence that took away the father? When parents say, *“I’m just fine. I am doing OK,”* You should gently challenge them by saying, *“In my experience, people in your situation often deal with financial stress, depression, and difficulty raising the kids. Are you sure you are not having any of these issues? If so I can offer some resources.”*
* *Building a support network*. The resident should be aware of the local resources available to help the mother deal with this recent loss. Know when to consult the social worker (if available) and what community organizations and group therapy options are available.
* *Is child safe?* Are the individuals who murdered the father somehow putting the child at risk? If so then child welfare authorities must be contacted.
* *How does losing your father at age 2 affect the child when they are 8 years old?* Children want to emulate their parents. Whereas some children envision a life as a teacher or doctor because their parents held those professions, children who have lost a parent to gun violence may feel that that is their fate. It is the role of the pediatrician to encourage the mother to talk to the older child and tell them that she can be whatever she wants. Her life does not have to be the same as her father’s life. The sense that one’s life will end sooner than it should is known as *fatalism*.[10](#_ENREF_10) Urban youth often have very high rates of unrealistic fatalism when compared to youth from less violent neighborhoods. While the pediatrician may initiate these types of conversations, they are best performed by a child therapist with training in grief counseling.
