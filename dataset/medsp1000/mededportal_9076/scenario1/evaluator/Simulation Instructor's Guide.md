# Acting Intern On Call Simulations: Instructor’s Guide

N. Benjamin Fredrick, MD   
Associate Professor, Family and Community Medicine   
Penn State College of Medicine

# Purpose.

The purpose of these simulation cases is to provide a 10 minute realistic experience for acting interns fielding floor calls on their own until back-up help arrives. Through these experiences acting interns develop a discomfort with having to make time-pressured patient management decisions. This discomfort is useful in motivate acting interns to develop personal learning objectives related to assuming increased clinical responsibility of patient care.

These three cases are simulation tools intended to be used by $4 ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ year medical students on their acting internship (sub-internship) rotation. The cases are all of inpatient adults with acute undifferentiated complaints.

# Importance.

The acting internship courses play an important part in allowing the fourth year medical student to assume greater clinical responsibilities. This course can provide an opportunity to challenge students out of the observer role and begin to take on a greater role in patient management. During the third year rotations, however, the medical student typically does not handle on call issues. These simulations provide a safe environment for acting interns to be challenged with a reality they will face only a year away: managing acute undifferentiated complaints. How will they respond to an acute floor call? How will they respond under pressure when they recognize that they must make some decisions that could have significant consequences for a patient?

These simulations, which are typically done at the beginning of fourth year when students are interested in completing their acting internship, provide new insight for the student into how they might prepare for the intern year. The simulations are generally eye-opening experiences as the students realize they must now prepare to assume a new level of patient care: management. Students favorably express that they have not experienced the level of stress and pressure that these simulations provide, and they come away with a sense that they will need to go back to the books in order to re-read conditions from the perspective of how to manage those conditions.

# Learning Objectives.

1. The student will experience and demonstrate increased assumption of clinical responsibility for patient care, similar to those responsibilities of an intern on call.

2. The student will demonstrate advanced clinical thinking, including the independent development of differential diagnoses, evaluation and management plans appropriate to the clinical scenarios.   
3. The student will appraise his/her decision-making skills in the initial management of patients with acute-onset undifferentiated problems.   
4. Students will experience on call stress through the simulation.   
5. Students will recognize the importance of good communication during patient hand-off at sign-out   
6. Students will properly evaluate the acute issue by obtaining an adequate history, performing targeted physical exam, reviewing the sign-out sheet, and correctly interpreting vital signs, lab results, EKG and chest x-ray.   
7. Students will prioritize diagnoses, develop action plan, and commit to a course of action to stabilize the patient.

# Practical Implementation.

These cases are designed to be implemented in a simulation lab with ability to alter the mannequin’s vital signs in real-time based on factors related to time and medical decision-making. Alarming monitors for abnormal vital signs are essential to the experience by adding stress into the scenario. A mannequin that is able to simulate a seizure (for case scenario #3—hypoglycemic seizure) would be ideal, but not necessary.

The scenarios generally work best with up to three students at one time. Each student takes turn being the Intern On Call with one of the three scenarios. Doubling-up students on a case is less preferable as it tends to reduce the stress level of the student in general.

The total time to orient the students to the lab, provide instructions, and run the three simulations is about 60 minutes. Debriefing can occur during this time-frame or after all cases are concluded.

Students need to be oriented to the lab and the mannequin with opportunity to listen to “normal” heart sounds and lung sounds and to check pulses. Instructions about the simulation (see “Simulation Set-up and Preparation” for details) includes a re-assurance that this is only a simulation. The purpose of the simulation is to provide an experience of what it feels to be an intern on call who must make medical decisions. Debriefing will include discussion about specific medical management decisions, but students should recognize they are not being graded on their performance. Other students may not assist the “intern on call’ during the scenario by providing recommendations or suggestions. The Intern On Call may use reference tools such as medication dosing helps if available. The student must give specific orders. For example, when ordering medications the student needs to provide the name, dose, and route of administration. Ordering a medication class (“Beta blocker”) will result in no action, and the student should be reminded to list out the details.

When the 10 minutes runs out the scenario will stop. If the student stabilizes the patient and makes the correct diagnosis before the 10 minutes concludes the scenario can be stopped early at the discretion of the faculty member.

Typically we begin setting up for the next scenario, have the students rotate ‘jobs’ (Intern On Call, Nurse, Observer), and then begin the next scenario. Debriefing (see Simulation Set-up and Preparation for details) then occurs with all students at the conclusion of the simulations.

# Limitations and Areas for Improvement.

Faculty members may need to provide specific instructions regarding adjusting of mannequin parameters during the simulation, depending on the knowledge of the simulation technician. Specific instructions could include statements such as, “Bring the systolic blood pressure down to $9 0 ^ { \dprime }$ or “Reduce the blood pressure by $2 5 \% "$ .

When using a mannequin that does not simulate a seizure, faculty must continue to remind the student every minute or so that the mannequin remains in a seizure. Alternatively, another student could shake the bed. If the mannequin stops seizing (or restarts seizing) the faculty member needs to mention this to the student.

While the simulations focus largely on the experiences, there are opportunities to improve the quality of the evaluation process. For example, a checklist to keep track of whether students correctly read an EKG or chest x-ray, or complete certain essential tasks such as talking with the patient and doing a physical exam, could be used to provide feedback to the student at the conclusion. A checklist approach could also potentially identify students with remediation needs or identify more systemic school training issues (e.g. “Very few medical students were able to properly perform...”).

The educational value could be further enhanced by requiring students to develop and complete specific learning objectives related to the case scenario by the end of the rotation.
