# INSTRUCTOR’S GUIDE FOR KAWASAKI DISEASE SIMULATION SCENARIO

Title: “18 month old male with Incomplete Kawasaki Disease”

Target Audience: Medical Students, Emergency Medicine and Pediatric Residents, Physician Assistants, Nurse Practitioners

# III. Overview:

This case centers on an 18 month old male, Timmy Baker, who presents with several days of high fever, truncal rash, strawberry tongue, erythema of the palms and soles, and abnormal lab values. His parent, who is distressed over the fact that his/her son has been sick for 5 days and has been extremely irritable, will accompany him. His visit with his primary care physician 2 days ago provided no answer to why his/her son was sick but started him empirically on Amoxicillin.

Those participating in the scenario will be expected to obtain a focused history of present illness, past medical and social history, and perform a physical examination. During the examination, the SimBaby will be dressed with a non-vesicular truncal maculopapular rash. Images will be available as well to help demonstrate the physical exam findings, including: a strawberry tongue, cracked lips, and erythematous hands. During the history or physical, they will be expected to ask their nurse to place the patient on a monitor, obtain a set of vital signs, and order appropriate lab work. They will then be expected to make a diagnosis, explain it to the parent, and consult the appropriate pediatric services, specifically, cardiology. Consultation with infectious disease or critical care may be obtained, but are not necessary, as these patients are typically safe for regular inpatient admission. Treatment may be ordered in the emergency department, including high dose aspirin and IVIG, but this is not necessary to complete the case, as this therapy is typically started once the patient is moved to the wards.

# IV. Purpose

This case is designed to help remind emergency physicians and pediatricians on the various ways that Kawasaki Disease may present, including the classic and incomplete patterns. This is a relatively common disease in the pediatric population and one that must remain high on a physician’s differential for fever of $> 5$ days, as the long term consequences of coronary artery aneurysms can be devastating in missed cases. In regards to incomplete Kawasaki Disease, this entity may carry a higher risk for the development of coronary artery aneurysms. This is one of the few diseases where a specific treatment is available that is extremely efficacious and safe and can be able to be initiated in an emergency department setting. IVIG is considered one of the most cost effective medicines available and dramatically reduces the incidence of coronary artery aneurysms if initiated prior to 10 days from the start of fever.

# V. Conceptual background

There are three to five-thousand cases of Kawasaki Disease per year in the United States, the vast majority of which occur in children under the age of five. Although as traditionally taught, Kawasaki Disease presents with a fever of greater than five days and four of five clinical criteria, physician’s must be aware of cases of incomplete Kawasaki Disease. The clinical consequences and treatment of both presentations are similar, and this case helps to strengthen and broaden a physician’s differential diagnosis of fever of greater than five days without a clear explanation.

# VI. Implementation

This case requires use of the SimBaby and its associated software, program files, powerpoint slides, and pictures. Having a projector or computer monitor to display pictures of physical exam findings is helpful but not necessary (if not available, print outs of the pictures should be provided for the participants). There should be 1-2 physician participants, a nurse, and someone who plays the role of the patient’s parent. The facilitator should be able to run the Laerdal software, serve as the over-the-phone consultants for the case, and be able to conduct a debriefing with the powerpoint presentation. A real-time audience may be present to observe the case. The encounter should take no more than 10 to 15 minutes to complete. Rehearsal of the program handlers, familiarity with the embedded trends, briefing actors on the scenario and its progression, and anticipation of participant requests for medications and resources will help the scenario run smoothly and successfully.

# VII. Limitations

It may be difficult to appropriately dress the SimBaby in preparation for the case, so a reliance on pictures to help demonstrate physical exam findings is necessary. Ideally these can be presented via a projector so both active and passive learners can go through the findings in real time. In cases where participants miss the diagnosis, there is not a lot of procedural learning to be obtained from the scenario, as patients who present with Kawasaki Disease typically are not decompensating; however, the main objectives to this case are to help emergency physicians and pediatricians broaden their differential for a patient with a fever and a rash. Furthermore, participants should be aware of what an appropriate disposition for these patients is, and the debriefing will further help both active and passive participants solidify their theoretical approach to a patient with a fever and rash.

# VIII. Learning Objectives:

# a. Primary:

i. When presented with a child with a fever, participants will be able to assess airway, breathing, and circulation, obtain a focused history and physical exam, order appropriate lab work, start treatment, and establish an appropriate disposition for the patient, including appropriate consultations.

# b. Secondary:

i. Participants will review the appropriate dosing of aspirin and IVIG for patients with Kawasaki Disease   
ii. Participants will review the criteria for diagnosis of complete and incomplete Kawasaki Disease

# c. Critical actions checklist:

i. Introduce yourself to the parent

ii. Ask the nurse to underdress the patient, place them on a monitor, and obtain a set of vital signs   
iii. Obtain a history from the parent, including HPI, past, surgical, birth, family, and social histories   
iv. Perform a physical examination v. Ask the nurse to obtain IV access and draw labs   
vi. Obtain an EKG   
vii. Review lab work and make a diagnosis of Incomplete Kawasaki Disease   
viii. Consult cardiology for an echocardiogram   
ix. Admit the patient to an inpatient pediatric service

d. Additional, non-critical actions:

i. Administer a $2 0 \mathrm { c c } / \mathrm { k g }$ IV bolus of crystalloid fluid ii. Consult infectious disease iii. Initiate a $2 ~ \mathrm { g / k g }$ IV infusion of IVIG iv. Administer a $2 5 \mathrm { m g / k g }$ dose of aspirin

IX. Environment

a. Lab Set Up: Emergency Department (ED) examination room   
b. Manikin Set Up: High fidelity SimBaby manikin driven by Laerdal SimBaby 1.6.2 software i. Patient’s attire: age appropriate clothing ii. Moulage: truncal rash – can be applied with standard make up that can be purchased at any convenience store   
c. Props: electrocardiogram, photographs (strawberry tongue, cracked red lips, erythematous hands), laboratory results sheet

X. Actors

a. Roles: patient’s parent (mother or father), nurse, pediatric cardiologist   
b. Who may play them: actor, nurse, nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or physician   
c. Distractors: none

XI. Case Narrative

a. Scenario background given to participants. Mode of arrival is by personal car. In the emergency department room are the patient, parent (mother or father), and a nurse. The nurse will bring the learner into the room, introduce the patient and family, and the chief complaint of “fever.”

b. Learner is expected to obtain missing information from the parent:

i. History of Present Illness: "My son has been terribly sick the past 5 days. He has been having a fever that won't go away and just won't stop crying. I don't know what to do. The pediatrician said this would all go away. He just looks so uncomfortable." When asked about disease progression - "Well it first started with the fever. And then I noticed the rash on his chest and back two days ago. And most recently I noticed his lips began cracking and his tongue turned bright red. He looks so uncomfortable and won't stop crying."

ii. Past Medical History: "He's an extremely healthy child. Full-term, has received all his immunizations. No reported allergies. We have been giving him Tylenol and ibuprofen for the fever."

iii. Social and Family History: Lives with mother and father, no siblings, no smokers at home, no sick contacts

# c. Scenario conditions initially:

i. History: obtained by interviewing the parent ii. Patient’s initial vital signs in the ED: Heart rate: 140, Respirations: 32, Blood pressure: 95/60, SpO2: $9 9 \%$ on Room Air, Temp: $4 0 \%$ , Weight: $1 5 \mathrm { k g }$

iii. Exam:

1. GENERAL APPEARANCE: irritable, crying, awake, eyes make contact with parent an physician   
2. HEAD, EYES, EARS, NOSE, THROAT: airway patent, fissures on lips, strawberry tongue   
3. RESPIRATORY: tachypneic, otherwise clear bilaterally   
4. CARDIOVASCULAR: sinus tachycardia, no murmur, $2 +$ distal pulses throughout, cap refill $< 2$ sec   
5. ABDOMINAL: soft, non-tender, non-distended, normal bowel sounds   
6. GU: bilaterally descended testes, normal genitalia, circumcised   
7. SKIN: diffuse truncal rash, erythema noted of palms and soles   
8. NEUROLOGIC: intact, no gross deficits

# d. Expected behaviors/actions:

i. Obtain history and perform physical exam ii. Place patient on monitoring and obtain initial ED vital signs   
iii. Obtain intravenous access   
iv. Obtain lab work including: CBC with differential, ESR, CRP, Transaminases, Protein, Albumin, Urinalysis, EKG, Chest X-ray v. Consult parent about performance of an LP (not necessary)   
vi. Explain the leading differential diagnosis and disposition to parent   
vii. Interpret lab results   
viii. Consult with cardiology for performance of an echocardiogram   
ix. Admit the patient to the pediatric service x. May choose to initiate high dose aspirin and IVIG therapy in the ED, but not required for completion of the scenario

# e. Response to treatment:

i. Tylenol administration does not improve fever ii. IV fluid administration will slow down the heart rate mildly

# f. Lab result interpretations:

i. Chest x-ray: normal   
ii. CBC with differential: WBC 18.8K, $9 0 \%$ PMN (typical of KD, leukocytosis with PMN predominance; $\mathrm { W B C } > 1 5 \mathrm { K }$ required for incomplete KD), platelet 510K (thrombocytosis common if disease progresses $> 1$ week, often reaching $> 1 0 0 \mathrm { K } )$   
iii. ESR $6 0 \mathrm { m m / h r }$ , CRP $1 0 \mathrm { m g / d L }$ $( \mathrm { E S R } \ge 4 0 \mathrm { m m / h r }$ and/or CRP ≥ 3 mg/dL required to diagnosis incomplete KD)   
iv. AST/ALT 70/80 (transaminases typically $2 { - } 3 \mathbf { x }$ normal limits, elevated ALT criteria for incomplete KD) v. Protein $1 0 . 2 \ : \mathrm { g / d L }$ (elevated due to high serum immunoglobulin levels)   
vi. Albumin $2 . 0 \ { \mathrm { g / d L } }$ (typically decreased, $< 3 \ \mathbf { g } / \mathrm { d L }$ criteria for incomplete KD)   
vii. CSF: 20 WBC, $2 \%$ PMN, $9 1 \%$ mono, glucose 64, protein 27, negative GS, culture pending (consistent with an aseptic meningitis with mononuclear cell predominance)   
viii. U/A: neg LE, $\mathrm { p H } 6$ , blood 0, ketones 0, glucose 0, nitrite 0, WBC 15, RBC 1, no bacteria, no squamous cells (consistent with sterile pyuria, which is urethral in origin, so need to obtained a bagged specimen; $> 1 0$ WBC in the urine micro required for incomplete KD)   
ix. EKG: sinus tachycardia

# g. Telephone Consultations (anticipated dialogue)

i. Admitting team - "Okay, so you think he might have Kawasaki Disease? What's the story? Current vital signs? Okay, admit him to the floors and if you don't mind can you place a call to cardiology? Thanks."   
ii. Cardiologist - "You think he has Kawasaki Disease? What are your clinical exam findings? How about the labs? Did you get an EKG? Okay, sounds good. I'll see him on the floor and get an echocardiogram performed. I would recommend admitting to the inpatient team to start him on high dose aspirin $1 0 0 \mathrm { m g / k g / d a y }$ divided into four doses as well as IVIG $2 ~ \mathrm { g / k g }$ over 10 hours. The inpatient team can start it up on the floors."   
iii. ID (if consulted) - "You think he has Kawasaki Disease, huh? Did you speak to the cardiologist yet? What is the workup like so far? And you don't think this is measles or Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever or Scarlet Fever? Okay, I'll be by to see him on the floor. Thank you for the consult."

# XII. Instructor’s Notes

a. Tips to keep scenario flowing:

i. Practice performing a lumbar puncture, should the team decide to perform one, is not a tested competency in this scenario. The nurse will prompt the team that the LP has been performed and CSF sent to the lab after the team has consented the parent   
ii. The team can given Tylenol or ibuprofen as antipyretics, but this should not have any notable affect on the patient’s fever   
iii. The nurse can prompt the team early on in the scenario if the team would like the patient undressed, placed on a monitor, and have an IV placed, if they do not specifically instruct the nurse to do so at the beginning of the case   
iv. If the diagnosis is unclear to the team, the nurse can consider prompting them to consider an infectious disease consultation if they believe that an acute infection is the source of the patient’s fever. The ID consultation may then prompt them to consider their diagnosis of Kawasaki Disease vs. Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever vs. Lyme Disease vs. Leptospirosis, etc.

v. Once the patient has been admitted and cardiology has been consulted, the scenario may end. It is not necessary for treatment to be initiated to conclude the case, although it may be ordered.

XIII. Debriefing Plan

a. Method of debriefing: Powerpoint lecture for all participants in the group b. Goals of debriefing:

i. Review basic epidemiology and diagnosis of KD   
ii. Review criteria for diagnosis of incomplete KD   
iii. Review management of KD including consultations, disposition, and medical management (high dose aspirin and IVIG)   
iv. Review main differential diagnosis of “conditions with fever and a rash” in the pediatric population

XIV. Lessons Learned a. Pilot Testing

i. Participants - this case has been run two times with a group of emergency medicine residents as active participants. The two times the case was run, a PGY1 or PGY2 resident was the primary learner of the case, with a PGY3 or PGY4 as a “backup” resident for the case, should the junior resident have any questions or require help. A group of passive participants were also present during the simulation and debriefing. In total, there were 4 active learners and approximately 15 passive learners for the case. Both active and passive learners were asked to provide feedback at the end of the case.

ii. Instructors – The primary author of the case, a PGY2 emergency medicine resident, as well as a pediatric emergency medicine attending each ran one of the two pilot cases. The instructors were familiar with the Laerdal SimBaby software and were responsible for running the program software, providing live feedback to the actors and nurses in the case, and debriefing the case with both the active and passive participants.

b. Modifications: we received generally positive feedback from residents on the case, stating that it was realistic and relevant to their learning. No specific changes were made to the simulation programming after the pilot testing, as the programming was relatively straightforward and no difficulties were encountered with running the simulation. Likewise, the teaching materials and debriefing were mainly kept intact, with the exception of removing images that we used for the case but do not have license to publish.

c. Take home points:

i. The case is most suitable for junior level emergency medicine or pediatric residents

ii. High quality photographs or moulage is essential to give learners diagnostic clues for the diagnosis

iii. The main goals for the case were:

1. Review the epidemiology, clinical features, diagnosis, and management of complete and incomplete Kawasaki Disease.

2. Review of the differential diagnosis of fever and rash in the pediatric patient.
