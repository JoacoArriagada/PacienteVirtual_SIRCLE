**INSTRUCTOR GUIDE**

**Description:**

This OSCE involves a 5-minute patient encounter that assesses a trainees’ basic resuscitation skills in responding to a patient with an un-witnessed collapse in an out-patient clinic setting.

**Target Audience:**

Pre-clinical medical students; nursing students; junior resident physicians or other trainees who have gone through first-responder and BLS training.

**Introduction:**

A 2002 survey found that 64% of US medical schools provided exposure to emergency medicine (EM) in the first year of the curriculum and 54% in the second year1. It has been recommended that medical schools include at least 2 weeks of EM teaching during the preclinical years, including patient assessment, basic procedures and areas unique to EM1.

The first-year medical school curriculum at the Weill Cornell Medical College in Qatar (WCMC-Q) involves an Annual Introductory Emergency Medicine Clinical Skills Course (“Course”), an expanded first responder course2. The Course involves a combination of lectures and small group hands-on teaching sessions, which utilize simulation. Topics covered include basic management of airway, breathing and circulation, and a rapid, systematic approach to common medical and traumatic emergencies. One month after the Course, our students take a Basic Life Support Course. Students do not get any further formal exposure to EM or basic resuscitation skills during the first-year.

This OSCE case was developed to assess the efficacy of this first-year EM curriculum.

**Case Development:**

At WCMC-Q, we routinely utilize OSCEs and Standardized Patients (SPs) for various portions of the pre-clinical and clinical curriculum. We have at our disposal a modern facility, the Clinical Skills Center (CSC), with mock outpatient examination rooms, one-way observation windows, and video recording for these OSCEs.

This 5-minute OSCE case was developed by the Director of the Course, an EM physician (author HG); the Assistant Dean for Clinical Curriculum and Medical Student Education (author SS); and the Director of the CSC (author LS), who is in charge of SP training for all OSCEs at our medical college.

The OSCE case was developed through multiple trials with several SPs functioning as the “student” and other SPs acting out the “friend’s” role. Many of our SPs hold previous medical degrees (such as RNs and physical therapists) and so were able to give constructive feedback, which was used to guide the case development. Our SPs have extensive experience using OSCE scoring checklists to evaluate student performances and their input was used in developing the scoring checklist for this OSCE. SP input was also applied in finalizing the patient’s script, and they emphasized using short, unambiguous and realistic phrases.

The case was developed to be as realistic as possible, despite reliance on a mannequin. Case run-throughs were key in determining the case flow, timing of cues, overall case length, and room set-up (for example, in deciding where and how the mannequin should be positioned relative to the video cameras to maximize the evaluator’s ability to assess student’s actions). Case run-throughs were repeated and the case was edited until the authors were confident that the case would run smoothly on the actual exam day.

A “breathing assessment” was not made a critical action in this case since a pulse-less patient is assumed to not be breathing. The 2010 AHA guidelines encourage laypersons, untrained rescuers, and trained but not proficient rescuers to do “Hands Only” CPR to encourage initiation of and minimize delays in, chest compressions. Since our Course had trained our students on the performance of airway maneuvers and mouth- to -mouth ventilation, we included these two critical actions in the OSCE. However, based on the new AHA guidelines, instructors may choose to make these two critical actions optional, or include a discussion of the change in priority of these actions in post-OSCE feedback sessions.

Since the case includes an un-witnessed collapse, students are expected to do a jaw thrust maneuver to open the patient’s airway due to the possibility of a traumatic cervical spine injury. As it is not be possible for one rescuer to keep the airway open using a jaw thrust maneuver and still perform adequate mouth to mouth ventilations, the grading checklist for the airway maneuver critical action gives the students credit if they performed a jaw thrust maneuver first but does not grade them down if they follow the jaw thrust with a head tilt chin lift maneuver to keep the airway open.

The case was refined after the first actual run of the OSCE (spring, 2012). We incorporated timed cues to provide a more structured flow to the case. Furthermore, we edited the ‘friend’s’ script to make her reports on the patient’s status unambiguous. When the student checks the mannequin’s pulse, earlier versions of the script had the friend saying: “I don’t think she has a pulse.” However, many students disagreed with the friend and told her during the case, “No, she does have a pulse.” We therefore, edited the case to have the SP check the patients’ pulse herself (for realism) and say “She does not have a pulse!”

The OSCE was given as a “surprise” assessment to our students at the end of their first year, approximately 9 months after the Course. Students were not cued to study for its contents beforehand, in order to truly assess retention of first- responder skills and knowledge.

After the OSCE, students were given brief face-to-face feedback on their performance by the author HG (who had reviewed parts of their OSCE performances in real-time). They were also given a scored checklist of their performance. In this way, we were able to use the OSCE to assess and reinforce the EM management concepts taught in the Course and BLS.

Medical educators can easily use this OSCE as a long-term assessment tool, as we did, but several variations are also possible. It can be used immediately after a first-responder and BLS-type training course in order to assess immediate efficacy of the courses. Students can be told beforehand that they will be taking an OSCE to assess their EM knowledge and basic resuscitation skills in order to encourage students to review the material. The feedback to students after the OSCE can be done in a more traditional lecture format to review management of the case and re-emphasize teaching points.

Reuesseler et al3 (“simulation”) have suggested that scenario based Emergency Case OSCEs (ECOs) in which students manage an emergency situation from initial assessment to medical treatment, are a more valid and authentic measure of student capability than traditional OSCEs but that multiple OSCEs are needed for adequate assessment. This OSCE can be used in combination with other OSCEs as well as a teaching tool to supplement lectures and small group teaching of basic EM concepts and basic resuscitation skills

**Goals:**

The goal of this 5-minute encounter is to assess a trainees’ ability to function as a first-responder and initiate BLS in an out-patient clinic setting.

**Objectives:**

To gauge student competence in the following critical actions:

1. Assess responsiveness, pulse, and airway patency in a pulse-less patient with an un-witnessed collapse
2. Airway repositioning using a jaw-thrust maneuver in a victim of trauma/un-witnessed collapse
3. Knowing when to call for an AED
4. Performing CPR, demonstrating correct technique of chest compressions and mouth- to- mouth ventilations
5. Calling EMS in an emergency situation and providing adequate verbal hand-off to EMS
6. Managing a scene and using available resources during emergency situations

**Case Overview:**

The OSCE is meant to be a 5-minute encounter, with an overhead announcement indicating an end to the encounter. Students are provided a brief information sheet introducing the case on the doorway of the examination room, after which they are told to enter the room to begin the encounter.

The context of the case is that of a patient presenting to an out-patient clinic complaining of vague chest pain. The encounter begins as the student enters the office and a “friend” (i.e. SP) who had accompanied Mona to the office visit, informs the student of the situation. The friend notes that she had stepped out of the room for a minute, and when she returned, Mona was on the ground. The patient, Mona, is a mannequin on the ground. The student is expected to perform several critical actions, including assess Mona’s responsiveness, check for a pulse, initiate and continue chest compressions and mouth to mouth ventilation, call for EMS and an AED, and communicate with EMS personnel. Students are expected to open the patient’s airway using a jaw thrust maneuver since it is an unwitnessed collapse and cervical spine injury is a possibility. They are also expected to inform EMS that the patient had been complaining of chest pain, as this may be a cause of her collapse.

Each student encounter is evaluated by the SP using a detailed checklist, to be completed at the end of each encounter, ideally utilizing video review of the performance. Video review of each OSCE performance also allows the Course director to confirm the SP’s evaluations.

**Results:**

The OSCE at our institution was run at the Clinical Skills Center in the spring of 2012 (n= 43) and 2013 (n=38), with all students in the first-year class participating.

Student OSCE performance was scored out of a total of 10 points which was used to calculate a percentage score for each student.

Percentage scores were reported to each student with the graded checklist, which detailed the breakdown of their scores. The OSCE was meant to be formative for the students and to provide feedback on the course’s efficacy for the course directors. The OSCE grade did not contribute to the students’ overall medical school grades, although our OSCE checklist can easily be used as a grading tool as well.

The OSCE was successful in assessing student performance of the critical actions, and highlighting areas that require emphasis and review for our students. We found that students had the most difficulty remembering to check for a pulse prior to starting chest compressions (only 51.2% did this), open the airway using a jaw thrust (2.2%) rather than a head-tilt/chin-lift maneuver (74.4%), calling for an AED (37.2%), and the ratio of chest compressions to ventilations. Only 34.9% performed the correct number of chest compressions, and only 27.9% gave at least 2 breaths per cycle.

**Practical Information:**

**Outline:** A patient presenting to an out-patient clinic with vague chest pain collapses while waiting to see the physician in the office. The student finds the patient, Mona, a mannequin, on the ground of the office accompanied by a “friend” (i.e. the SP). The student is expected to perform several critical actions until EMS arrival.

**Length of session**: 5 minutes per student

**Faculty/Facilitator needs per encounter:** 1 Standardized Patients; 1 faculty responsible for student and SP movements into and out of the room and to ensure overhead cues are running at appropriate times; 1 AV personnel for videotaping, which is recommended

**Materials:** 1 Laerdal Little Anne mannequin (product/manufacturer #2002401)\* dressed in a hospital gown; Pre recorded timed cues and announcements to be played overhead during case; Laerdal Little Anne mannequin disposable airways; A fake telephone receiver/head and mouthpiece (for giving report to EMS).

\*Allows airway opening maneuvers and “clicks” when chest compression depth is at least 2 inches.

**Room setup:**

1. Set the room up to resemble an out-patient physician’s office
2. Remove phone from room
