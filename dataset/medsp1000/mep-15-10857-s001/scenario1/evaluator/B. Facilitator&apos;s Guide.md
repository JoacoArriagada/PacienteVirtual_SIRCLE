**“Brown Bag” medication reconciliation simulation: Facilitator’s Guide**

**II. Simulation Activity**

1. **Overview:** one learner will be designated as the “patient.” The other two learners will play the role of the healthcare professional. The full duration of the activity is 45 minutes total:
   1. Facilitator(s) to dispense materials (1-3 minutes)
   2. Orientation to activity (1-2 minutes)
   3. “Brown Bag” review within groups of 3-6 (20 minutes)
   4. Debriefing and discussion as a large group (20 minutes)

If time does not allow for the full 45-minute period, the simulation may be condensed to 10-15 minutes and the debriefing to 10-15 minutes for a 30-minute total class time. For our pilot testing, the simulation activity was completed in a 30-minute period.

1. **Ideal Space:** a large room with areas for smaller groups to congregate around tables.
2. **Materials:** The facilitator will use this document as a guide during the session.
   1. The facilitator will provide the pre-filled patient “Brown Bags” (one per group, **Appendix C: Demonstration Guide**).
   2. Each “patient” will need a copy of **Appendix A: Standardized Patient Case Development Tool** (1 per group).
   3. Each learner will need a writing utensil and a copy of **Appendix E: Handout for Learners.**
3. **Pre-surveys:** Immediately prior to the simulation activity, learners may complete an optional self-efficacy pre-survey (**Appendix F**). The self-efficacy survey may take learners approximately 5 minutes to complete. Learners may also complete an optional knowledge pre-survey (**Appendix G**, answer key **Appendix H**) which may take 7-10 minutes to complete. This may be done prior to the session start or while the facilitator(s) distribute materials.
4. **Getting Started**:
   1. The “patients” are instructed to review **Appendix A: Standardized Patient Case Development Tool** to become familiar with their role for 2-3 minutes. During this time, the facilitator will distribute the pre-filled “Brown Bags” to each patient.
   2. When he or she is ready, the “patient” informs the learners that:

*“This bag contains all of my medications. I brought them all in today to go over with you. I fill my own pillbox and I refill it independently every Thursday. Today is Monday. Go ahead and ask me any questions that you have about my medicines.”*

* 1. The patient will then instruct the learners to open the “Brown Bag” and review the contents including the pillbox, medication vials, and associated medications/supplies (i.e., inhalers).

1. **Session** **flow**- The goal is for the learners to make connections between the patient’s medication list, the instructions on the pill bottles and how the pillbox is prepared by the patient.
   1. The facilitator may migrate around the room to observe, offer insight or probe questions to promote discussions.
   2. If the participant that is playing the role of the “patient” is asked a question related to something that is not included in **Appendix A: Standardized Patient Case Development Tool**, he or she may either respond with “I don’t know” or they may choose to improvise.
   3. If the learners appear to be stuck or cannot uncover additional existing issues, the “patient” may cue the learners based on the errors found in **Appendix A: Standardized Patient Case Development Tool.**
   4. After 20 minutes of “Brown Bag” review, the facilitator will call the group together.

**III. Debriefing**

1. **Method**: Informal and open-ended, group discussion. No additional materials are necessary for the debriefing.
2. **Duration**: 20 minutes. The following outline lists several teaching points. Facilitators likely will not get through everything below. The debriefing may be expanded beyond 20 minutes as time allows. This may be done by using the **For Further Discussion** points below.
3. **Purpose:** The purpose of this debriefing is to first discuss errors identified by learners during the simulation and to connect these errors with teaching points.
   * Errors within the simulation were based on common medication management errors seen in practice (labeled **Potential Medication Concern** below)
   * Each **Potential Medication Concern** is paired with a more specific description of the error within the simulation (labeled **Simulation Patient Example**)
   * During the debriefing, facilitators will discuss the associated **Teaching Point** with learners, and may choose to delve deeper into these teaching points via the **For Further Discussion** section and **Possible Learner Responses** are provided as examples.

| --- | --- |
| **Potential Medication Concern #1:** | **Patient taking expired or old medications1,2** |
| **Simulation Patient Example:** | Patient has expired or outdated medications in his brown bag that he is actively taking (zolpidem and insulin pen) |
| **Teaching Point:** | The “brown bag” method for medication reconciliation is the gold standard. In this format, patients bring in all of their prescription and over-the-counter medications and supplements, including medication organizers like a pillbox. The “brown bag” method yields information we may have missed using electronic records alone, such as expired medication bottles or additional medication that are not on the patient’s list. |
| **For Further Discussion:** | What strategies can we use to reduce the risk of patient’s taking expired or outdated medications? |
| **Possible Learner Responses:** | Learners may discuss medication disposal techniques, such as referral to the local police station or Drug Take-back Days. Learners may also suggest frequent medication reconciliation via the “brown bag” method in order to keep abreast of medication changes, and appropriate discarding of outdated or expired medications. |

| **Simulation Patient Example:** | Patient has kept insulin and zolpidem (previously stopped/expired medications) and has purchased an over-the-counter “PM cold medicine” which he is taking at night. |
| **Teaching Points:** | * When possible a “brown bag” method for medication reconciliation should be used (additional details described above) * Ask open-ended questions about over-the-counter medication use * Ask the patient if he or she is taking any medications that are **not** on the medication list |
| **For Further Discussion:** | What are some examples of open-ended questions that you can use to uncover these types of errors in medication management? |

| **Potential Medication Concern #3:** | **Certain patient factors, including factors related to aging, may lead to errors in medication management1-3** |
| **Simulation Patient Example:** | * Visual impairment: accidentally mixing two different medications in one bottle * Trouble swallowing: inappropriately cuts and crushes a long-acting medication (potassium XR) due to trouble swallowing the large pills * Low health literacy vs. cognitive impairment: patient only using one of his three total inhalers and is using it incorrectly |
| **Teaching Points:** | Effective medication reconciliation techniques may elicit these patient factors and associated errors, including:   * Ask the patient to explain his or her medication management system, especially when you notice:   + Cut or crushed medications in pill bottles or in the pillbox   + Two distinct pills in the same pill bottle * Ask the patient to demonstrate inhaler technique and the frequency of inhaler use in a given week * Health literacy assessment and/or cognitive assessment |
| **For Further Discussion:** | What are some examples of open-ended questions that you can use to identify the above patient factors that made medication management challenging? |
| **Possible Learner Responses:** | Learners may respond with suggestions for open-ended questions that they used in the simulation or new questions that they could have used. Examples may include:  ***“It looks like this bottle has two different pills inside. Can you tell me more about that?”***  ***“Pills can be small and difficult to keep track of. How often do you have difficulty seeing or manipulating your pills?”***  ***“Some pills can be quite large. How often do you have trouble swallowing your pills?”***  ***“To make sure your inhaler is working properly, would you mind showing me how***  ***you use this inhaler?”*** |

| **Potential Medication Concern #4:** | **More than one medication administration time may lead to patients skipping or missing doses4-6** |
| **Simulation Patient Example:** | * Metoprolol tartrate is a twice a day medication but the patient takes both doses of this medications at once * Glipizide is a three times a day medication but the patient only takes it twice a day, as all of his other medications are once or twice a day |
| **Teaching Points:** | * Consider what medications can be switched to a long-acting medication to decrease administration times to help improve adherence * Consider how often a patient currently takes medications when adding a medication that is twice daily or greater to align prescribing with patient preferences and routines |
| **For Further Discussion:** | What medications with multiple administration times can be adjusted in this patient’s medication regimen to improve adherence? |
| **Possible Learner Responses:** | * Metoprolol twice daily 🡪 long-acting formulation once daily * Glipizide decreased to once daily or deprescribed, depending on patient-specific goals |

| **Potential Medication Concern #5:** | **Medication adverse effects can lead to patients skipping doses and can contribute to polypharmacy7-9** |
| **Simulation Patient Example:** | Patient intentionally skipping doses of furosemide due to increased urination |
| **Teaching Points:** | Medications side effects may lead to:   * Missed medication doses * A prescribing cascade8 (for example a new provider may not recognize frequent urination as a side effect of furosemide and may misdiagnosis a new condition, urinary incontinence for which they may prescribe another medication). |
| **For Further Discussion:** | * What change can be made to the furosemide dosing to prevent this patient from unwanted side effects at night while maintaining an acceptable volume status? * What prescribing cascades or adverse effects could result in our patient case, starting with the side effect of increased urination from furosemide? |
| **Possible Learner Responses:** | * Switch furosemide from twice daily (AM and PM) to #2 tablets once a day in the morning * Increased urination not recognized as a side effect and misdiagnosed as a new condition (urinary incontinence) 🡪 Urinary anticholinergic agent prescribed 🡪 constipation from anticholinergic side effects 🡪 treated with docusate * Trouble sleeping not recognized as a side effect related to the nocturia misdiagnosed as a new condition (insomnia) 🡪 sleep aid (zolpidem/diphenhydramine) 🡪 cognitive adverse effects/daytime sedation/falls |

| **Potential Medication Concern #6:** | **Glipizide has a high hypoglycemic risk and may not be indicated for diabetes depending on patient-specific A1c goals10-12** |
| **Simulation Patient Example:** | Patient is older and is taking glipizide twice a day for diabetes without regards to meal times |
| **Teaching Points:** | * Glucose-dependent diabetes medications have a high risk for hypoglycemia when taken without eating (sulfonylureas) * Individualized hemoglobin A1c goals in older adults should be based on duration of diabetes, other comorbidities, and patient goals of care (see AGS 2013 guideline and AGS Choosing Wisely Campaign)   1. For example, a 71-year-old with a 20+ year history of diabetes and end-stage heart failure may be indicated for an A1c goal of 8-9% rather than a more stringent goal   2. Deprescribing unsafe medications can reduce the risk of hypoglycemia   3. Prescribing medications with a nonglucose-dependent mechanism of action (ex: SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-IV inhibitors) may reduce the risk of hypoglycemia |
| **For Further Discussion:** | * What should we consider when setting an individualized A1c goal for our patient? * What changes might we make to our patient’s diabetes regimen to reduce the risk of hypoglycemia? |
| **Possible Learner Responses:** | * Age, comorbidities, duration of disease, hypoglycemia, microvascular complications * Deprescribe glipizide and check an A1c 3 months later to see if another diabetes medication is needed; if renal function permits, consider oral agents with low hypoglycemic risk (metformin, DPP4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors) |

| **Potential Medication Concern #7:** | **Some medications on an older patient’s list may be AGS Beer’s Criteria Potentially Inappropriate Medications6,11,13** |
| **Simulation Patient Example:** | Patient is taking zolpidem and “PM cold medicine” which may contain diphenhydramine every night to help with sleep. Both are on the AGS Beer’s Criteria. |
| **Teaching Points:** | * Use the AGS Beer’s Criteria to identify potentially high-risk medications in older adults (see iGeriatrics downloadable application for smartphones or tablets) * Safe approaches to deprescribing high-risk medications have been published and can guide efforts to reduce potentially inappropriate medication use13 |
| **For Further Discussion:** | * What potentially inappropriate medications is our patient taking? * Describe a deprescribing plan for one of these medications |
| **Possible Learner Responses:** | * Glipizide, zolpidem, insulin (though not using), “PM cold medicine” * Stop glipizide and check an A1c 3 months later to see if another diabetes medication is needed * Stop zolpidem and “PM cold medicine” (contains diphenhydramine) for sleep, reinforce good sleep hygiene and consider melatonin if needed * Properly dispose of insulin |
