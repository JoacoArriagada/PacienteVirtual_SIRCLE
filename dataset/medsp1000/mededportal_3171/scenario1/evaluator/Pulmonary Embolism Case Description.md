I. Title: Simulated case of Pulmonary Embolism

II. Target Audience: $3 ^ { \mathrm { r d } }$ year medical students

III. Objectives for Human Patient Simulator Exercises

# A. Primary Objectives

1. Obtain a focused history and physical examination pertinent to the chief complaint in the scenario.   
2. Develop appropriate differential diagnoses for dyspnea with chest pain and perform diagnostic decision making skills in a time sensitive manner.   
3. Interpret diagnostic information correctly.   
4. Develop appropriate therapeutic decision making skills in a time sensitive manner.   
5. Understand the treatment of pulmonary embolism.   
6. Document complete hospital admission orders for the simulated patient.

# B. Secondary Objectives

1. Communicate with the simulated patient in a respectful and courteous manner.   
2. Keep patient adequately and sensitively informed of decisions and therapies.   
3. Work as a member of a team to provide care to the patient

# V. Actors

A. Roles 1. Patient (voice of the mannequin) - optimally this is done from a control room where the student cannot see the person with the microphone for the simulator. This role should be the instructor or someone that understands the scenario. 2. Nurse or assistant – this person functions to take lab, EKG and CXR data to the student during the scenario. They also administer drugs ordered by the student.

C. Scenario branch points

1. With supplemental oxygen the pulse ox should elevate slightly but not correct the dyspnea or hypoxia completely.   
2. If student gives a nebulized beta agonist the heart rate should be increased further without change in hypoxia or dyspnea.

# VII. Instructor’s Notes

This scenario is designed to teach students about middle aged patients who present with the acute onset of dyspnea. The student should think through the differential diagnosis of this chief complaint and order appropriate testing to ultimately arrive at the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism.

The process which the student should follow includes:

 Performing a focused history and physical examination. (See checklist) . Formulating a differential diagnosis for the chief complaint.  Ordering appropriate diagnostic tests  Interpreting the diagnostic tests to make the diagnosis of a pulmonary embolism.  Knowing the most appropriate treatment for pulmonary embolism (IV heparin) Recognizing the acute problem of pulse oximetry below $9 0 \%$ and starting supplemental oxygen (very early upon entering the exam room)..

The scenario ends at the point where the student has appropriately ordered all pertinent studies, reviewed them and come to a logical differential diagnosis and treatment plan.

If a student begins to proceed down a wrong diagnostic and therapeutic path (i.e. giving albuterol and antibiotics for a presumed diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) the instructor should gently try to steer the student back on track with further history. If this is not successful the case can be stopped and a debriefing session is initiated. The simulator should not be allowed to expire or proceed to a pulseless rhythm as this is beyond a third year student expertise and can be traumatizing for a student.

A. Tips to keep scenario flowing –   
1. In our lab we have a one-way window into the control room. The student is able to write “orders” on pieces of paper and place them against the glass. These orders are things like “CBC, Chem panel” or “CXR.” The assistant then delivers the results to the student. Alternatively, orders could be given to an assistant who will then obtain the data and distribute to the student.   
2. Occasionally a student will get diverted to an alternate diagnosis such as asthma or pneumonia. I continue the scenario anyway and then use the feedback session to discuss why pulmonary embolism is more likely.   
3. If the student orders a CT scan (which they should do as indicated on the checklist) I have the assistant tell them the scanner is backed up for half an hour. This way they have to continue thinking about what to do to arrive at the diagnosis (likewise with a lower extremity ultrasound). This is realistic as these tests are often delayed in an emergency room and the idea is to stimulate continued thought and therapeutics for the likely diagnosis B. Tips to actors – mentioned in other sections

C. Scenario programming

1. Optimal management path:

 As mentioned I alter the pulse ox slightly for supplemental oxygen. After all the data have come back and if the student starts IV heparin I generally stop the session rather than alter the physiology (heparin wouldn’t alter PE physiology quickly anyway).

2. Potential complications path

The patient should just continue to be dyspneic during the scenario. I think it is beyond the scope of third year students to have any complications develop.

VIII. Debriefing Plan

A. Method of debriefing –

Once the student has taken the scenario to its logical completion or at a hopeless impasse end the scenario and walk into the room to give direct and immediate feedback.   
Start by asking them to explain their reasoning. “So tell me what you thought was going on with Dan?” Give them time to explain their reasoning and then go through the history, physical, lab, EKG and CXR findings one by one. Show them the checklist and let them keep it.   
Ask if they have any questions.   
End the session by asking them to write a set of admission orders for the patient and give them written feedback on the order set at a later date.

B. Debriefing materials – labs, EKG, CXR and checklist

C. Rules for debriefing –

Students tend to be a little intimidated by the simulator and feedback so I keep it positive. Try to say something positive about what they did during the session.

D. Questions to facilitate the debriefing

Tell me what you thought was going on with Dan? How did the laboratory data back up your leading diagnosis? Show me how you interpreted the EKG? CXR?  Is there anything you could have done therapeutically while awaiting the CT scan? (Heparin!) What did you learn from this experience that you will apply to the next patient you see with dyspnea?

IX. Pilot Testing and Revisions

A. Numbers of participants – I usually have 2 students in the room with one functioning as the decision maker and the other as a nurse or scribe. Roles are reversed and a different scenario is given to the second student. Each student usually takes 15-20 minutes to complete the scenario and another 10 minutes for feedback.

B. Performance expectations, anticipated management mistakes –

It is fairly rare for a student to make the jump from diagnosis to therapy in this scenario.   
Probably only $2 0 \%$ will start the heparin (possibly they are not sure they have the diagnosis without the CT scan).   
It is very instructive to point out that they need to think about treatment before the diagnosis is clear.

# Appendix 2 Electrocardiogram

The attached PDF file is a copy of an electrocardiogram from a patient with a large pulmonary embolus. It demonstrates right axis deviation, including a large S wave in lead I. It also has a small Q in lead III (insignificant by criteria) and inverted T waves in III, AVF, V1, V2, V3, V4, V5 and flat T’s in V6. There is poor R wave progression across the precordial leads. It is useful to have the student interpret the EKG and then point out the abnormal findings. If an old EKG was present this EKG alone would be very suspicious for a pulmonary embolus (see attached PDF labeled “pulmonary embolism ekg”).
