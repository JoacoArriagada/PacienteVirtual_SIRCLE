# INSTRUCTOR’S GUIDE FOR CARBON MONOXIDE/INHALATIONAL POISONING SIMULATION SCENARIO

I. Title: “An adolescent boy with systemic toxicity due to smoke inhalation”

# III. Overview:

William Robinson is a 16-year-old generally healthy boy who is found by EMS personnel in a smoky room adjacent to the source of a house fire. Initially confused with complaints of headache and nausea, he becomes obtunded en route to the emergency department. The learners must perform a rapid basic assessment for evidence of burn injuries, provide initial critical care, and must identify the likelihood of inhalation toxicity – both carbon monoxide and cyanide – and must initiate directed therapy, including preparation for hyperbaric therapy and antidote(s) to cyanide.

# VI. Implementation

This case was developed using the Laerdal SimMan and its associated software, though it can be easily adapted to a variety of low- or high-fidelity simulation manikins. Included in this case guide are the associated program files, Powerpoint slides, and pictures. Availability of a projector or computer monitor to display radiographic and

electrocardiographic studies is helpful but not necessary (if not available, print outs of the pictures may be provided for the participants).

SimMan can be prepared with soot and smoky moulage. To add an olfactory component to the assessment, “smoke water” made with liquid smoke hickory seasoning can be applied to clothing either directly or using a spray bottle.

There should be 2-3 physician participants, 1-2 nurse actors, and an actor to play the role of prehospital personnel. If additional actors are available, they may take the roles of respiratory therapists or additional hospital personnel. The facilitator should be able to run the simulation software, and may serve as the over-the-phone consultants for the case. A real-time audience may be present to observe the case. The encounter should take approximately 15-20 minutes to complete. Rehearsal will ensure that the simulation programs – including handlers and trends – run smoothly, that actors understand the progression of the scenario, and that necessary resources are available.

# VII. Limitations

Apart from basic moulage, there are few visual cues to suggest toxicity from inhalational toxins. Emphasis, therefore, should be placed on the lack of surface burns in this case, to avoid participants being sidetracked by thermal burn management. Actors’ cues may be the most important guiding points in assisting participants to recall the importance of inhaled toxins. These cues should be tailored to the learning level of the participants to avoid “leading” the learners.

# IX. Environment

a. Lab Set Up: Emergency Department (ED) critical care room

b. Manikin Set Up: High fidelity SimMan manikin driven by Laerdal software i. Patient’s attire: Sim Man in civilian clothes, sprayed with smoke-water for olfactory effect. ii. Moulage: sooty moulage to the face and hands
c. Props: chest x-ray, electrocardiogram, laboratory results sheet
d. Distractors: none

X. Actors

a. Roles: EMS personnel, nurses, respiratory therapists as available. By phone: toxicologist
b. Who may play them: actor, nurse, nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or physician
c. Action Role: The actors primarily serve as informants (particularly the EMS personnel early in the case, and the nurses as the case progresses). Actors also are present to perform roles and tasks that are not the typical responsibilities of physicians, allowing the physician-learners to focus on the knowledge and behaviors that will allow them to perform in their clinical roles in the emergency department.

XI. Case Narrative

a. Presentation: A 16 yo adolescent was found trapped inside a smoky room during a house fire. He was initially awake but confused, complaining of headache and nausea, no signs of thermal burn or other injury. Cervical spine immobilized, patient on backboard.

# b. Initial call from EMS en route from the scene:

“We are en route to your facility with a 16 year old found down by firefighters at the scene of a house fire - he was found stuck in a room next to where the fire itself was, but couldn't make it out because the fire blocked his exit. He was really confused when we found him, said he had a terrible headache and was going to vomit. We administered oxygen by non-rebreather, he has an 18 gauge in the right AC. On the way here, he went from being confused to being pretty much unconscious.”

En route he becomes obtunded. As he arrives, he is becoming more tachypneic and unresponsive, progressing to respiratory failure.

c. Learner is expected to obtain any additional details from EMS personnel before they exit:

i. History of Present Illness: Essentially as above – there are no parents or other bystanders to corroborate the history. A fingerstick blood glucose measurement of $1 1 8 ~ \mathrm { m g / d L }$ will be reported if elicited by the learners.
ii. Past Medical History: Unknown, including medications or allergies
iii. Social and Family History: Unknown

# d. Scenario conditions:

i. History: limited history obtained from EMS personnel

ii. Patient’s initial vital signs in the ED: Heart rate: 118 bpm, Respirations: 26/minute, Blood pressure: 92/50 mm Hg, SpO2: $9 6 \%$ on Room Air, Temp: $3 7 \mathrm { ^ { \circ } C }$ , Weight: $7 0 \mathrm { k g }$

iii. Exam:

1. General: Obtunded, flinches to painful stimuli

2. Airway: Patent, no burns or singed hairs, no soot in the nares or mouth

3. Breathing: Tachypneic, with deep respirations, but essentially clear

4. Circulation: The skin is pink and moist, but capillary refill is delayed at 3-4 seconds

5. HEENT: Dirt (moulage) is noted on the face, but there is no soot in nose, nor singed nasal hairs

6. Neck : Cervical collar is in place, there are no visible or palpable injuries

7. Chest: Clear, equal chest rise

8. Abd: Soft

9. GU: Normal male

10. Skin: No obvious burns, pink, dry

11. Neuro: Obtunded, non-purposeful movements

12. Extremities: No evidence of major trauma

# f. Additional possible interventions:

i. Trauma activation

1. Indicated on a case-by-case and institution-specific basis if there is history or exam findings concerning for traumatic injury. In this case, we specifically have omitted any evidence of trauma, as it may distract from the learning objectives

ii. Vasopressors (dopamine, epinephrine/norepinephrine)

1. The specific choices will be dependent on participant preference and to some degree on institutional norms, though some circulatory support in addition to fluid resuscitation will likely be ordered.

iii. Referral for hyperbaric $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$

1. Available resources will vary based on location, and its utility remains controversial, especially in the unstable pediatric patient. In any case, however, discussion of and referral for hyperbaric therapy should not delay the appropriate administration of $1 0 0 \% \mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ , aggressive fluid resuscitation and other supportive critical care.

# g. Response to treatment:

i. With appropriate intervention:

1. Airway intubation will allow for continued adequate oxygenation and improved ventilation
2. IV fluid administration will slow down the heart rate temporarily
3. Once cyanide antidote is administered, the patient's vitals will begin to normalize over the course of 15-20 minutes (this can be compressed in the interest of time).

4. Patient remains critically ill, but stabilizes ii. Without appropriate/expected interventions:

1. Without appropriate fluid resuscitation and antidote therapy, vital signs continue to deteriorate – HR rises, BP drops.

2. If the learner provides airway management, fluid resuscitation and pressor support only, but does not recognize the need for specific cyanide antidote therapy, the team may buy time to re-evaluate but will ultimately need to identify the metabolic derangements related to cyanide toxicity.

3. The patient will become increasingly acidotic and may progress toward PEA/VFib arrest if appropriate airway and antidote therapy is not instituted. We recommend temporarily allowing return of circulation if these arrhythmias are treated appropriately (the focus is meant to be on identification of cyanide toxicity rather than PALS/ACLS algorithms).

4. The patient may die during the scenario if appropriate antidote therapy is not considered despite cues from actors.

# h. Lab result interpretations:

i. Chest x-ray: no focal opacities are noted – if performed after intubation, there will be an ETT in the trachea above the carina
ii. CBC with differential: WBC $5 . 9 \mathrm { ~ x ~ } 1 0 ^ { 3 } / \mu \mathrm { l }$ , $\mathrm { H g b } 1 2 . 5 \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { d L }$ , Hct $3 8 \%$ , platelet $4 1 9 \mathrm { ~ x ~ } 1 0 ^ { 3 } / \mu \mathrm { l }$ -- unremarkable
iii. iStat: $\mathrm { p H } 6 . 9 5 $ , $\mathsf { p C O } _ { 2 } 2 5$ , $\mathrm { P O } _ { 2 }$ 120, Lactate 10.4, carboxyhemoglobin $1 8 . 1 \%$ -- There is clear metabolic acidosis with normal $\mathrm { P O } _ { 2 }$ . Significant elevation of lactic acid should be a clue to the presence of cyanide toxicity. The carboxyhemoglobin level is also significantly elevated, but should not in and of itself lead to this degree of acidosis.
iv. Chem 7: Na 138 mEq/L, $\mathrm { \Omega } \mathrm { \Omega } \mathrm { \Omega } \mathrm { \Omega } \langle 3 \mathrm { m E q / L }$ , Cl $1 0 2 \mathrm { m E q / L }$ , $\mathrm { H C O _ { 3 } } 6 \mathrm { m E q / L }$ , BUN 15 $\mathrm { m g / d L }$ , $\mathrm { C r 0 . 8 \ m g / d L }$ , Gluc 124 mg/dL – confirms acidosis, as above.
v. EKG: sinus narrow-complex tachycardia
vi. Available later (recommended to be reported after the case conclusion): Cyanide $1 \mathrm { m g / L }$ (39 umol/L) – confirms elevation of cyanide. However, the team should certainly not wait for this confirmation prior to initiating empiric treatment

# i. Telephone Consultations (anticipated dialogue)

i. Toxicologist: Recommend a blood gas, carboxyhemoglobin, and a lactate to evaluate for intoxication due to smoke inhalation. Based on the acidosis and the high lactate, recommend cyanide antidote (Cyanokit, hydroxocobalamin - 5g is the adult dose, $7 0 \mathrm { m g / k g }$ is the peds dosing, given over 15 minutes). If you don't have the Cyanokit, you can use the IV part of the traditional cyanide antidote kit (formerly the “Lilly Kit” - IV sodium thiosulfate, 7g/sq. meter body surface area, max 12.5g, given over 10 minutes). Be aware that the other components of the traditional “Lilly Kit” may lead to methemoglobinemia and exacerbate any hypoxia related to concomitant carbon monoxide poisoning. Given his mental status and his carboxyhemoglobin level in the setting of smoke inhalation, you should assume concomitant carbon monoxide intoxication. Administer $1 0 0 \% \mathrm { F i O } _ { 2 }$ , and once the patient stabilizes, consider initiating contact with nearby facilities with hyperbaric chamber options. They may or may not be able to accommodate a critically ill adolescent. As always, consider co-ingestions or other intoxication as well.

j. Additional scripted cues (we recommend that not all cues be routinely provided - these may be used or withheld at the facilitator’s discretion):

i. The bedside nurse should comment on the bright red color of the venous blood obtained while placing an IV.
ii. Nurse or other actor may comment on the relatively high venous $\mathrm { P O } _ { 2 }$ . Actors may also suggest possible interventions for carbon monoxide, toxicology (available anywhere in North America via regional poison control centers: 1- 800-222-1222), and cyanide antidote, as needed.
