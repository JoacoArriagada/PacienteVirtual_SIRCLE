Headache OSCE Part 3: A 22-year old woman presents to the emergency department after waking up with the sudden onset of a severe headache

# FACILITATOR’S GUIDE

Resource description

This 15-minute OSCE is part 3 in a series. It requires that the examinee take a detailed headache history from a standardized patient, generate an initial differential diagnosis, request and interpret diagnostic tests, and defend the final diagnosis. Five additional minutes for immediate and structured feedback are recommended. Criteria for passing and a grading system and have been established. Remediation plans are prescribed, depending on the type of error made by the examinee.

Learning objectives

The objectives of this OSCE are aligned with the following curriculum guidelines: the neurology clerkship core curriculum guidelines, endorsed by the American Academy of Neurology (AAN)i ; the recommended curriculum guidelines for family medicine residentsii, endorsed by the American Academy of Family Physicians; and the core curriculum for a first-year neurology resident, endorsed by the AAN’s Headache and Facial Pain Sectioniii.

1. To systematically evaluate a patient with headache.   
2. To obtain a complete and reliable history.   
3. To formulate a differential diagnosis based on lesion localization, time course, and relevant   
historical and demographic features.   
4. To interpret abnormal findings of the neurologic examination.   
5. To use and interpret common tests used in diagnosing neurologic disease.   
6. To recognize and evaluate potentially life-threatening neurologic conditions.

Literature search

Non-traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a serious cause of secondary headache that affects 30,000 patients in North America annually. Due to a wide spectrum of presentations, misdiagnosis of SAH has been reported to occur in a significant proportion of cases. Headache, the most common chief complaint, may be an isolated finding. The neurological examination may be normal and neck stiffness absent. Physicians must decide which patients to evaluate beyond history and physical examination. Computed tomography (CT) scanning and lumbar puncture (LP) are straightforward, but each test has important limitations. CT sensitivity falls with time from onset of symptoms and is lower in mildly affected patients.

Managing patients with headache and a negative CT scan can be difficult. The sensitivity of detecting acute SAH with third-generation scanners has been suggested to be $98 \%$ to $100 \%$ if performed during the first 6 hours after ictus. However, the American Heart Association iv currently recommends that an LP be performed to rule out SAH if head CT is negative or inconclusive. Migraine headache is in the differential diagnosis of subarachnoid hemorrhage. According to the International Headache Societyv , migraine headaches last between 4-72 hours. They are typically unilateral in location, pulsating in quality, and moderate to severe in intensity. Associated symptoms can include nausea, vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia.

This particular OSCE requires examinees to systematically evaluate a patient presenting with the worst headache of her life.

Justification and Development:

Prior to the development of Parts $1 ^ { \mathsf { v i } }$ and $2 ^ { \mathsf { v i i } }$ in this series, we conducted a literature search on MededPORTAL using the term “headache.” There were no published headache OSCEs involving young women of child-bearing age. We needed an assessment that would require students to consider primary headache disorders, as well as life- or vision-threatening secondary headache disorders. We developed evaluation tools, in which a 22-year old obese female presents with headache. Her young age of onset brings into question the common primary headache disorders, including migraine and tension headache. Her obesity and fertility bring into question vision-threatening and life-threatening secondary headache disorders such as pseudotumor cerebri (idiopathic intracranial hypertension) and venous sinus thrombosis. In some of the scenarios, she also has signs and symptoms of potentially life-threatening neurological conditions, such as increased intracranial pressure and subarachnoid hemorrhage. Our OSCEs require the examinee to take a detailed headache history, request and interpret a head CT and lumbar puncture, and defend the final diagnosis. The diagnoses for Parts 1, 2 and 3 are subarachnoid hemorrhage, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor), and migraine, respectively.

We have been using Parts 1, 2 and 3 for summative purposes in our neurology clerkship. The OSCE score counts as $5 \%$ of the students’ final grade. We decided to create the third version of the headache OSCE, because we heard that students talked with each other about the diagnoses. The structure of the OSCEs is identical. In each case, the patient has enough red flags to warrant both a brain CT and a lumbar puncture.

We implemented this OSCE as part of the end of third year promotional OSCEs. The students had already been exposed to a headache OSCE during their neurology clerkship.

# Length of session

15 minutes for the patient encounter

5 additional minutes for immediate feedback would be ideal. Scoring may have to be done at the end of the day.

# Faculty preparation needs

Clinical faculty should be comfortable with history and physical examination findings of headache patients. The case is very straightforward. Nevertheless, faculty should plan to spend an hour reviewing the resource files.
