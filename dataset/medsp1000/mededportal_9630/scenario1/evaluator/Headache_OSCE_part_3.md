Headache OSCE: A 22-year old woman presents to the emergency department with a severe headache—Part 3

Headache OSCE Instructor Packet

1. You will assess the examinee’s ability to take a focused, but complete headache history from the patient.   
2. You will read aloud the physical examination findings, then ask the examinee to generate an initial differential diagnosis.   
3. You will read aloud the results of basic labs and ask the examinee what diagnostic test he or she would order first.   
4. Regardless of whether non-contrast head CT was ordered, you will pull the head CT from the sheet protector, hand it to the examinee, and ask for an interpretation.   
5. Regardless of whether the CT was interpreted correctly, you will read aloud the correct interpretation.   
6. You will then ask the examinee if any further diagnostic test is necessary.   
7. Regardless of whether a lumbar puncture is ordered, you will remove the results from the sheet protector, hand it to the examinee, and ask for an interpretation.   
8. You will ask the examinee to correctly diagnose and defend this patient’s headache, by listing the pertinent positives.   
9. The OSCE will last approximately 15 minutes.   
10. Ideally, 5 additional minutes will be available for immediate feedback. The type of feedback given will depend on the examinee’s performance. Prescribe any necessary remediation plans, based on the type of error made by the examinee.

It is very important to read the instructions, verbatim.

Use the checklists to indicate examinee responses. There will not be time to complete all of the rating scales until the OSCE is over.

Read this, verbatim: “You have 6 minutes to take a complete, but focused history on this patient with headaches. You may take notes. You will be given a warning when 2 minutes remain.”

# Overall grading and remediation plans for the OSCE:

Criteria for failure of the OSCE:

If the examinee missed the final diagnosis at the end of the session, the examinee fails the OSCE and needs remediation. The recommended remediation plan is to write an essay comparing and contrasting the erroneous diagnosis with migraine. Upon successful remediation, the examinee will receive a score of $7 5 \%$ . If the examinee failed, turn to the feedback section on page 12.

# Criteria for needs remediation of the OSCE:

1. Failure to order brain imaging as the first diagnostic test.   
2. Failure to order a lumbar puncture.   
3. Failure to order a CT prior to the lumbar puncture.

Recommended remediation plans are as follows: write a one page paper on the indications for head CT in a patient with headaches; or write a one-page paper on indications for and contraindications and complications of lumbar puncture. The grade assigned after remediation is $80 \%$ . If the examinee needs remediation, turn to the feedback section on page 12.

# Criteria for passing the OSCE:

1. Examinee must state the correct diagnosis at the end of the OSCE.   
2. Examinee must order brain imaging as the first diagnostic test.   
3. Examinee must order a lumbar puncture after the brain imaging.

Calculating the final grade takes time. You may wish to simply tell the examinee that he or she passed and tally the points later. Then, move forward to the feedback section on this page.

# Structured feedback:

For examinees passing or needing remediation:

Feedback is simple in the majority of examinees that either pass or need remediation of the OSCE. However, there is always room for improvement and topics for discussion. Inform the examinee that he or she arrived at the correct diagnosis and see below for potential talking points.

# For examinees failing:

If the examinee failed to arrive at the correct diagnosis at the end of the session, inform him or her of this. You may tell the examinee the correct diagnosis and ask why an alternative diagnosis was chosen. The examinee may have listed migraine as the most likely diagnosis in the initial differential diagnosis. In that case ask, “What made you change your mind?” Once you understand the examinee’s thought process, you may proceed with the feedback suggestions below.

# Talking points:

Medical history: An example of how you may give feedback follows. “You elicited many/most of the items on the checklist. In addition to those items, we would like you to ask about the following clinical features.”

Initial differential diagnosis: We consider the diagnoses listed in bold to be more likely in a patient of this age and gender. An example of how to give feedback for this portion follows: “You listed subarachnoid hemorrhage first and also included migraine. That is excellent. What made you also consider temporal arteritis in this patient?” Take the opportunity to go over the clinical features of temporal arteritis. (Please see the appendix for a list of different types of headaches and the pertinent positives and negatives.)

First diagnostic test: If the first diagnostic test ordered was not brain imaging, ask the examinee to list the indications for brain imaging and red flags for patients presenting with headache.

Head CT interpretation: If the head CT was not interpreted as normal, review the image with the examinee.

Second diagnostic test: If a lumbar puncture was ordered before the head CT, ask the examinee to list the complications of lumbar puncture, and how to avoid them.

Lumbar puncture interpretation: If the lumbar puncture was not interpreted correctly, review this with the examinee.

Defense of the diagnosis: If the examinee missed any of the items listed, review this.
