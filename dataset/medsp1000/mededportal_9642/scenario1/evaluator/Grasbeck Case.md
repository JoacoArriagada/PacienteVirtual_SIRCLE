# Clinical case presentation

# Megaloblastic Anemia + E. coli-induced diarrhea = trouble

Abstract: This is an active learning activity presented to 2nd semester medical students in our Progressive Academic Education (PAcE) program, a kind of scholars program with 150 students per class. It presents a pediatric patient who presents with an acute infection, but has an underlying chronic disease, megaloblastic anemia caused by vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ uptake deficiency (Imerslund-Gräsbeck-syndrome [1], OMIM #261100). The task of the students is to order the required tests and come to the correct diagnosis. They have a restricted budget which does not allow them to order all tests available. This requires students to

• know how relevant clinical tests work and how to interpret the results   
• integrate clinical and basic sciences (here: vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ )   
• develop analytical thinking and problem solving skills   
• work effectively in a group   
• use growth charts

This exercise requires students to work at level 5 (synthesis) and 6 (evaluation) of Bloom’s taxonomy [3]. The exercise is complemented by NBMEstyle [5] multiple choice questions on the basic science related to this case. This material has been presented in preliminary form [2, 4].

# 1.2. Patient presentation

Jane Gutterand, a 5-year-old Caucasian girl, is brought to the campus clinic three days before the first school day of the on-campus day school. For the past 3 days she has been affected by non-bloody, watery diarrhea with the passage of 4-6 stools a day. She complained also of abdominal cramps, mild fever, and feeling faint upon standing. She is accompanied by her mother, who is an incoming first term student. Jane’s family came from Pennsylvania 2 weeks ago.

Prior to the families move to the University, Jane’s grandmother cared for her at home. Jane had neither attended day care nor preschool. Since her mother did not have health insurance, Jane had not received regular medical care other than routine vaccinations. Her mother says that Jane had frequent respiratory infections but that this was probably due to the older home in which they lived. In other respects her mother feels that Jane has been doing well. Her mother is concerned that if Jane does not recover soon, she won’t be able to attend the first day of school, which she is very excited about.

# 3. Discussion of the case

This pediatric patient presents with a chief complaint of traveler’s diarrhea. Signs of moderate dehydration and weakness are therefore to be expected, namely orthostatic changes of pulse and blood pressure. However, the pale mucous membranes point to some form of anemia. There are also signs of slight neurological problems. A CBC quantifies the anemia and identifies the type, here: megaloblastic. This can be caused by a deficiency of either cobalamin (vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ ) or folate (vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 9 }$ ). Chemical analysis of blood plasma reveals a deficiency of cobalamin, whilst [folate] is normal. Unless the patient has a vegan diet and hence a nutritional deficit, this means that uptake of cobalamin must be defective. In a child the most likely cause for this is genetic, hence a Schilling-test is appropriate.

In an elderly patient the cause is usually an auto-immune gastritis, which interferes with intrinsic factor production (pernicious anemia). Thus, in this patient group a test for auto-immune antibodies would be more appropriate (faster, cheaper, no radio-nucleotides required). This aspect can be used for a discussion of diseases in pediatric vs geriatric patients.

Since this patient cannot absorb cobalamin even if intrinsic factor (from hog stomach or genetically engineered) is also given, the problem is with the intestinal cobalamin receptor and the proper diagnosis is ImerslundGräsbeck-syndrome. Since in this disease the utilization of cobalamin in the body is normal, management of the patients is simply by monthly i.m. injection of cobalamin. Unlike most other water-soluble vitamins cobalamin is stored in the liver, hence daily uptake is not required.

Methylmalonic aciduria cbl-type would be caused by the inability to transport cobalamin into mitochondria or to convert dietary cyano- or methylcobalamin into the adenosyl-cobalamin used as enzyme cofactor. In this type of patients, the Schilling-test would be negative (i.e., normal), and they would have normal plasma [cobalamin]. However, the activity of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase would be low. There is no need to test for this disease in our patient, as methylmalonic aciduria presents in early infancy with lethargy, vomiting, poor feeding, developmental retardation, metabolic acidosis, methylmalonic aciduria, long chain ketonuria and intermittent hyperglycinemia. Seizures and other neurological signs may also be present. A patient with this disease would require management in a tertiary care facility.

Common cause of travelers diarrhea are enterotoxic $E$ . coli (ETEC) from food or water that has been exposed to human sewage. This can be avoided by using boiled, filtered or bottled water. Food should be fried, cooked, or pealed.

Causes of megaloblastic anemia The results of laboratory tests suggest that impaired absorption of the cobalamin-intrinsic factor complex in the terminal ileum is an underlying pathology in this case. For this specific patient what other possible cause most likely might have produced the same pathology (i.e., megaloblastic anemia)?

A Lack of secretion of intrinsic factor B Lack of intrinsic factor in the diet \* Lack of cobalamin in the diet D Proteolytic destruction of $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ E Proteolytic destruction of intrinsic factor

Lack of cobalamin (vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ ) in the diet would cause very similar clinical problems – megaloblastic anemia – as an uptake deficiency. In either case, cobalamin is not available in the tissues that need it, most notably the bone marrow and the nervous system. Dietary cobalamin deficiency is seen in vegans, who do not eat any meat, eggs or milk products. In theory, vegans could get cobalamin from yeast, however, this is difficult in practice and supplementation with cobalamin is recommended for this group.

Intrinsic factor is a soluble glycoprotein produced in the parietal cells of the stomach. It binds to cobalamin in the chyme and allows the efficient uptake of this vitamin in the small intestine. It is not a normal part of the diet, but in patients lacking intrinsic factor it can be given orally to improve cobalamin uptake. IF can be isolated from hog stomach or produced by genetically engineered microorganisms. Lack of intrinsic factor is not common in pediatric patients, but seen in the elderly as result of an autoimmune gastritis. These patients (if untreated) wither away as consequence of anemia that worsens over time (“pernicious anemia”).

As cobalamin is not a protein, it is not subject to proteolysis. IF has evolved to be resistant to proteolysis by digestive proteases. In the stomach, another glycoprotein (haptocorrin) is produced and binds to intrinsic factor. Haptocorrin is proteolytically degraded in the duodenum, which is required for efficient uptake of cobalamin/IF-complex in the ileum.

Reactions requiring $\mathsf { B } _ { 1 2 }$ The megaloblastic anemia caused by Vit. $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ deficiency can be “cured” by high doses of folate. However, this does not prevent the neurological problems due to white matter degeneration. The pathomechanism of the latter involves which of the following compounds?

A) 5-methyl tetrahydrofolate B) 5,10-methylene tetrahydrofolate C) homocysteine D) S-adenosyl methionine \*) methylmalonyl-CoA

In the human body, two enzymes are known to use vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ as cofactor: methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. The former is required to regenerate 5-methyl-THF (folate trap) to THF, and therefore make folate available to carry C1-bodies from amino acid degradation to nucleotide synthesis. Inability to perform this reaction interferes with cell division and hence hematopoiesis. Excess folate can mask this problem.

Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase converts methylmalonyl-CoA (from odd-chain and branched chain fatty acid, and amino acid degradation) into succinylCoA. Failure of this enzyme results in accumulation of methylmalonyl-CoA, which is then used as substrate for fatty acid synthesis instead of malonylCoA. The resulting branched-chain fatty acids are build into phospholipids, and interfere with their tight packing in membranes. This results in demyelinization in the CNS, which is not prevented by folate.

Outcome of an experiment Imagine a patient with folate deficiency were given an injection of $[ ^ { 3 } \mathrm { H } ]$ -thymidine, and a bone marrow biopsy were taken several hours later. From this biopsy the DNA is isolated, and the radioactivity determined. Compared to the DNA of a normal control person treated in the same way, the radioactivity in the patient’s DNA would be

\*) higher B) lower C) same

Absence of folate interferes with nucleotide biosynthesis and hence cell division. Most affected are tissues with high mitotic rate, in particular the bone marrow. If nucleotides, in particular thymidine, are supplied, they will be used for DNA synthesis. In a person with normal folate supply, endogenous thymidine will dilute the radioactive nucleotides, and less radioactivity will be build into the DNA. In addition, in the patient the rate of cell division, and hence DNA production, may be higher once enough nucleotides are available.

Cells involved in $\mathsf { B } _ { 1 2 }$ uptake The complex of vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ and intrinsic factor is mainly absorbed at which of the following organs?

![](images/7a5db72071b5a39c4975441426cca48983d5b4d7c74d0c9fd518eb851883ecba.jpg)

The correct answer is D (Ileum). The finger-like villi in the mucosa and the Peyer’s patches in the submucosa are characteristic features of the ileum. Under normal conditions, the complex of vitamin $\mathrm { B } _ { 1 2 }$ and intrinsic factor is mainly absorbed in the terminal ileum.

A is not correct. The organ shown in A is the liver. A classic liver lobule and a portal area are obvious.

B is not correct. The organ shown in B is the duodenum. The villi in the mucosa, and the Brunner’s glands in the submucosa are characteristic features of the duodenum.

C is not correct. The organ shown in C is the pancreas. The darkly staining exocrine portion and the pale-staining oval shaped endocrine portion (the islets of Langerhans) of the pancreas can be easily identified here.

E is not correct. The organ shown in E is the appendix. The tubular structure with a narrow lumen, the distinctive colon-like histology, and the large lymphoid nodules in the lamina propria and the submucosa are characteristic features of the appendix.
