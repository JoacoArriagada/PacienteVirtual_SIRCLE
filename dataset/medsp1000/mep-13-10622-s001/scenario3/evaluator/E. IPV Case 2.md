# Part 1

A 30-60 year old patient presents to the orthopedist for follow-up of a right elbow fracture and is now found to have bruising over the left clavicle.

The patient lives with his/her spouse who controls all of the finances, shopping, and social interactions. The patient is recovering from a bad ankle sprain and had been using crutches up until 2 weeks prior. The patient fell down at home a few weeks ago and broke his/her arm after his/her spouse took away the crutches because s/he wanted the patient to “stop acting like such an invalid.” The broken arm required an overnight stay in the hospital because of pain and difficulty getting around. The bill was enormous, and the spouse was very upset that s/he had to tap into savings to pay it. S/he yelled at the patient and told the patient that s/he “shouldn’t be so stupid and clumsy.” Since returning from the hospital, the patient has been even more reliant than usual on his/her spouse. The spouse is still angry and is punishing the patient by refusing to buy the food s/he likes. Yesterday, the spouse shoved the patient because s/he “wasn’t moving fast enough,” which resulted in the patient falling again, this time bruising the left collar bone badly. The patient notes, “It was really just an accident,” because his/her spouse “has a lot on their plate” and was in a rush.

HITS: Physically Hurt (rarely), Insult (frequently), Threaten with physical harm (never), Scream or curse (frequently)

Change importance scale: 2 out of 10
Change confidence scale: 4 out of 10

The patient does not think that anything needs to change right now. S/he views these injuries simply as accidents that happened because his/her spouse is under a lot of stress. However, the patient does feel that if s/he wanted to, s/he could make changes.

# Feedback and discussion

This patient exhibited denial that a problem exists and minimized the injuries. Here are some tips to consider when debriefing this case:

Remember that not all denial is absolutely separated from reality – some patients will know that they have a problem but may deny the impact it has on themselves, their families, their job, etc. Patients who typically deny that they have a problem or who deny the impact of this problem will often react with anger and outrage when questioned. Be sure to acknowledge this emotion and remember that it is not directed towards you as an individual, but rather at the situation and the context.

Remember that some patients who have learned to live daily in denial are often untouched by a rational argument or perspective.

As noted in the checklist, even when a patient does not want to accept that a problem exists, be sure to leave the door open and show support. You can consider saying “Okay, but before we move on, I just want to make sure you understand that I am concerned about your situation and am available to help you figure out some options, if and when you’re ready. In the meantime, I have some resource materials that I’d like to share with you.”

Try to explore the underlying motivational factors of your patient without being accusatory. Consider saying “I am hearing from you that you don’t think the incident that happened last week was a big deal because you were able to recover without your family or co-workers knowing. I can appreciate how this might influence how you view the situation. How might you view this situation if a family member or co-worker came to you with the same concerns?”

Remember to work together with your patient to find what works for them. It is easy to close yourself off when someone denies they have a problem. Avoid the mentality of “they don’t want help”.
