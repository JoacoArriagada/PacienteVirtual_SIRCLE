**Psychiatry Objective Structured Teaching Encounter (OSTE)**

**Literature/Supporting Evidence:**

Most faculty members aspire to excellence in teaching, but they do so in an environment that pushes them to address competing concerns. Offering professional development opportunities that fit within the workplace environment, take little time, and build upon faculty's existing knowledge that will assist in enhancing faculty success with students in busy and complex clinical environments1.

There is a call out to expand and diversify faculty development to include mechanisms that provide specific and direct feedback to educators on their performance. The objective structured teaching exercise (OSTE) is a faculty development tool that may meet this challenge. The OSTE consists of a simulated teaching scenario involving a standardized learner with objective and immediate feedback given to the teacher, and includes a pre-determined behaviorally based scale or checklist to assess teaching performance2. Standardized students used as part of an OSTE focused on clinical teacher skill development provide a high fidelity, low risk, simulated environment in which faculty can reflect on and experiment with new teaching behaviors. Such encounters can enhance the effectiveness and impact of faculty development programs to improve clinical teaching skills3.

In the early literature on OSTEs Prislin et al. reports an OSTE that reflects realistic teaching situations can be successfully developed. Objective structured evaluation can be successfully applied to assessing faculty teaching performance. However, it may be no more discriminating than are student evaluations. An OSTE's utility may be greatest in guiding faculty development initiatives4.

Objective structured teaching evaluations (OSTEs) have been utilized to evaluate educational curricula and for resident and faculty development. In a study by Appelle et al. that used an OSTE as part of a faculty development program, there was statistically significant improvements self-assessed teaching skills by faculty participants. Faculty reported the OSTE was a rewarding experience and improved their teaching abilities5. A study by Ottolini et al. studied a faculty development program with hospitalists to address their teaching skills during Family Centered Rounds. Their results supported OSTEs, as a faculty development strategy, were an effective strategy for improving faculty teaching behavior6.

Designing and implementing an OSTE for psychiatry preceptor development, specific to their clinical teaching skills with early learners in psychiatric care settings, is additive to the current literature on OSTEs as a faculty development strategy.

**Resource Description:**

In this OSTE, faculty development was provided to psychiatry preceptors. Their role was to precept 2-3 MS 2 medical students during a psychiatric patient encounter. The goal was to conduct a complete history and a mental status exam on the patient. Using a standardized patient in the role of a psychiatric patient and MS 4 students in the role of MS 2 students, a simulated interaction was conducted in small group. This role play mimicked the preceptor, student and patient encounter. The focus was on patient interviewing skills in addition to keeping all 3 students, who were observers, engaged in the interaction and learning. This was followed by a small group discussion to focus on teachable moments.

Attachments provide a blueprint of the session with necessary supporting resources to conduct the session, which is transferrable to other disciplines with modification in the standardized patient script.

**Goals:** Upon completion of the OSTE session preceptors will be able to:

1. Conduct and debrief 3 student-psych patient interviews in one session.
2. Create a learning environment that engages both the student interviewing as well as the students observing the psychiatric interview.
3. Facilitate students learning the diagnostic interview with a focus on the History of Present Illness (HPI) and the Mental Status Exam (MSE).

**Learning Objectives:** Upon completion of the OSTE session, preceptors will be able to:

1. Observe a student conduct a psychiatric interview with a standardized patient
2. Observe a preceptor debrief (content and process) of the psychiatric interview with a group of students
3. Identify small group facilitation strategies to create a learning environment that is inclusive of all students

**Uniqueness:**

The Initial Clinical Experience (ICE) program provides medical students early exposure to various clinical disciplines, including psychiatry. The psychiatry rotation in MS2 year occurs at our inpatient psychiatric hospital and each preceptor is paired with 2-3 students for an afternoon session over 4 hours/week for 6 weeks. The students practice the psychiatric interview and mental status exam with a patient admitted to an inpatient unit. The patient is pre-selected by the preceptor. This ICE experience requires the psychiatry preceptors to be able to facilitate a feedback session with the student who performed the interview and also engage the student observers so they can learn from their peers.

The OSTE faculty development session demonstrated the skills required to engage 3 students in a group interview session provide feedback to a student in front of peers and engage other students in their learning from observation and peer feedback. This interactive design permitted the faculty preceptors to not only observe, but practice their role as well with a standardized student and patient. The large group debrief with all preceptors who were observing highlighted teachable moments and constructive feedback, which the preceptors applied to their actual session with their own students and real patients. OSTEs allow an enactment of an objective structured teaching encounter and creates a safe space for practice and ultimately builds preceptor skills and confidence to assure their student interaction is a valuable learning experience.

In reviewing the publication list for OSTEs, there is no publication specific to the psychiatric interview and mental status exam that focuses on psychiatrists who interact with medical students, especially in the setting of an early clinical exposure.

**Intended Audience:**

Psychiatry preceptors that will be assigned MS 2 students for an initial clinical experience in psychiatry

**Setting:**

Small group room with optional AV requirement, if you intend to record the OSTE to review at a later time

**Pre-Work:**

None for the participants

**Program Requirements:**

1. OSTE Faculty (Director of the Initial Clinical Experience, the Associate Dean of Educational Skills Development, and the Psychiatry site director) to lead the preparation of the OSTE and debrief the discussion
2. Three MS 3 or MS 4 students to role play an MS 2 student or alternatively, standardized learners can be used
3. Standardized patient (SP) to role play a psychiatric patient based on the OSTE SP Training Notes
4. Psychiatry preceptor to role play a preceptor
5. Important Documents to provide the preceptors attending the OSTE:

* Goals and Learning Objectives of the OSTE with a sample agenda
* Psychiatric Interview checklist and Mental Status Exam checklist – Observer Version Handout (2)
* OSTE Post-Evaluation

**Implementation Plan:**

OSTE faculty prepare a standardized case to be used to train the standardized patient. The clinical skills center trains the standardized patient using the case. The students or standardized learners are instructed by the OSTE faculty to do a full psychiatric history on the standardized patient. The standardized case is not shared with the students to simulate a real student-patient encounter.

A psychiatry preceptor volunteers from the audience and is paired with a group of 3 students and a standardized patient. One student interviews the SP while the other two students observe. The preceptor is given 2 handouts for observation: 1) psychiatry interview checklist and 2) mental status exam checklist. The preceptors in the audience are also given the same document to follow along. These checklists are a guide for the preceptor to debrief areas of constructive feedback. Post-interview, the psychiatry preceptor who volunteered for the role play conducts a debrief with the students on the psychiatric interview and mental status exam. As this is an OSTE with a psychiatric patient, the SP is not part of the debrief.

**Post-OSTE Discussion (See Psychiatry OSTE Debrief Questions):**

**OSTE Faculty conducts a debrief with the small group** (students and preceptor involved in the role play) post interview

* Debrief content and process of teaching
  + Content – How to conduct a diagnostic interview inclusive of the HPI and MSE
  + Process - How to include all students observing interview and student interviewer in debrief post interview (SP is not debriefed)

**OSTE Faculty conducts a debrief with the large group** (audience of preceptors)

* Debrief content and process of teaching
  + Content – How to give feedback to a student on their ability to conduct a diagnostic interview, inclusive of the HPI and MSE
  + Process - How to include all students observing interview in debrief post interview to assure learning among all

**Debrief with medical students that volunteered or alternatively the standardized learners, after the OSTE:**

* This debrief can be in person or via email:
  + *Describe the experience of taking on the role of a standardized student for the purpose of teaching faculty a skill set.*
    - *Do you feel you learned anything from the experience; if yes, what?*
  + *Any suggestions for improvement?*

**OSTE Post-Evaluation:**

* **Preceptor Response in the Post-Evaluation:**
  + Please see attached for the complete Psychiatry OSTE Survey Report
  + Summary of Report:
    - Our survey queried the preceptors that participated in the OSTE. We asked a total of nine questions. Three questions pertained to if the goals were successfully met. Three questions pertained to if the learning objectives were successfully met. The final three questions asked if they felt better prepared to participate in psychiatry ICE by participating in this OSTE. For all nine questions, the preceptors either agreed or strongly agreed. This was evidence to us that there was value to this activity.
    - Reported mean value for each question ranged from 3.67-3.89 out of 4.0.

**Feedback from MS 2 Students on Psychiatry Initial Clinical Experience:**

This OSTE faculty development session is preparatory for the preceptor’s role in the psychiatry initial clinical experience. We used the evaluation by the MS 2 students, who completed a required initial clinical experience in psychiatry, as evidence that this faculty development was effective.

The psychiatry Initial Clinical Experience data from 2014-15 (N=77) had 12 evaluation questions with a mean score of 3.9 out of 4.0. The 1 question which directly related to the faculty development OSTE scored 4.0 out of 4.0. The question was as follows:

“Directly observes my clinical skills when interacting with patients”

A student comment directly from the evaluation:

“I really liked the structure of psych ICE and how we were assigned in a group. I think having the physicians choose a couple of patients with pathologies for us to interview was extremely valuable and made the experience more memorable of a learning experience. I also think it was extremely helpful to get feedback from both the preceptor and my peers as well as observe my peers interview, and would encourage a similar method to be implemented within peds or medicine ICE.”

**Reflective Critique:**

To engage preceptors who are required to participate as preceptors in an early clinical experience, it is important to consider faculty development strategies beyond introductory letters, how-to manuals, videos and power points. The OSTE framework permits enactment of the desired education strategy and reinforces what we expect clinical teachers as well as expectations from students during a teaching encounter. It also unveils gaps that can be addressed in future faculty developments to assure learning for our students is consistent and on target. We learned that MS 4's were not readily available to participate. We were only able to have 1 student act the role of an MS 2. The 2 observing students were role played by preceptors, which is difficult. The MS 4's had difficulty regressing to the MS 2 level even for role-playing the interview.

It is not ideal for preceptors to role play the student learners during the OSTE. We learned that using senior students would provide a more authentic experience. Therefore, it is essential to recruit senior medical students early to assure students will be available to “act” during the OSTE. With this pre-planning, there will be time to train the senior students for their role as MS 2’s students during the activity.

The resources required to implement an OSTE faculty development must be considered early to assure they are available to conduct a successful session. Support from the clerkship director is essential for logistics (e.g.: invites, required attendance, and release from clinical activity for precepting). The OSTE has to be designed each to year to accommodate the needs of returning faculty and new faculty to ensure engagement of all participating. Student feedback is essential to assure that the proposed strategy in the faculty development is implemented during ICE. An area of refinement would be to have more standardized students, which is currently a limiting factor to allow more preceptors to actually practice and not only observe a peer.

Our early clinical experience includes the following disciplines internal medicine/family medicine, pediatrics, ob-gyn and surgery. Due to the success of OSTEs in psychiatry, we would like to expand this format to the other disciplines in our early clinical experience. In addition, we can encourage psychiatry faculty leadership to share this faculty development method at their regional and national education meetings with colleagues who plan education for medical students. This interactive faculty development can support a clinical learning environment that meets the needs of our students and our SOM education principles.
