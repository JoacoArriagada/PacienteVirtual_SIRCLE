| **Brief narrative description of case** | | Ashley is a 22-year-old G1 female at approximately 38 weeks gestation by last menstrual period who presents with rupture of membranes; cursory investigation by the obstetrician on call finds breech presentation without active labor, reassuring fetal heart tones, and glucose of 442. The obstetrician pushes for urgent cesarean section for breech presentation. After additional studies are made available, the patient’s condition of diabetic ketoacidosis becomes apparent. The learner requests that the non-emergent cesarean section be delayed for medical optimization, at which point the obstetrician requests help with medical management of diabetic ketoacidosis. However, during this discussion, the patient’s nurse informs the team of severe fetal bradycardia, after which the cesarean section is then deemed an emergency. The learner’s anesthesia plan is discussed (general anesthesia, rapid sequence induction, continued fluid resuscitation and insulin and electrolyte management), and the cesarean section proceeds uneventfully until the infant is delivered. The infant is found to be meconium-stained and have a low Apgar score; the learner is then asked to resuscitate the infant, which he or she does after quickly creating a plan to watch over the anesthetized mother. Despite appropriate airway management, the infant’s Apgar scores remain low. **Ideal Scenario Flow**

*The trainee enters the room and is given the stem of the case, learning that the patient is at term with her first pregnancy. She has had minimal medical care to this point. She appears lethargic. She is found to have positive rupture of membranes and breech presentation without evidence of active labor. Fetal heart tones are found to be reassuring. The trainee asks for laboratory studies and subsequently diagnoses diabetic ketoacidosis. The obstetrician pushes the learner to go straight to cesarean section, but the learner recognizes that this critically ill patient is not medically optimized for non-emergent surgery and suggests medical management before surgery. The obstetrics team requests guidance with treatment of diabetic ketoacidosis, which prompts the learner to recommend fluid resuscitation with isotonic fluids, potassium replacement for potassium below 5.5, insulin infusion (to be held for potassium below 3.3), and addition of a dextrose source when the blood glucose level falls below 250.*

*Following initial management, the nurse returns four hours later with scheduled laboratory studies that demonstrate improved glucose and normal electrolytes but persistent anion gap. The surgical team again asks if surgery is acceptable at this time, to which the trainee responds, “Resuscitation is not complete until the anion gap closes; the patient is not medically optimized for surgery.” However, as this discussion is occurring, the patient’s nurse rushes in to inform the team that fetal heart tones are demonstrating frequent late decelerations. The obstetrician informs the trainee that the procedure is now emergent.*

*Upon presentation to the operating room, the trainee selects a rapid-sequence style induction of general anesthesia. Any induction consistent with a rapid sequence is acceptable (etomidate vs propofol with succinylcholine). Following securement of the endotracheal tube, the learner is now informed that the child has been delivered but is meconium-stained and appears depressed with an initial Apgar score of 3.*

*After confirming that the mother is stable and having a nurse in the room monitor the mother’s vital signs, the learner assists with resuscitation of the infant. The airway is cleared and secured with minimal stimulation using the ‘suction, intubate, suction, ventilate’ sequence; however, this does not improve the infant’s status. After investigating further and obtaining a point-of-care glucose on the infant, hypoglycemia is diagnosed, dextrose supplementation is provided, the neonate improves, and the case ends.*

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. *Failure to make the diagnosis of diabetic ketoacidosis: Some junior learners may have difficulty initially making the diagnosis of diabetic ketoacidosis. The BMP provides some clues (elevated glucose, significant anion gap), but some trainees need additional guidance to ask for confirmatory tests (B-hydroxybutyrate, urinalysis). As a result, diagnosis of DKA is specifically addressed in the debriefing documentation.*
2. *Failure to delay the case for medical optimization: A few trainees may feel uncomfortable ‘standing up’ to a surgical team member and insisting on medical optimization prior to non-emergent surgery. This is meant to be a key part of this case; by not holding surgery for medical optimization, the simulated patient becomes dangerously hypotensive intraoperatively due to hypovolemia. This branch point is designed to be a learning tool for trainees if encountered, as it is the responsibility of the anesthesia provider to care for the patient as a whole and weigh the risks and benefits of surgery to confirm that we “First: Do no harm.”*
3. *Failure to supplement potassium: A common error in treating diabetic ketoacidosis is to provide insulin and isotonic fluid without potassium replacement. Because patients in DKA are often total-body potassium depleted (even though they frequently present initially with hyperkalemia), providing insulin moves potassium into cells and drastically lowers the serum concentration. In addition, rehydration and correction of acidosis removes the driving force for potassium shift to the extracellular environment. If supplementation of potassium is not given, dangerous arrhythmias stemming from hypokalemia can result. If this occurs, it is necessary to hold insulin while hypokalemia is corrected. Only when the potassium is normalized can insulin infusion resume. This learning point is also addressed in the debriefing handout provided to learners following the simulation.*
4. *Failure to diagnose hypoglycemia in the neonate of an uncontrolled diabetic mother: One common metabolic derangement in infants of diabetic mothers (particularly those with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus) is hypoglycemia from upregulation of insulin production in utero. This is discovered with either laboratory studies or point-of-care glucose check and is supplemented with dextrose for treatment. This learning point has been addressed in the debriefing document accompanying this simulation.*
