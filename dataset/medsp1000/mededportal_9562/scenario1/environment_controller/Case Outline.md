1. **Title: Pediatric Airway Foreign Body Training Experience**

23. Environment:
24. Lab Set-up: Simulation begins in a simulated outpatient Otolaryngology clinic, where the participant has been consulted to evaluate a young child with recurrent cough and fever. In the clinic, the participant interviews the parent and examines the patient. Once suspicion of airway foreign body is high, the participant should suggest that the patient go to the emergency room for further evaluation. The participant should be prompted to state what they would want ordered for radiography and lab tests. They should also be prompted to explain what they would be looking for on radiography. Once the airway foreign body is diagnosed, the scenario moves to the Operating Room (OR) where the participant performs direct laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy and foreign body extraction.

28. Actors:
29. Roles:
    1. Child’s parent: This person answers the participant’s questions about history, signs, and symptoms.
    2. Anesthesia colleague: Discusses the plan for induction and anesthesia with the participant over the phone (real or simulated). This role can also be played by the same case instructor as the child’s parent once the participant volunteers that he would like to speak with the anesthesiologist on call.
    3. Operating room nurse: Is available to the participant once the simulation moves to the OR stage where removal of the foreign body can occur. This person helps the primary surgeon during the case.

34. Case Narrative:
36. HPI: A mother brings her 18 month old son to the outpatient Otolaryngology clinic for evaluation of a chronic cough and fever. There was no witnessed foreign body ingestion. There was a witnessed coughing/gagging episode 2-3 weeks prior. This information is not volunteered unless specifically sought by the learner.
37. Past Medical History: One prior episode of bronchitis
38. Meds and Allergies: No daily medications and no known medical allergies
39. Family/Social history: Lives with mother and father. Two cats at home. No tobacco users at home.

40. Scenario Conditions Initially:
41. History patient gives: Provided by parent in clinic
42. Patient’s initial exam: Patient appears healthy, with stable and normal vital signs, borderline tachypnea, decreased breath sounds on right.

43. Scenario branch points:
44. In the clinic:
    1. Move to the emergency department: Participant can decide that the patient’s condition warrants a trip to the emergency department, where the appropriate imaging can be ordered. Once the appropriate imaging is interpreted and an airway foreign body is diagnosed, the scenario can move to the operating room.
    2. Changes in patient’s condition: Optional, patient can develop post-obstructive pneumonia if the participant decides to observe the patient and follow up in several months (then the patient can present to the emergency department with pneumonia).
    3. Responses to treatments: Patient would minimally improve after bronchodilators but the overall course would be gradual decline into pneumonia, or recurrent pneumonia, over days to weeks without a trip to OR for removal of the foreign body.
45. In the OR:
    1. Changes in patient’s condition:
       1. The vocal folds should be topicalized with simulated lidocaine prior to manipulating the larynx. SimBaby can be made to go into laryngospasm whether or not the cords are topicalized prior to laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy.
       2. During bronchoscopy, desaturation can occur, requiring removal of the “guts” (the optical instrument) to allow better gas exchange.
    2. Responses to treatments:
       1. At our center, we typically perform pediatric bronchoscopy under spontaneous ventilation. Topical lidocaine typically prevents laryngospasm in these cases. Appropriate *initial* responses to laryngospasm include cessation of laryngeal stimulation, positive pressure ventilation, oxygenation, and topical lidocaine if this has not already been performed. If these initial maneuvers do not resolve laryngospasm, intravenous propofol or succinylcholine can be used to pharmacologically break the spasm. Succinylcholine will eliminate all spontaneous respiration and hence is used as a last resort at our center.
       2. If desaturation is not addressed either with removal of guts (or less optimally removal of bronchoscope with mask ventilation) then the patient will arrest secondary to hypoxia and bradycardia.
    3. An actual foreign body should be removed from the SimBaby. A small bit of paper was used successfully to simulate a foreign body in our scenario; however, a small toy, peanut, or other object may be used. During removal, “real” problems like dropping the foreign body in the trachea or oral cavity may occur. If in the trachea, it should be pushed back down into the previously occupied mainstem bronchus, the patient oxygenated, and removal re-attempted. If in the oral cavity, it should be retrieved with the help of a laryngoscope.

46. Instructor’s Notes:
47. Tips to keep scenario flowing: Give realistic responses to the participants based on their questions, ordered labs and radiography, and physical exam. If the participant wants to admit the patient to the hospital for observation, the patient should worsen and additional clues about foreign body can be provided as needed.
48. Tips to direct actors: The instructor can essentially play the parent and anesthesiologist roles, either in the first or third person.

49. Scenario programming:
50. Optimal management path: The simulator is initially programmed with obstruction of the right lung ventilation, which is able to be appreciated on physical exam by the presence of diminished breath sounds on the right. It should be noted that the decreased breath sounds can be difficult to decipher on auscultation. Additionally, two complications were programmed into our simulation in order to teach participating residents how to manage possible complications. First, upon initial attempt at airway instrumentation, one of the instructors triggered a lifelike laryngospasm of the simulator. The laryngospasm was resolved by the instructor’s manipulation of the simulator upon demonstration of appropriate management by the participating resident (return to bag mask ventilation, consider pharmacologic treatment as needed, topicalize the larynx with simulated lidocaine if not already done). When the bronchoscope was then advanced into the trachea, one of the instructors triggered an episode of desaturation. Again, after appropriate demonstration of management by the participant (removal of bronchoscope “guts”), the desaturation was resolved by the instructor’s manipulation of the simulator.
