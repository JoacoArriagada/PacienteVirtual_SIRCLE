# Instructor’s Guide Pediatric Airway Foreign Body Training Experience

# List of Resources

1. Case outline
2. Pre- and post-simulation self-evaluation
3. Post-simulation case evaluation

5. Pediatric airway foreign body instructional Powerpoint presentation

# Explanation of When to Use Resources

The following description details how the authors administered the airway foreign body training experience.

When participants arrive, they are asked to complete the pre-simulation self-evaluation, which provides information about their level of training and experience with bronchoscopy. The participant then completes their initial iteration of the simulation experience, which is considered the pre-test. During this and the following trials, the raters use the performance evaluation to score the participant. The participant then views the pediatric airway foreign body instructional Powerpoint. After the presentation, the participant completes additional iterations of the simulation until they receive scores of >= 4 (independent or independent and efficient) in all categories from all raters. Typically this is iteration 4 (three trials after the pre-test) for less experienced participants (junior residents) or iteration 3 (two trials after the pre-test) for more experienced participants (senior residents). Fellows and attending physicians would likely demonstrate competent, independent performance after just 1 or 2 trials.

Once the participant achieves “independent” performance on the simulation, he completes the post-simulation self-evaluation and the post-simulation case evaluation.

1. The case outline provides a thorough description of the case flow, participant roles, and potential pitfalls.

2. The pre-simulation questionnaire ensures that the participant’s training level and prior bronchoscopy experience is known to the raters. This informs medical education research using the simulator, and enables the raters to provide appropriate feedback to each participant.

3. The pre- and post-simulation self-evaluation asks the participants to self-assess their ability to manage a pediatric airway foreign body in a real patient. Changes between the pre- and postcase self-evaluations demonstrate the simulation’s impact on “self-efficacy.”

4. The post-simulation case evaluation allows participants to provide feedback on the training experience. Such information is important to allow continual quality improvement of the case over time.

5. The scorer’s performance evaluation includes eight categorical scores and an overall score for the participant. A separate evaluation is performed by each rater, for each participant, on each trial.

6. The instructional Powerpoint is a 15-minute long original, narrated multimedia presentation that describes the rationale and clinical approach to a child with a potential airway foreign body. Specifically it addresses patient presentation, history-taking, appropriate radiographic workup, and patient management in the Operating Room (OR).

# Conceptual Background

Pediatric airway foreign body is a relatively rare but potentially life-threatening event. A significant proportion of foreign body aspirations are unwitnessed, and there are often few early symptoms after the initial coughing/gagging episode. Hence, practitioners must have a high index of suspicion for this diagnosis in any child that presents after such an event. Initially undiagnosed airway foreign body can present later with new or atypical wheezing, cough, stridor, or pneumonia. The radiographic work-up is somewhat specialized, and rarely used elsewhere in medicine. Once diagnosed, safe removal of a pediatric airway foreign body requires effective communication and coordination between the surgeon, OR staff, and anesthesiologist. Assembly of the bronchoscope can be confusing given its many small, interlocking parts. At many institutions, the daytime OR staff assembles the bronchoscope for the surgeon and so it may have been some time since the surgeon, particularly a resident, has assembled the bronchoscope himself or herself. Laryngospasm and desaturation during foreign-body removal are potentially lethal complications if mismanaged. For all of these reasons, the authors felt that a pediatric airway foreign body simulation training experience would be beneficial.
