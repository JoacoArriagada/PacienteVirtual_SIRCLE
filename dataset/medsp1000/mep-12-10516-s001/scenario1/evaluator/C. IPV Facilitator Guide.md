**Pros and Cons to Mandatory Reporting of IPV**

Mandatory reporting pros/cons (does not include mandatory reporting if children are present and witness the abuse or if firearms are used). These two caveats would be subject to child abuse laws and mandatory reporting of violence inflicted by guns.

***Pros:***

* May facilitate the provision of assistance
* Establishes legal ramifications for the abuser
* Lifts the burden of reporting off the victim

**Cons:**

* May discourage victims from seeking care
* Violates patient autonomy and confidentiality
* May escalate violence by the batterer towards the victim
* Physicians may not comply with the law if the victim does not want them to report
* May result in stigma and possible marginalization from family and community
* *Material aspects of daily life are disrupted:*  Legal statuses, housing and economic resources (bank accounts, taxes), transportation, social group and organizational memberships, and routine activities of work, school, child rearing, and personal maintenance are shared or at least intertwined (legally for those who are married and/or share children).
* *Triggers the criminal and civil justice systems.* These systems are slow working, invasive, and have unpredictable outcomes. For some, involvement in these systems may pose risk to their employment or their partner’s employment (e.g., people with security clearance). Even with legal and confidentiality protections for victims, there are no guarantees that a victim will not be identified by the system as a perpetrator. People with previous criminal history, active probation or parole, substance abuse problems may also be hesitant to trigger these interventions. The use of community-based services may also be a problem for this group, who are often excluded from shelters or other services due to prior criminal convictions or identification as a possible IPV perpetrator. Further, communities with a history of marginalization involving criminal and civil justice systems may also not see these systems as a viable option for intervention. Many people do not perceive these systems as effective interventions and fear increasing their personal involvement as well as the involvement of their partners.

For people with shared children, custody and visitation issues may also play a role in whether a person chooses to seek formal help. Family law prioritizes family unification. This means, even where a history of violence between partners exists and couples separate, the requirement to co-parent may continue to exist. Some may be uncomfortable with the idea of providing the abuser access to the children without supervision and choose to stay in the relationship or avoid help seeking out of fear of being separated from the children—even temporarily.

Some of the reasons for not reporting are the same as those for why victims stay with an abuser. See this website for a list of reasons why IPV victims do not leave their abuser, many of these also explain why victims do not call the police: <http://www.domesticviolenceroundtable.org/abuse-victims-stay.html>
