**Appendix A: Teaching Scenario Instructors Guide**

**Case 2**

Rationale: It is not uncommon to have a student who is very excited to be in the ED and jumps in with both feet but whose clinical skills are lacking to be an effective caregiver in the environment.

Objective: Recognize the need to acknowledge the students interest and reframe their expectations of themselves so that they can get the most out of the clinical experiences.

Set-up: The student in this scenario has been on shift for a couple of hours, has picked up 6 patients already and is struggling to provide adequate care to the patients. The nurse on shift brings it to the simulated supervisor’s attention that several patients and family members are concerned about things the student has said or done. The student then presents several patients, both with inappropriate plans and no inkling on the student’s part that they are not on the right track.

The setting: The RN working the low acuity side pulls you aside and asks “Who’s that terrible medical student working today?” He goes on to say that two families have complained to him already in the first two hours about the medical student failing to discuss what was going on, and another patient was in tears because she believed the student had said that she was too fat and needed to lose weight to solve her knee pain. The nurse also is upset because the MS failed to throw away their exam gloves 3 times, leaving them on the supply cart in the room. You have just arrived to staff, and note that this student is already carrying six patients, most of which have been in the ED for over an hour.

The presentation: Hi, sorry I haven’t been able to get to you. I’m trying to get everything together for you. I’m really excited about going into EM and I’d love to talk to you about it today. Do you want to hear about my patients? My first one is a 5 week old baby with a fever of 101.7. He looks great and I’m going to send him home with acetaminophen for that and follow up with his pediatrician. I don’t know how to dose that in babies—can you help me with that? My second patient is a 45 year old lady with chest pain. I took a long social history on her because she smelled like alcohol and it turns out she smokes crack! Wow! I’ve never met a crack smoker before! I think that probably can explain her chest pain and I’m going to send her home, too, with some instructions to stop doing that. Should I take care of those two first and then get back to you?

Debriefing Notes: The overzealous and incompetent scenario is designed to encourage the simulated supervisor to acknowledge the student’s enthusiasm while reframing their expectations in such a way that they are able to facilitate appropriate care. The supervisor also has the opportunity to provide feedback to the learner on the nurse’s comments which should prompt some specific constructive points for the learner.

* The teaching resident should acknowledge that this student is excited about being in the ED but also should help the student understand that at their level of training it is important for them to be able to present the pertinent positives and negatives from their evaluation in order to justify their assessment and plan for the patient. One option for the staffing resident in this scenario is to teach by example, have the student come with you to the bedside of the patients to observe your focused history and physical then briefly discuss the rationale for your work up. This is efficient in terms of delivering patient care as well as role modeling the behavior you want to see from your learners.
* The teaching resident should recognize that there are other issues going on with the student’s patients that they haven’t brought to their attention and address the issues raised by nursing at some point in the encounter. This may occur either before or after dealing with the new patients, but should include the complaints from patients and leaving dirty exam gloves for others to clean up.
