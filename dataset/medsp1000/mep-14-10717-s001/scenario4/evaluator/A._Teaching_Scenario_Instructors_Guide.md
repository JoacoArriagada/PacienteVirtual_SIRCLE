**Appendix A: Teaching Scenario Instructors Guide**

**Case 4**

Rationale: This scenario is less commonly experienced but provides the resident with the opportunity to practice having difficult conversations with students about their performance. The dishonest scenario necessitates that the resident confronts the student about lying to them and gives them the opportunity to intervene early.

Objective: Recognize the need to provide information in an evaluation even if it is adequately dealt with real time is discussed as a way to evaluate for ongoing trends or issues with student performance.

Set-up: The student in this scenario picks up an older patient presenting with syncope who has a significant GI bleed. The student has taken a good amount of time before presenting the patient so the supervisor briefly looked at the patient and it is clear they have melena by the smell but they note that the patient is still fully clothed. While presenting the patient the resident is likely to ask about the findings on a rectal exam, which the student has not performed but in an effort to not look bad they reply that it was normal. The resident may challenge the student on that based on the fact that the patient remains in their clothes and if they do the student will admit to not performing the exam. If the resident doesn’t then the facilitator can intervene by interjecting and asking the resident when they are planning to do a rectal exam.

The setting: You are working an evening shift in August and your student picks up his first patient of the rotation. It is your first staffing shift of the year and you are freaked out. You see that he has picked up an 86-year-old syncope patient in room 5 on the low acuity side. He hasn’t staffed it for about 50 minutes, so you go in to make sure they are not dead before he presents. It is obvious from the door that this lady is bleeding—smells like melena, looks like a ghost. She is still fully clothed.

The presentation: I saw this patient in room 5. She comes in complaining of getting more and more tired for the last two weeks. She seems to not have as much energy to do regular activity every day. She hasn’t had any fever, no chest or abdominal pain. No nausea, vomiting, diarrhea. She has diabetes, HTN, and arthritis. She’s on metformin, HCTZ, and naproxen. Her exam is pretty normal. I think she might be hypothyroid, so I would send thyroid function tests. (Responds that rectal exam was normal when questioned.)

Debriefing Notes: This is a difficult scenario for the simulated supervisor, they need to call the student out about being dishonest, focusing on the need to provide accurate information to supervisors at all times to ensure effective communication. Another item for discussion during the debriefing should be what to put on the student’s formal evaluation. If the resident indicates that they would not mention the situation on the evaluation as they felt like they have adequately dealt with the issue, the need to determine if this was a one-time event or is a trend should be introduced. Describing the situation as it unfolded including the mitigating factors that the student identified and the ensuing discussion can be included in the summative feedback.

* The teaching resident often focuses on the patient’s critical status and that the medical student didn’t recognize a sick patient and miss the fact that the medical student has lied to them. They occasionally need to be prompted to ask specifically about the physical exam and specifically the rectal exam. One option is to have a nurse come to the resident asking about if they are going to do a rectal exam.
* If the student mentions they did the rectal exam and the resident takes the student to the bedside then have the patient mention that the nurse warned them that you would be coming to do a rectal exam.
* Once the teaching resident recognizes that the student was dishonest, they usually explain why honesty is important and do a good job calling out the student for being dishonest. The student should be prompted to ask the resident “Will you put this in my evaluation?” to prompt more of a discussion about the importance of escalating potential learner problems. If this doesn’t happen in the scenario it can be brought up in the debriefing.
* Many teaching residents will be uncomfortable formally discussing the learner’s dishonesty (e.g. on an evaluation form) if they feel like they have addressed the issue in person. Discussion should revolve around how it is important to recognize trends in behavior and that even if this is a one-time event, other teachers might have also had the same issue and also dismissed it as a one-time event.
* Formal feedback on evaluation forms doesn’t have to be punitive. It is acceptable to discuss the facts of the encounter but to also discuss mitigating factors. For example: “This learner stated that they had done a portion of the physical exam that they hadn’t done. This was their first patient of their first day and they were eager to make a good impression. I confronted them on their dishonesty and they were remorseful. I had no other issues with this learner the rest of the day.”
