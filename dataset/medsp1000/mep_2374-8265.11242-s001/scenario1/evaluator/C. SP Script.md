Appendix C: Standardized Patient Script

Name of Case: Shingles

Patient Name: Leslie Davis

Chief Complaint: “Rash”

Most likely Diagnosis and Differential with rationale from history and/or physical exam: This is a case of shingles (herpes zoster) from varicella zoster virus. In this encounter, the patient has already been diagnosed with shingles. The learner’s tasks for this encounter include discussing with the patient the learner’s treatment recommendations as well as providing patient education about the recommended treatment (rather than collecting a full history and physical exam for diagnostic reasoning).

Type and level of learner: Pre-clerkship (1st or 2nd-year) medical students

Case Objectives:

1. Identify the first-line antiviral medications for treating herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus, and cytomegalovirus infections.

2. Describe to a patient the mechanism of action of famciclovir/penciclovir, valacyclovir/acyclovir, and valganciclovir/ganciclovir.

3. Explain to a patient what an antiviral prodrug is.

4. Provide information to a patient about the adverse effects associated with acyclovir, valacyclovir, penciclovir, and famciclovir.

| MANAGEMENT OR DIAGNOSTIC PLAN | Overview: Valacyclovir or famciclovir should be recommended for treating this patient’s shingles. The student should explain to the patient:   * This medicine interferes with viral DNA replication; it is a DNA base analog (specifically, it is a guanosine analog). * A possible adverse effect from this medicine is kidney problems (for example, kidney stones or injury to the kidneys/kidney function). For this reason, the student should recommend to you to stay well hydrated while you’re taking this medication. * They recommend ordering a blood test called a basic metabolic panel (or simply a creatinine level, which is one of the tests included in basic metabolic panels) to measure your basic kidney function.   Detailed SP instructions:  *The student has been instructed to tell you about the treatment options for this type of infection, and to recommend the medication they think will work the best (which the student will choose; it will likely be either famciclovir or valacyclovir).*  ***Part 1: Questions about how this medication works:*** *Respond to the student by inquiring about how the medication works:*  “Ok, great. How does this medicine work, exactly?”  *The student should explain that the medicine interferes with viral DNA replication. Nod your head in response to their explanation (act as if you actually understand what they say here).*  *If the student mentions during this explanation that the medicine is a “DNA base analog,” follow up by asking,*  “Which DNA base?”  *The student should respond by telling you that it is a “guanosine analog”; it’s okay if they can’t remember—just continue the conversation.*  *If the medication that the student recommends for treating your rash is acyclovir (the pill form), ask the following:*  “Is there a better oral option than that one?”  *The student should then explain that famciclovir or valacyclovir are similar oral versions of acyclovir but are taken less frequently. Alternatively, the student may not know the answer to your question (and that’s okay), in which case, simply proceed with the conversation as follows.*  *If the student has not already addressed how this medication will impact your pain symptoms, ask the student a question about it, such as:*  *“Will this medication help with my pain?”*  *The student should explain that this medication is not a pain-killer type of medication, but that it should help by reducing the severity and duration of the pain as the patient’s infection heals.*  ***Part 2: Questions about this medication versus other ones you’ve heard of:*** Your next question should be:  “Earlier this year, my mother was hospitalized for viral encephalitis. I think she ended up getting IV acyclovir. How is that medicine different from the one you’re recommending?”  *The student’s response to this question will depend on which medication they are recommending for you.*  *Your next question should be:*  “When my son was a baby, he had a ‘blueberry-muffin rash’ from some virus that I can’t remember, and he received ganciclovir, I think. He got a lot better after that. Is that drug different than the one you’re recommending?”  *The student should then explain that ganciclovir works the same way as the medicine they’re recommending for you, but that it works better on the virus that causes the “blueberry rash” in young babies. Alternatively, the student may not know the answer to your question (and that’s okay), in which case, simply proceed with the conversation as follows.*  ***Part 3: Questions about medication side effects:*** *Next, ask the student,*  “What are the side effects of this medicine you’re recommending?”  *The student should tell you that this medicine can cause kidney problems (for example, kidney stones or injury to the kidneys/kidney function). For this reason, the student should recommend to you to stay well hydrated while you’re taking this medication. If the student doesn’t instruct you about this, prompt them by asking:*  “Is there anything I can do to prevent those problems?”  *The student may or may not know how to answer this question (that’s okay). Regardless of their response, proceed to your next question, if the student hasn’t brought this up already:*  “Are there any tests that I need to have done before I start taking this medicine?”  *The student should respond by saying, yes, they recommend ordering a blood test called a “basic metabolic panel” (or they might recommend obtaining a “creatinine level,” which is one of the tests included in basic metabolic panels; creatinine measures a patient’s basic kidney function).*  ***Part 4: Conclusion:*** *At this point in the conversation, express your consent to go along with the student’s recommendation of this medication; for example:*  “Okay, so it sounds like this medicine makes the most sense for me. I think that is all of the questions I have.”  *End the encounter by thanking the student for their help.* |

SP instructions for this encounter:

For this scenario, the student will not obtain a history from you about your illness. For the context of this encounter, both you and the student will already know your diagnosis (shingles) before the student enters your exam room.

The student’s tasks are to: 1) recommend to you a specific medication for treating your shingles; 2) explain to you how to take this medicine, possible side effects to anticipate, how the medicine works, etc.; and 3) answer your questions about this medicine.

The character you are playing has some background knowledge of science (for example, they have a basic understanding of DNA structure), but they’re not medically trained and are not familiar with most medical lingo. Unless indicated in the script, please prompt students for clarification on any medical jargon they mention during this encounter with you.
