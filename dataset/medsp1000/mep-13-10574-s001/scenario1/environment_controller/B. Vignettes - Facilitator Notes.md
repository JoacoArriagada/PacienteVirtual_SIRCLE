**CASE 1**

This case is based on a real patient, and I have recommended against screening this patient. He does not have a family history of prostate cancer. Additionally, his previous PSA tests have been < 2ng/mL.

However, after informed conversations, the patient has opted to continue to have a PSA test every 2-3 years, assuming he stays in excellent health.

1. “Family history“ – I note that a family history is probably common in the “PSA era” from the late 1990’s to the mid-2010’s. Many men have actually been overdiagnosed during this timeframe. I tell the students that I clarify the family history further by determining the number of affected first-degree relatives and by determining age of onset in affected family members.

2. “Symptoms” – This answer is invariably given, but I remind students that lower urinary tract symptoms are also seen in BPH – and may in fact be less common in prostate cancer. Furthermore, I note that if you are checking a PSA for symptoms, you are now engaging in ***case finding***, not ***screening***.

3. “His previous PSA values” – This response has merit as the degree and pace of PSA elevation can predict cancer to some degree. That said, I remind students that this patient is now nearly 70 years old, and that the question at hand is, “Should you continue screening him?” So instead of determining his risk of having prostate cancer, I am trying to determine his likelihood of deriving a benefit at his age and his risk of overdiagnosis.

4. His understanding of screening and his approach to an abnormal PSA – I am always pleased when students mention this point. I remind students that PSA screening is not just checking a blood test but is potentially the door to an involved diagnostic and therapeutic cascade.

5. His comfort with “watchful waiting” as a treatment strategy – This option is not explicitly covered in the screening readings, but many students are aware of the increasing role of expectant management in low grade, localized disease. Most groups have cited that they would like to know how this patient views treatment. I point out my own practice of asking men if they would agree to watchful waiting as this approach potentially shifts the balance of risks and harms of screening for and detecting prostate cancer.
