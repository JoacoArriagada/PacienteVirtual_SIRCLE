**Case details:**

Scenario A: discussing prognosis and exploring goals of care

**Patient/family background** (not available to learners but they should elicit during conversation)

**Patient/Chris**: Patient has been living with heart failure, high blood pressure and kidney problems for years. He/ She is aware of these diagnoses, so if the interviewer names them, patient is not surprised but would admit he/she prefers to ignore them. Although reluctant to admit it, patient’s breathing, energy and appetite has slowly worsened over the past few years, especially in the last several months. He/ She is in bed more often taking naps and eating less. It is getting hard to move around and that’s why there are skipped doses of medications, so that he/she doesn’t have to go to bathroom. Patient requires nasal oxygen at home continuously at 4 liters and has turned on emergency alarm button twice in the past 6 months after falling on the floor while walking.

Chris’s spouse passed away few years ago in an automobile accident. While there are a few close friends, others have died unfortunately. Chris misses spouse and friends terribly, values independence and doesn’t really want to leave home. Patient is a retired high school teacher and collects a small pension and Social Security. He/ She was raised Catholic but doesn’t go to church anymore. There are two children and 3 grandchildren; Chris worries about being a burden to your children, managing at home.

**Family/Child**: Very devoted and have watched Chris decline in recent months. In particular, he/she worries about the weight loss, and wonders if Chris would be better if he/she just ate more. The child also feels guilty that maybe he/she doesn’t do enough and should do more. They realize that Chris is never going to get much better but still believe he/she could improve with better diet and maybe some more help. While Chris doesn’t seem good, child believes he/she has a few years left, rather than months.

**Information shared with acting patients**

Your scenario should open with the following statements (feel free to ad lib):

**Patient**: I am feeling better doc, I think I am ready to go home. I just want to be home.

**Family**: He/She says that all the time, but he/she has not been doing well in last few months. It is hard for him/her to move around. He/She does not eat well and has lost a lot of weight. He/She has been admitted so many times and nobody has been doing anything to make him/her better. We really are concerned about his/her weight loss and we think he/she will do better if he/she just eats better.

**Patient**: I am fine, and my family just worries a lot. I know I’m not going to be around forever, but I’ve got a while left, right, doc? I just want to be home. What do you think, doc? When will I be ready to go home? I usually stay here for 1-2 days only.

If the doctor asks you how you are REALLY doing and other similar questions about home, you will admit that things seem to be going downhill; after each hospitalization you seem to have a bit more trouble, be a little weaker. You signed DNI/DNR orders previously; you want them still in effect (if asked) and specifically will say, “If my heart or my breathing stops, I don’t want any CPR (or anyone pounding on my chest) and I don’t want any tubes. I know someone who died on a ventilator and it seems like a horrible way to go. I will leave it up to God.”

**Both:** Your challenge is to walk a fine line between denial and the reality of Chris’s failing health and diminished functioning. When done skillfully and with empathy, you will face up to that reality. If the doctor mentions the DNI/DNR order you signed, you remember it but think of it as being only for ‘a real emergency’; it doesn’t seem relevant to the last few admissions when you get ‘patched up’.

**Chris’s emotion:** real sadness but also some acceptance – you aren’t expecting a prognosis of months but were hoping for a year or even two.

**Family member emotion:** deep sadness and surprise at what you feel is a ‘grim’ prognosis (if given) and then acceptance.

Be careful not to let the discussion between the two of you take over/monopolize the interview.
