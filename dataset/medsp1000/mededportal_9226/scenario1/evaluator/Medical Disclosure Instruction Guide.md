**Title:** Disclosure of Medical Error –Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) (Authors: Antoun, Romani and Saab)

**Purpose:** The purpose of this OSCE is to develop an OSCE station that evaluates the resident’s communication skills in disclosing a medical error.

**Educational Objectives:**

1. Demonstrate effective communication skills when disclosing medical error
2. To be able to disclose medical error without blaming others
3. To assume the responsibility of the error
4. To be able to offer apology
5. To recommend current and future actions after the medical error event.

**Description of development process:** The Family Medicine Department at the American University of Beirut administers an OSCE as a summative assessment to evaluate the clinical skills of their residents at the end of each residency year. Faculty members are encouraged to submit an OSCE station annually. Due to the recent emphasis on the importance of quality of care and disclosure of medical error worldwide and an initiative on patient safety was launched at our institution, our department delivered a didactic lecture to the residents about the subject and thus we attempted to develop an OSCE station to assess their skills.

The medical literature 1-5 was reviewed and revealed that there is a gap between the needs of the patients and the physicians after a medical error. Key points were deducted from the literature concerning the needs of the patients such as desire for apology, receiving information about what has happened and how to prevent similar errors in the future. Physicians, in general, do not provide emotional support to the patient. A summary of the guidelines and elements needed for proper disclosure of medical error was formulated in the form of the checklist statements. Then the patient scenario was created to reveal a medical error along with specific personal life experiences that would enrich the psychological aspect of the medical error (for details, see methods used for training section below).

The OSCE station was written and was reviewed by 2 faculty members as a general feedback with comments and modifications to the checklist and scenario. The faculty are members of Communication Skills Working Group at the institution with good training and skills in giving bad news. Moreover, they were instructors in a workshop on how to create OSCE stations and deliver OSCE sessions. They are the members who usually review all the OSCE stations that are submitted by the faculty.

**Information pertaining to how the materials have been used:** This station was part of the end of the year OSCE that is composed of 12 stations, each lasting 5 minutes. The residents were provided with written instruction at the door of the station office that explains the problem and their task. Faculty members were present in the station office to evaluate the residents using a predetermined checklist.

**Methods used for training:** The Simulated Patient (SP) was carefully chosen to be able to portray the case properly based on our experience with him as (1) a good actor who can respond to the residents’ behaviors and actions properly, (2) punctual; and (4) good ability to stick o the scenario with no improvisations and change of facts. The SP is graduate in Business Administration and he has experience in acting and producing as member of the Drama club. The SP has been trained to show anger when the physician discloses the error. The history was created to reveal concerns and personal life experiences that would aggravate his anger towards this particular error such as the recent news that his brother is having more dialysis session and his daughter is leaving the country and this will leave the SP with a lessened social support. The SP was also instructed to calm down if the resident was empathic and apologizing or increase his agitation if the resident was defensive and argumentative. Training of the SP occurred for one hour before the OSCE session where he was given the material to read; then the trainer went over the scenario, tactics and emotions to be displayed. Then the examiner role-played with the SP few times to insure that the he has mastered his role.

**Data to support the content of the materials:** The OSCE station was developed based on the Canadian Disclosure Guidelines (Disclosure Working Group. Canadian disclosure guidelines. Edmonton, AB: Canadian Patient Safety Institute; 2008).

**Data to support the reliability of any related checklists or rating scales:** The OSCE station was videotaped and 2 faculty members evaluated the 16 residents. Inter-rater reliability was 0.816.

**Data to support the accuracy of case portrayal:** The residents commented on the realism of the portrayal by the SP. Observing the videotapes have clearly proved the accuracy of the case portrayal.

**Lessons learned and general suggestions for using the materials:** The SP should be carefully chosen from the SP pool to be an excellent actor who can accommodate smoothly to the emotions and behavior of residents. As we have noticed from the implementation of this OSCE, it is very difficult to anticipate the behaviors and actions of the residents; therefore the SP should be intuitive and be able to respond properly.

The examiner should be informed that in questions 1.1 and 2.1, the resident should state directly that he/her has bad news and he/she apologizes. The residents should attempt to relay the message using direct words. Some residents were showing that they are sorry but did not use the words ”I am sorry”.
