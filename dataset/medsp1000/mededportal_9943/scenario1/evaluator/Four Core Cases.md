CASE ONE

I. Title: Status Asthmaticus

III. Goals and Objectives:

A. Goals: o Ensure resident facility with the management of complicated status asthmaticus o Develop resident procedural competency with peripheral intravenous (IV) line insertion o Improve junior and senior residents' crisis resource management and team leadership skills

B. Learning Objectives:

o Recognize the signs and symptoms of status asthmaticus
o Correctly place a peripheral intravenous line
o Interpret potential labs and imaging (venous blood gas, serum electrolytes and chest x-ray) in the management of status asthmaticus
o State the indications, advantages and disadvantages of initiating non-invasive ventilation in a patient with status asthmaticus
o For team leader, assume leadership of the situation, assign clear roles and demonstrate closed-loop communication
o For team leader, intermittently summarize and maintain a global view

C. Critical Actions Checklist: as listed in the scenario branch points and Tool for Resuscitation Assessment Using Computerized Simulation (TRACS)

VI. Case Narrative: night team residents are receiving sign-out on the pediatric inpatient floor from the day team.

A. Scenario:

o Chief Complaint: Respiratory distress
o History of Present Illness: A 4 year-old boy with past medical history of moderate persistent asthma was admitted for status asthmaticus in the setting of allergen exposure to his grandmother's cat. In the Emergency Department (ED), the patient was afebrile and tachypneic (respiratory rate 30 breaths per minute), with a room air oxygen saturation of 98%. He was not grunting, but was flaring with suprasternal, subcostal and intercostal retractions. His lungs had fair air entry bilaterally and biphasic wheezing. He received 3 sequential nebulizer treatments of albuterol mixed with ipratropium, and 2 mg/kg of prednisolone, after which his oxygen saturation dropped to 93%. His air entry and work of breathing were somewhat improved, and he was admitted to the floor. He was inadvertently spaced on his albuterol treatments, and now the nurse reports increased work of breathing at the 3-hour mark.
o Past Medical History: Asthma, with two prior hospitalizations, and one prior intensive care unit (ICU) stay for continuous nebulization; no prior intubations
o Allergies: seasonal (spring), animals, mites, rodents, roaches; no known drug allergies
o Medications: albuterol as needed (pro re nata, PRN), Flovent, Singulair
o Weight: 14 kg

B. Initial Scenario Conditions: the nurse asks an intern to assess this patient due to tachypnea and increased work of breathing.

Initial Physical Exam:

o Vitals: Temperature (T) 98; Heart Rate (HR) 135; Blood Pressure (BP) 110/50; Respiratory Rate (RR) 32; Oxygen Saturation (O2) 96% in room air
o Gen: patient is short of breath, and says it's hard to breathe. He is flaring but nontoxic appearing
o CV: tachycardic, otherwise normal
o Lungs: shallow tachypnea; poor air entry bilaterally with distant, symmetric biphasic wheezing, diffuse retractions and nasal flaring

Critical Actions Checklist:

o Intern calls for backup from supervising resident
o Administer additional three nebulizers of combined albuterol and ipratroprium (instructor may simulate time lapse without change in patient's status)
o IV placement
o May obtain venous blood gas and serum electrolytes
o Administer 20 cc/kg normal saline bolus
o Administer 40 mg/kg IV magnesium sulfate
o Consult or notify pediatric intensive care unit (PICU)

C. Scenario Branch Point: approximately 1hr 20 minutes has passed pursuing all the interventions above and the patient's respiratory status is worsening.

Physical Exam:

o Vitals: T 98; HR 140; BP 100/45; RR 36; O2 92% in room air
o Gen: patient is flaring and retracting. He is only able to say 1-2 words but remains alert
o CV: tachycardic, otherwise normal; capillary refill ~ 2 seconds
o Lungs: shallow tachypnea, very poor air entry bilaterally, diffuse retractions

Critical Actions Checklist:

o If not previously performed, do not allow patient to eat or drink (nil per os, NPO) and administer IV methylprednisone
o Order chest x-ray
o Initiate continuous albuterol (suggested dose 0.5mg/kg/hr; maximum total 20mg/hr) and titrate to heart rate
o Recognize the need for non-invasive ventilation and call respiratory therapy
o Alert Attending and initiate PICU transfer
o May also perform the following actions (not necessary):
o Repeat normal saline bolus
o Initiate H2 blocker or proton-pump inhibitor
o Initiate terbutaline: suggested loading dose 5-10 mcg/kg IV over 10 min, then infuse at 0.4 mcg/kg/min IV; if initiating terbutaline, consider following serial electrocardiogram, troponin and creatinine kinase every 12hrs
o Consider epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg intramuscular (IM)
o Verbalize potential need for endotracheal intubation

D. Conclusion of Scenario: if respiratory therapist is participating in the scenario, have them set up noninvasive ventilator (and demonstrate) before concluding.

Instructor Guide:

a. Algorithm for status asthmaticus management:

o Back-to-back combined albuterol and ipratropium for 3 doses
o Methylprednisone 2 mg/kg IV or Prednisolone 2 mg/kg orally (per os, PO) (max = 80 mg)
o If poor response, add magnesium sulfate 40 mg/kg IV (max 2 grams) over 20 minutes with normal saline bolus and continuous nebulized albuterol at 0.5 mg/kg/hr (max = 20 mg/hr)
o If persistent poor response, add: terbutaline with suggested loading dose 5-10 mcg/kg or subcutaneous (SC) over 10 minutes, then infuse at 0.4 mcg/kg/min IV. Increase by 0.1 mcg/kg/min to 1 mcg/kg/min, then increase by 0.5 mcg/kg/min titrating to HR
o May consider Heliox if fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) requirement is less than ~35%
Teaching points regarding non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) in status asthmaticus:
o Venous blood gas must be interpreted in clinical context - normalizing PCO2 may actually represent fatigue and relative hypercarbia
o In asthma, distal airways retain residual pressure at the end of expiration (auto-positive endexpiratory pressure, or auto-PEEP)
o Auto-PEEP increases the required negative intrathoracic pressure to trigger inspiration, thus increasing work of breathing and respiratory muscle fatigue
o Applying extrinsic PEEP with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) stents open the more proximal airways that are prone to collapse, improving work of breathing, oxygenation and potentially also ventilation
o Bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) improves tidal volumes, aiding ventilation
o May cause gastric distension with increased aspiration risk - high risk in emergent intubation
o NIPPV is a temporizing measure, and does not provide definitive airway control
