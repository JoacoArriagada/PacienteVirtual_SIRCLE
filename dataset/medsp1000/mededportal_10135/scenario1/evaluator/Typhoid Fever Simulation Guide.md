# Typhoid Fever presenting as Heat Stroke

A Simulation Case

Target Audience: Medical Students, Interns, Residents

# Intended Educational Use and Objectives

This document is intended to support the simulation based training of the target audience and may also be used in a simulation based evaluation of resuscitation in the content areas of heat related illness and travel medicine.

# Learning Objectives

1. Gain comfort approaching a pediatric patient in need of immediate resuscitation.   
2. Become familiar with the presentation of heat stroke and the initial management of hyperthermia.   
3. Become familiar with Typhoid Fever, and specifically Typhoid Encephalopathy as a presentation of this disease.   
4. Understand the need for a broad differential, even in patients that seem straightforward, and appreciate the importance of a good travel history.

# Critical Actions Checklist

1. Perform Airway/Breathing/Circulation (ABC) evaluation immediately.   
2. Ask that patient be placed on monitor, 2 large bore peripheral IV lines obtained, and oxygen supplementation initiated.   
3. Check finger stick blood glucose.   
4. Initiate a 20 mL/kg bolus of crystalloid and monitor response in mental status, heart rate, and blood pressure.   
5. Begin rapid cooling efforts: this may include ice packs, mist, fans. Learners should note the importance of continued core temperature monitoring.   
6. Consider at least heat stroke and sepsis / septic shock in the differential.

7. Draw blood cultures and request broad spectrum IV antibiotics.

8. Initiate appropriate laboratory workup, including complete blood count (CBC), basic metabolic panel (BMP), liver function test (LFT), chest x-ray (CXR), urinalysis (UA), urine culture, urine pregnancy test (UPT), Urine tox screen, ETOH level, blood gas, lactate, coagulation profile, consider creatine phosphokinase (CPK), bedside US for resuscitation evaluation, also perfectly reasonable to consider computed tomography (CT) of the head and lumbar puncture (LP) as well, provided patient is stable enough to perform these safely.

9. Admit patient to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU).

Note: See Scoring section for a discussion on what constitutes meeting these critical actions.

Management: This section is intended to be a summary of the overall management goals for the learners. The progression of patient states below will help the instructor to guide the learners through the case in response to successful (or failure of) completion of the various critical actions.

ABC’s should be addressed on arrival. This means the learners should verbally acknowledge what the presenting airway / breathing / circulation status is for this patient and then intervene. She is protecting her airway, breathing adequately to maintain oxygenation and ventilation, but is in shock. Resuscitation should thus commence with immediate attention to stabilization of her shock. She should be placed immediately on cardiac monitor, pulse ox, and oxygen applied. Two large bore peripheral intravenous (IV) lines should be requested, bolus IV fluid resuscitation in the form of at least one 20 mL/kg crystalloid bolus started. Learners should perform or ask for frequent blood pressure (BP), heart rate, and mental status monitoring and patient reassessments, and if there is failure to recognize and intervene initially, learners may consider pressors for deterioration in BP or mental status. Bedside ultrasound (US) of heart, inferior vena cava, and lungs to evaluate etiology would also be an option (left to the discretion of the instructor). Learner must recognize importance of acute management of environmental hyperthermia with altered mental status (i.e., heat stroke) and initiate active cooling immediately while workup progresses. This may include ice bags, fans, cool mist, removal of any clothing, etc. Ultimately, while initiating resuscitation for what appears to be heat stroke, caregiver must entertain wide range of etiologies of fever and AMS. Given that septic shock is high on the differential, learner should obtain blood cultures and initiate broad spectrum antibiotics before the patient is dispositioned. Once stabilized, learner should ask family for any pertinent history, including travel history (parents may prompt this topic by asking if their recent travel to India makes any difference), to raise index of suspicion for Typhoid Fever or other tropical or travel medicine related infectious etiologies. Patient should ultimately be admitted to PICU.

This case simulation intended to be scored Pass / Fail.

To pass the case, the learner should successfully meet all critical actions. There will be plenty of cases in which the instructor must prompt or cue the participant to meet a critical action, and this should not result in a failing grade except in cases where a significant amount of assistance is required - beyond that which is deemed appropriate by the instructor.

# Discussion / Debriefing

A brief group discussion session with all learners should follow the completion of the simulation scenario, with the objective being two-fold. To review how successful the groups were at completing the cases and to review pediatric resuscitation, heat stroke, and Typhoid Fever itself.

This may include a didactic presentation if desired. On the pages that follow are copies of PowerPoint slides that can be used for a review of these topics. Instructors are encouraged to create and use their own didactic exercises / presentations if desired to best meet their unique circumstances. This set of slides is not intended to be a comprehensive review of the topics covered.

The following are topics / questions that may help facilitate discussion:

How successful were you at accomplishing the critical actions?

What specific limitations can you identify in your management of the case?

What things do you feel you did well during the case and why were you successful?

What feedback do you have for the facilitators for future uses of this case?

# Pilot Testing

This case was utilized during a 4 hour morning conference simulation experience for Emergency Medicine residents and 4th year medical students at the Palmetto Health University of South Carolina School of Medicine Simulation Center in Columbia, SC in the Spring of 2013.

Approximately 20 learners participated in the simulation, divided into four groups of 5 members each. The groups rotated through four different scenario rooms, each containing a different travel medicine related simulation of which this Typhoid Fever case was one.

Each instructor had access to the critical actions checklist above and used it to either pass or fail the group during each run of the case. The case was run a total of four times, and each group passed in the view of the instructor.

One main challenge was noticed. Completing the critical actions or not is a fairly straightforward criteria, but whether and how much prompting is allowed before a group fails is much more difficult to specify. We have wrestled with the decision whether to try to specify more strictly how much prompting is allowed and have decided intentionally to leave this to the discretion of the instructor. In our view, simulation scenarios are fluid and fast paced exercises (much like actual patient care in the emergency department). The benefit of being able to make it over a few hurdles and get passed some gaps in skill or knowledge to make it efficiently through the case, we feel, far outweighs any advantage strict uniformity in simulation administration or scoring might confer.

An observation not unique to this simulation experience, but certainly one to make note of and account for is this: in a session that includes groups rotating through multiple sessions, the latter groups will have figured out the topic of the day and will likely be much better prepared to handle the particular nuances of the case than the earlier groups. Based on this experience, we suggest that instructors raise the bar, so to speak, for latter groups and offer less prompting. In this case, that would mean perhaps that the patients parents do not ever cue the learners to ask a travel history.

In terms of feedback from the pilot run of the case, a survey was sent to all learners following the case asking the learners to rate the quality of the presentation and the value of the content on a scale from 1 to 5 and providing a space for open comments.

Feedback was overall positive. On the 1-5 scale, on average learners rated the activity a 4.0 for the value of the content and a 4.36 for the quality of the presentation.

Specific positive comments included:

“Good review of stuff we don’t see often.” “Good review of how to handle foreign travel illnesses.”

Constructive criticism included:

“I wish the lecture was given more time, too much crammed into time allotted.”

We continually try to address constructive feedback. Since the original pilot (and since the original submission of this case) we have lengthened the powerpoint discussion section in order to better and more fully address the learning objectives.

Similarly, it became clear to us (as it did to our first reviewers), that this case, while concerning a patient with typhoid fever, was mostly a case geared toward successfully resuscitating a patient in shock. This prompted changes in the learning objectives and discussion to better account for this. We feel it is important to include these topics in simulation medicine experiences as they are rarely found among typical simulation medicine topics. What we hope is that by including such topics in scenarios like this, which can be exciting and memorable, we will link in learners’ minds travel medicine topics with exciting case simulations and thereby improve long term content retention.

# During the resuscitation

Once initial resuscitative efforts underway and ABC’s addressed, consider differential while performing secondary survey to look for clues.

Gather more history: ask parents about circumstances, patient’s history, meds, recent illnesses, recent travel.

In this case: Diaphoresis, AMS, temp of 105.8. Circumstances suggest heat stroke, but also patient recently returned from trip to India.

# Form differential and treat

In this case: Heat Stroke, infectious causes, NMS or other toxidrome, thyroid storm, DKA, status epilepticus.

Consider meningitis, encephalitis, sepsis from another source like pyelonephritis, cellulitis, pneumonia.

Workup and treat based on differential

Rapid cooling for presumed heat stroke.

Cultures, CXR, UA, LP, and broad spectrum antibiotics to cover possible sepsis.

# Heat Stroke

A true medical Emergency

Classic Triad: Temp $>$ 105, CNS dysfunction, Anhidrosis (but often get significant sweating with exertional heat stroke)

Also: Tachycardia, Tachypnea, Hypotension

Key to differentiate from mere heat exhaustion: AMS, i.e.:

Syncope, irritability, bizarre behavior, combativeness

Remember: no core temp cutoff at which heat stroke begins.

# Heat Stroke management

IV / O2 / Monitor / ABC’s all immediately

Remove patient from heat source

Rapid cooling: cool fluids, mist, fans, ice packs

Goal - cool to 102.2 and then stop all cooling to prevent overshoot.

Treat shivering with benzos.

Watch for Rhabdomyolysis, trend CPK.

Good prognosis: coma < 4 hrs, AST < 1000 after 24 hrs

Poor prognosis: coma $>$ 10 hrs, temp > 108, high PT

# Typhoid Fever

Cause: Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi or S. enterica serotype paratyphi $A , B , C .$

Highest incidence in impoverished areas with poor sanitation. SE / S. Central Asia, Southern Africa.

200-300 cases in U.S. per year. 80% among unvaccinated travelers to these areas.

Transmission: fecal/oral. Humans are the only reservoir. Incubation is 5-21 days.

Chronic asymptomatic carriage in 1-6% (Typhoid Mary)

# Presentation - "enteric fever"

Classic: abdominal pain, fever, chills

Untreated course: mortality 15%

lst week - rising stepwise fevers, bacteremia, chills, pulse/temp dissociation

2nd week - abdominal pain, rose spots

3rd week - HSM, GI bleed, intestinal perf from lymphatic hyperplasia (15%)

Typhoid Encephalopathy in 17%: AMS, delirium, confusion. Poor prognosis.

Labs: Leukocytosis/Leukopenia, elevated LFT

# Management

Diagnosis - Blood culture

Treatment: Antibiotics for 10-14 days. Susceptibilities important given significant MDR strains.

Start IV: Amp, Roceph, Cipro, Azithro

$\textcircled{2}$ Then PO: often Cipro.

Manage complications, e.g., surgical intervention for intestinal perforation.

# Case Conclusion

Patient had been in India for 3 weeks. Presented to ED 2 weeks after returning. Food always cooked, but $+ /$ - bottled water.

Initial treatment focused on cooling. Had Fever until hospital day 4.

Cultures: S. enterica serotype paratyphi C

Started empirically on Amp and Roceph IV ampicillin, then 7 days of PO Cipro.

In keeping with heat stroke like process, transaminitis and elevated CPK developed.

Complete recovery, d/c on hosp day 9.

# Prevention

Vaccines: Live and Polysaccharide. Neither is completely effective for S. Typhi No vaccine for S. Paratyphi

Good hygiene. Don't drink the water. Eat cooked food or food you peel.
