# Typhoid Fever presenting as Heat Stroke

Instructors Guide

# III. Goal of this simulation case:

This simulation case is intended to support the simulation based training of the target audience and may also be used in a simulation based evaluation of resuscitation in the content areas of heat related illness and travel medicine.

# IV. Conceptual Background:

This case grew out of an actual patient encounter during a hot summer in the Southeast of the United States, where heat illness is common but where typhoid fever is very rare. The author adequately stabilized, resuscitated, and admitted the patient, but failed to uncover the appropriate travel history that might have allowed a more prompt diagnosis of typhoid fever to be made. In response, this simulation case was created to highlight both the resuscitative core competencies involved in stabilizing a patient presenting clinically with heat stroke and the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and taking a thorough history, in particular a travel history. As we have recently seen with Ebola in West Africa and its spread throughout other parts of the world (as just one prominent example), a keen awareness of possible infectious etiologies and the habit of obtaining a good travel history are critical skills for the Emergency Physician to aid in putting these diagnostic puzzles together.

# V. How to run the case:

Prepare the simulated exam room (or any area you choose for this purpose) with a mannequin on an exam bed. Do not connect monitors, oxygen, or other devices prior to starting the case. Instruct role players (parents, paramedics, etc) to wait in the room until participants enter. Gather participants outside the exam room. Select a leader of the group who will be the primary person in charge and who will play the role of the lead physician. Instruct the other participants to contribute as would any ancillary staff member (or perhaps as a team including residents, interns, and medical students, depending on the makeup of your group). Give the participants a brief intro to the activity and then deliver the information on the Triage Form, either by reading it or by giving it to them to read themselves. Once they’ve reviewed this information, allow them to enter the exam room and begin the case. As they begin to interact with the simulated patient, the instructor may stay in the room or remain outside observing. Either way, we recommend staying in close communication with the participants so that the instructor can quickly deliver info, answer questions, and help guide the case through the various patient states in response to participant actions. Once the team has successfully stabilized and admitted the patient (or failed to do so and it’s time to stop), announce an end to the case and lead a brief discussion. This may be at the bedside with the small group or all together with multiple groups, depending on instructor preference. The included powerpoint is best delivered to the entire group at one time, but brief individualized feedback/discussion sessions immediately following the case may be useful for some instructors and groups.

# V. Practical Implementation Advice:

This case is fairly straightforward and can be run either with or without sophisticated simulation equipment, though a mannequin is preferred over a human volunteer so that if the participants decide to, they may initiate CPR, intubate, etc. One adolescent sized mannequin is sufficient for a group of 3-5 participants. The technical capabilities of the mannequin will determine the level of complexity it can achieve when attempting to personify the various states the patient goes through during the case. While it is possible for the participants to “imagine” that they are using standard emergency department equipment, it is helpful when running this simulation case to have on hand the following basic equipment:

Simulated monitor that can report real time vital signs   
Code cart   
Airway equipment

While it is certainly an option, using real ice and actual fans for the purpose of rapid cooling is best left to the imagination for two main reasons. First, too much mess is created. Second, typically it is most effective if participants do not know what the case is about before it begins. Having ice and fans on hand will easily tip off the observant participant.

This case should include a range of 3 to 5 participants for ideal instructional benefit. The simulation itself may be a standalone experience with small groups rotating through the scenario room or it may be included as part of a larger multi-simulation experience, where small groups of participants move from room to room. The case is designed to take no longer than 20-25 minutes to complete, but fast participants may finish in 10 minutes. Allow 5-10 minutes after completion for immediate questions, but hold off on full discussion until large group re-convenes for debriefing and didactic presentation.

Take time to review carefully the entire set of simulation materials, including especially the various patient states, verbal reports, critical actions and cues. The case can move quite quickly and it makes for an awkward simulation experience if the instructor has to continually refer back to the written materials in order to respond to participant actions and questions. Have enough familiarity with the case and the educational goals to be able to respond fluidly.

Only one faculty member (or instructor) is required to run the case. He or she may be in the room our outside depending on facility layout and desired level of team interaction. He or she may simply announce changes in the patient without making actual changes to the mannequin itself. It is helpful, if available, to have a simulation staff member who can make real time changes in the mannequin as the case unfolds.

# VI. Lessons Learned:

At our institution, we have a simulation center with a full staff and multiple simulation rooms. The setup we have used accommodates about 18-20 participants split into four groups which rotate through four simulation rooms, each running a different case centered on the same general topic. This culminates in a large group debriefing session after each group has completed each case.

In our experience, participants tend not to spend as much time as is required to obtain an adequate history. The simulation environment seems to foster an urgency toward immediate action (which is required) but participants often forget that while stabilization efforts are underway, information gathering can be very helpful. It may be helpful to carefully interject cues to remind participants that more history is available from, e.g., the mom and paramedics in this case.

Prioritize what you want to emphasize. We do not recommend that the instructor allow the case to bog down while participants try to correctly read a CXR, for example. If the case is stalling, help them quickly reach a correct interpretation and allow the case to move forward.

# VII. Limitations:

It is not easy to make dynamic and exciting simulation cases about travel medicine infectious disease topics. This case clearly includes goals pertinent to resuscitation and heat illness, while a primary overall educational goal is to emphasize to participants the importance of a broad differential including travel medicine topics. Though it may be more difficult for this case than it is for other simulation cases, we encourage you (and will try ourselves) to focus on this idea: in the same way that an exciting and memorable case in the emergency department leaves a lasting memory that can be called upon in future patient encounters, an exciting and memorable simulation case may have the same effect. Thus, using the excitement of resuscitation and heat stroke to piggyback the clinical details and severe presentation of typhoid fever, while it may seem a stretch, may be the best way to incorporate these travel medicine topics into a simulation setting. Framing it this way, both among instructors, and with participants after the session is over, may help everyone appreciate the importance and value of the case.
