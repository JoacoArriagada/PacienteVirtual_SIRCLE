# Discussion:

Acute mesenteric ischemia refers to decreased blood flow to the intestine and may be secondary to occlusive or non-occlusive etiologies.1, 2, 3 The occlusive etiologies include arterial thrombus or embolus and venous thrombus while the non-occlusive etiologies are secondary to hypoperfusion or “low-flow” states. Each etiology has risk factors associated with it which may provide clues to the etiology once the diagnosis is suspected. Arterial emboli are associated with cardiac dysrhythmias, arterial thrombi with underlying vascular disease, venous thrombi with hypercoaguable states and non-occlusive etiologies with sustained intestinal vasoconstriction or with shock states.1

The history and physical exam are key components of this condition. The classic history is abdominal pain “out of proportion” to exam, meaning that the patient’s abdominal exam is unrevealing, non-specific or non-acute, though the patient complains of severe, refractory pain. The abdominal exam, however, may range from the above to peritoneal signs as the gut infarcts and gut flora translocate into the peritoneum.1, 2, 3

There is no specific laboratory test to diagnosis. Leukocytosis is common.1, 2 As intestine loses its blood supply and infarcts, lactate levels will rise. Largely, the diagnosis will begin with a high degree of clinical suspicion and will be confirmed by imaging.

Plain films (XR) may show air in the biliary tree, obstruction, pneumatosis intestinalis (air in the bowel wall) or free intraperitoneal air.

Contrasted computed tomography (CT) can show actual clot in the vasculature, changes in bowel wall enhancement, fat stranding or solid organ infarction. Non-contrasted scans could show you air in the biliary tree, portal vein, pneumatosis intestinalis, bowel obstruction. Computed Tomography Angiogram with IV contrast is the study of choice if mesenteric ischemia is suspected. 1

Angiogram is considered the gold standard for this diagnosis and confers the advantage of allowing the operator to deliver intra-arterial thrombolytics or intra-arterial papaverine as well as visualize more peripheral vasculature. The downside of angiography include the inability to use it on critically ill patients, the dye load involved.1

Magnetic Resonance and Ultrasound have promising roles in the diagnosis of acute mesenteric ischemia but are not routinely used modalities. 1, 2, 3, 4

The treatment of these patients rests on stabilization and resuscitation. If the patient is hypovolemia, suffering from a dysrhythmia, not protecting their airway, etcetera, then controlling this is the first stage in the management of mesenteric ischemia. In this case, the patient is clearly suffering with an unstable tachycardia, for which ACLS protocols dictate the need for cardioversion. In several arms of the algorithm, the patient enters into a PEA rhythm which, if managed according to ACLS protocol, allows the participant to completely start over the case (albeit now with an intubated patient!)

Further, hemodynamic monitoring and monitoring of fluid status (via central venous pressure and fluid output)1, 2 are indicated in these critical patients.

Regardless of etiology, if the patient exhibits peritoneal signs, they require a surgical evaluation. 1 Our surgeon deliberately refused the consult in order to force the participant into stabilizing the patient. Ideally, this would not occur in reality.

Once the patient is stable and a diagnosis has been made, definitive treatment can begin. Treatment options depend on the etiology of the ischemia. Papaverine, surgical embolectomy and intra-arterial thrombolysis are options for arterial embolus. Arterial thrombus is managed via either papaverine or bypass grafting and venous thrombus is managed with anticoagulation.1, 4 Thrombolytics are contraindicated in the case of bowel infarction. 4 Non-occlusive disease can be managed with vasodilators or with angioplasty with or without stenting or revascularization.1, 4 Antibiosis should be broad2 and cover for gut flora.

The ideal, most concise path for the management of our patient would have begun with a complete history and physical while ordering cardiac monitor and venous access, fluid bolus, obtaining an EKG, ordering all lab work, two rounds of cardioversion, reassessment of vitals, obtaining CT with results, initiating NGT, more fluids and antibiosis while obtaining a surgical consult.
