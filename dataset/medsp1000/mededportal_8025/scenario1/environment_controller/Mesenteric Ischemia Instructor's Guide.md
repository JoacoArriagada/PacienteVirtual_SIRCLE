Instruction guide for “Mesenteric Ischemia”

**Practical Implementation Advice**: For the purpose of board preparation, this case can be executed without many extra materials or props. Walking students through the case verbally helps them think aloud and many of the buzzwords associated with mesenteric ischemia can be easily inserted into the case at the appropriate times. For example, should a student ask for an abdominal X-ray and the examiner replies that it comes back showing pneumatosis intestinalis or air in the biliary tree, this should be recognized and prompt the student to take action. The case is geared toward managing this condition, not toward the interpretation of X-ray, CT or EKG. As such, no materials are necessary.

As a teaching point, the patient’s mesenteric ischemia is secondary to an embolus, not a low-flow state. Understanding the various pathophysiologies of mesenteric ischemia is important as one might be inclined to give fluids to correct the hypotension- an intervention which the algorithm demonstrates will yield no hemodynamic response. While the fluids might seem like a solution, they can be implemented while instituting cardioversion without harming or worsening the patient. Following the ACLS protocols for unstable tachycardia will resuscitate the patient, regardless of the surgical issue.

The proctors of the case found the same points of the case challenging – to be unrevealing at a point where they know that the participant knows what to do but is unwilling to actually make that step. Generally, the proctor would encourage the team leader of the cases to consult with their “team” for the next step in management to facilitate the progression of the case.

Also, remembering the management of the patient’s arrhythmia is essential for hemodynamic stability and that no other service will do anything until this is done. People seem to become preoccupied on the abdomen and are not aggressive enough with airway, breathing and circulation.

The limitation of this resource is that it requires the proctors to have a thorough knowledge of mesenteric ischemia, the case as it is written and the management this disease. That there are several modalities for definitive treatment means that participants may suggest treatment measures that are correct but not the *most correct* at that particular time. This case requires the proctor to take an active role in leading the participant to some degree to avoid these alternatives. This has been overcome, in our experience, by emphasizing the instability of the patient and the need for resuscitation above all else.
