Simulation of Respiratory Distress for the 3rd Year Medical Student

Station 2

Simulation Portion

1. Introduction: The students will be expected to participate as if the simulation is in real time. Review what your simulation mannequin is capable of (breathing, heart sounds, pulses, vascular access points etc.). Allow the students to have access to the same equipment they would have in the patient care areas and give them time to familiarize themselves with the equipment. Discuss with the students the concept of team roles during a code situation, choosing a code leader and assigning specific roles to each participant. Choose a student to be the initial responder. During the case, the person running the simulation should not interact with the students except to answer prompts (eg state laboratory results) and the students should be reminded of this prior to starting the case.

2. Simulator set up: 21 month old with vital signs of HR 120 RR 40 O2 sat 94% with diffuse wheezing.

3. Case Scenario: You are seeing a patient who was just admitted to the floors. She came in through the ER and your intern got sign out but is now busy with another patient.

4. The case prompt should be enough to get the students started. Only give the included information that is asked for by the students.

1. History: This is a 21 month old girl with no past medical history who has been having a runny nose and cough for the past day. This morning she began wheezing and her father thought she was having trouble breathing. She had a fever at home to 101 and she was not eating or drinking as much as usual. She has never been sick before, she was born full term, has all of her vaccines and has no allergies. In the ER, she got some “medicine” but dad doesn’t know what it was.

2. Physical Exam: Vital Signs are HR 120 RR 40 O2 94% on room air. She is generally well appearing, acyanotic. She has a normal head and neck exam. She has subcostal retraction and abdominal breathing. She has diffuse wheezing bilaterally on her lung exam. The remainder of her exam is normal for age.

3. More detailed history and a more detailed exam can be provided from the above information and from examining the high fidelity simulator if available.

4. Runtime response:
   1. If oxygen is given via nasal cannula, oxygen saturation should rise to 96%.
   2. If oxygen is given via face mask, oxygen saturation should rise to 98%.
   3. If nebulized saline or albuterol are given, the oxygen saturation should rise to 98% and the respiratory rate should trend toward normal, with an improved physical exam.
   4. After 20 minutes have passed, the child again has an increased respiratory rate and decreased oxygen saturation.
   5. If another saline or albuterol neb is provided at that point, the child will only display minor improvement.
   6. If basic lab studies are requested, viral studies would show that the child has RSV bronchiolitis.
   7. If appropriate support is not given, the child’s respiratory and heart rates should increase while the oxygen saturation decreases. This decline should continue with impending respiratory failure.
   8. At any point the clinical status can improve with oxygen, albuterol or saline.
   9. If appropriate support continues not to be given, the child will progress to respiratory failure requiring bag mask ventilation.

4. End points for this case:
   1. Instructor can set a time limit, we recommend 15 minutes allowing time for a debrief after the case.
   2. Alternatively, instructor can stop the case if the students perfectly manage and stabilize the child.
   3. Do not let the child expire; remind students that a child can be supported by bag mask ventilation for a long time while awaiting further support from the PICU team and stop the case after the students have demonstrated proper bag mask ventilation which will increase oxygen saturation.
