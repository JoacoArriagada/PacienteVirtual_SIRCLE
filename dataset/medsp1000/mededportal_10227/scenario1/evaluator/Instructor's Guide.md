**Instructor Guide for TCA Ingestion**

**Resource Files and Use of Resource Files**

The case submission contains four elements to help run the simulation. The first element is the case narrative, which includes all pertinent case information, interpretations of the case information, and anticipated case branch points. The second element is Appendix A, a PowerPoint that contains pertinent case lab information, x-ray, and EKG to be used during the case simulation. The third element is Appendix B, a PowerPoint that contains teaching points for the debriefing session. Finally, an evaluation form is included, that contains recommended care actions.

**Purpose and Education Objectives**

Acute TCA overdose is an uncommon but life threatening ingestion. The purpose of this simulation is to present an acute TCA overdose case that allows medical students, emergency medicine residents, and pediatric emergency medicine fellows to test their knowledge base and critically evaluate the management of this type of ingestion.

Primary learning objectives include

1. Recognize the neurologic and cardiovascular effects of acute TCA toxicity
2. Identify symptoms of anticholinergic toxidrome in the patient examination
3. Implement fluid resuscitation for cardiovascular effects
4. Interpret the wide complex ECG changes as Na-channel blockade effect of TCA toxicity

Secondary learning objectives include

1. Recognize risks and benefits of activated charcoal administration
2. Use appropriately dosed sodium bicarbonate bolus to treat Na-channel blockade
3. Detect neurologic deterioration and appropriately intubate the patient secondary to altered mental status

**Conceptual Background**

Acute TCA ingestion is an infrequent but life threatening ingestion that requires an adequate knowledge base and critical thinking to manage. A simulation case allows real-time, hands on training for residents and fellows to practice managing this type of ingestion.

**Practical Implementation**

The simulation programming is not difficult, but the instructor should be familiar with their simulator software. Approximately 30 minutes is required for this simulation, 15 minutes for the case simulation, and approximately 15 minutes for debriefing and discussion. The debriefing session should be conducted in a nonjudgmental manner, while focusing on identifying knowledge gaps and critically evaluating the case management.

**Deployment and Limitations**

As this is an uncommon ingestion, most medical students, residents and fellows are not familiar with the management of this ingestion. Expected discussion topics include a basic overview and evaluation of a TCA ingestion, decontamination efforts, and use of sodium bicarbonate boluses for EKG QRS widening.

**Effectiveness and Significance**

This case was developed to review and teach medical students, emergency medicine residents and pediatric emergency medicine fellows critical aspects to the evaluation and management of a tricyclic antidepressant overdose. A group simulation case scenario was chosen to promote clinical decision making and to critically evaluate those management decisions. This case has been used numerous times for group simulation training; additionally, the case content has been used more than 15 times as an oral board type case.

Feedback of the case scenario occurred during each of these uses. The combination of participation in the simulation scenario, interactive feedback session that focused on debriefing questions, and review of the brief PowerPoint lecture addressed all of the listed objectives for the learners. The case was definitely effective in teaching management of TCA ingestion. Additionally, most of the participants identified a general lack of comfort in managing this ingestion (even when the management decisions were correct), secondary to lack of familiarity as this is an uncommon ingestion. In this regard, the case was received well by participants as it is an opportunity to critically think through and review the management decisions.

**Lessons Learned**

As this case is based on a real case, minimal changes have been made to the case during deployment. Some participants have asked for exam findings not originally incorporated into the case, and these have been added. Cues such as suggestions to re-evaluate the patient for decline in status and to re-evaluate the EKG rhythm were added to assist beginner learners and improve case flow. During deployment at our institution, education gaps included: lack of familiarity regarding when to administer sodium bicarbonate bolus based on EKG findings, administering insufficient dosing of sodium bicarbonate (common mistake to administer one ampule of sodium bicarbonate as opposed to a weight based dosing), or initiation of bicarbonate drip instead of bolus for wide complex EKG changes.
