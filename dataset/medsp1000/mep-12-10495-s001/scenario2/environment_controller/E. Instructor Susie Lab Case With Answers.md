**Susie’s Sippy Cup Dilemma: Early Childhood Caries**

**Part B:**

Susie returns with her mother at age 12 months for her MMR (Measles, Mumps & Rubella) vaccination. She is now in the 25th percentile for growth. Susie is not walking yet, but she is crawling and will grasp onto a table and stand herself up and even let go for a moment and then fall to a sit. Susie’s mother has introduced solids and because of your earlier advice she no longer sends Susie to bed with the bottle. She has introduced Susie to a sippy cup and Susie’s favorite drink is apple juice, which she sips on happily throughout the day. Susie now has 6 teeth (teeth #D, E, F, G, O, P). Susie’s mother says Susie is brushing her own teeth twice a day. She is using a non-fluoridated toothpaste. Susie’s mother has not seen a dentist. (Note: Medicaid does not cover fillings for cavities after age 18.) Sitting on the examining table in your office, Susie is very squirmy and she cries as she sits on your examination table and you examine her. It is difficult to examine herhead and neck area.

**You decide to use the knee-to-knee position to examine Susie’s mouth.**

This position allows for

* comfort of the infant or toddler and
* decreases separation anxiety for the child because the caregiver can be seen.
* It allows for parental participation and patient restraint, if necessary.

Once positioned, start by palpating the head and neck area for lymph nodes. Lift the lip and examine the front surfaces of the upper front teeth (central and lateral incisors teeth number (D, E, F, G). Visually examine all of the dentition, the oral mucosa and its structures and the oral pharynx.

The cells that manufacture enamel are sensitive to systemic insults. Disruption in the production of enamel will result in a defect which may be micro or macroscopic. It is difficult to distinguish early decalcification from enamel defects, but this does not affect management. Enamel defects are associated with substantially increased risk of ECC.

Discuss enamel dysplasia “white spots” versus decalcification “white spots” relative to caries risk assessment. It is possible that Susie has had some disruption of normal tooth development related to her premature birth and the white areas represent enamel abnormalities. This is not unusual in premature babies. This type of “white spot” anomaly is also a risk factor for the development of ECC. So the etiology of the white spot does not matter in caries risk assessment.
