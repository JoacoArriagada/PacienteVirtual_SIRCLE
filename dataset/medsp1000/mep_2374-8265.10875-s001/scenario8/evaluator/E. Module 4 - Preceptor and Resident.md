**CASE ONE**:

**Shawna is a 45yo woman who was referred to your primary care clinic after presenting to the emergency department twice, once for a headache and the second time for abdominal pain. She reports that she has no chronic medical problems. During your history, she discloses that she is a transgender male to female person. When reviewing her intake forms, you note a positive depression screen based on Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ9).**

1. **How do mental health issues affect LGBT patients? Can you think of any unique features that may apply to this subset of patients?**

Mental health conditions faced by LGBT patients are similar to those of the general population. However, there are important differences to highlight as people identifying as LGBT may have specific concerns related to being a gender or sexual minority.

**What is the prevalence of mental health issues in LGBT patients?**

LGBT persons are nearly 3 times more likely to experience **mood disorders** i.e. depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, PTSD, and other mental health conditions than the general population. Particularly for LGBT youth aged 10-24, suicide is a leading cause of death and they are 3 times more likely to experience suicidal thoughts, attempt suicide, or engage in self-harm than heterosexual people; an estimated 38-65% of transgender individuals have experienced suicidal ideation.1 As with all patients, the main goal of a mental health assessment is to assess the patient’s safety and identify appropriate treatment options.

**What are some unique features that may apply?**

Fears and prior experiences of violence/discrimination, the coming out process, lack of familial/social support, and environmental stressors, can contribute to increased depression, substance abuse, and suicidality. A key difference is to recognize and explore how the patient has experienced stigma surrounding their sexuality, identity, family and social support – as well as their own internal stigma - all of which will vary by the individual.

**Any differences in treatment? What about conversion therapies?**

Treatment of mental health conditions involves the same modalities as for heterosexual patients, such as medications and psychotherapy. Notably, the American Psychiatric Association and the American Psychological Association both hold that conversion therapies (attempting to change sexual orientation through means of psychotherapy) are harmful, unethical, and should never be used with LGBT patients.2, 3

1. **What are the current trends in substance use and abuse in LGBT patients?**

Substance use in the United States is normative (particularly with alcohol and tobacco, and increasingly so for marijuana). Substance use disorders are linked to biological signs of abuse (withdrawal, tolerance), and/or familial, societal, interpersonal, or work place problems.

Studies indicate that LGBT individuals are more likely to use drugs, particularly rates of alcohol and tobacco use, and are more likely to continue use later in life, when compared to the heterosexual population.4 This appears to be due to earlier age of onset of use, likely as a maladaptive coping strategy, and exposure to use given higher rates of use among peers.

Certain classes of drugs appear to be more popular among LGBT people. Men who have sex with men have higher rates of stimulant use including party drugs (such as ecstasy), and crystal methamphetamine.4

Screening all patients for prior or current substance use disorders is recommended. For those patients who screen positive for at-risk use, brief interventions are often appropriate. Referrals for more intensive treatment, including pharmaco- and behavioral therapies, should be considered in all patients as needed, LGBT or otherwise.

**CASE ONE continued:**

**She discloses that she is bisexual, and recently got out of a “stressful relationship” with a male partner.**

1. **Is she at risk for intimate partner violence (IPV) or sexual violence (SV)? How would you screen her?**

**Are LGBT people more or less likely to experience IPV?**

The CDC’s **National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (NISVS): 2010 Findings on Victimization by Sexual Orientation** found that lesbians and gay men reported IPV and SV over their lifetimes at rates equal to, or higher than those of heterosexuals5. **Bisexual women are most likely to experience intimate partner violence.** Limited research exists on IPV and SV among transgender populations, although one study reported that 1 in 5 transgender individuals have experienced IPV as a direct result of their gender identity/expression 9 and another reported that 58% of older transgender individuals have experienced one or more instances of forcible sex in their lifetime10.

**Is there a recommendation to screen for DV and IPV in LGBT individuals?** The USPSTF gives a grade B recommendation to screen women of childbearing age for domestic violence, and IPV. There is no specific statement for LGBT individuals. The 6 screening tools that achieved the highest levels of sensitivity and specificity were HITS (English and Spanish versions), OVAT, STaT, HARK, modified CTQ-SF, and WAST6

**HARK TOOL (for example):**

* **H**umiliation: "In the last year, have you been humiliated or emotionally abused in other ways by your partner?"
* **A**fraid: "In the last year have you been afraid of your partner or ex-partner?"
* **R**ape: "In the last year have you been raped by your partner or forced to have any kind of sexual activity?"
* **K**ick: "In the last year have you been physically hurt by your partner?"

**CASE ONE continued:**

**Shawna endorses emotional abuse in her past relationship, and she feels safe now that she is no longer in contact with him. She then asks if you could prescribe her cross sex hormonal therapy.**

1. **What is cross sex hormonal therapy? Would you prescribe her medications?**

Definition

Cross sex hormones are used to induce or maintain the characteristics of the sex that matches gender identity.

For an individual who is already receiving cross-sex hormones, it is recommended to review the current regimen and perform a thorough assessment of the patient’s general health. It is important to consider continuing the medications if no contraindications are found. You may decide to refer to a different provider such as an endocrinologist for future management
