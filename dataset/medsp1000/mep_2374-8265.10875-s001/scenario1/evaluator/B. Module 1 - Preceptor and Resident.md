**CASE ONE:**

Diana is a 23-year old graduate student with generalized anxiety who presents for routine follow-up. She is due for a pap test and would like refills of her citalopram. When taking her social history, she reveals to you that she is doing well in graduate school and excited about her classes this semester. She reports drinking 2-3 drinks socially for happy hour 4-5 times a week. She smokes ½ pack of cigarettes a day. She states that she had previously been sexually active with men. However almost 6 months ago she began a relationship with a female partner. She is in a monogamous relationship with this partner and recently they have moved in together. This has created some conflict with her family and friends. She is worried about rejection or prejudice as she shares this information with others.

1. **What might Diana’s stress and anxiety represent, and what resources can you offer her?**

Diana’s stress about facing prejudice/judgement is common within the lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender (LGBT) population and can be characterized by the concept of minority stress.

What is minority stress?

Minority stress is a theory that can be applied to various types of stigmatized minority groups. In the LGBT community it is characterized by the experience of

* External stressors including violence, harassment, discrimination
* Internal stressors including expectation of rejection, hiding and concealing one’s identity, internalized homophobia, and coping processes associated with being part of a minority group.
* These experiences can lead to chronically high levels of stress, as well as significant psychological distress including feelings of shame and guilt.
* Chronic anxiety from expectations of rejection based on their sexuality known as rejection sensitivity.

Why is this important? Minority stress and rejection sensitivity are associated with higher rates of depression, anxiety, substance abuse, suicide, and worse cardiovascular outcomes, thus it is important to address these issues in your LGBT populations1.

It is important to identify and treat medical and mental health issues that are associated with the LGBT population to provide optimal care and perform appropriate LGBT-related screening (both covered in later module). Additionally, there are several local and National Resources to help the LGBT population with issues associated with minority stress

1. **How prevalent are LGBT individuals in the US population?**

According to the Williams Institute, 3.4% of the United States population self-identifies as lesbian, gay, bisexual and/or transgender. They also found that 8.2% report at least some same-sex sexual behavior and 11% report at least some same sex attraction. Younger individuals are more likely to identify as LGBT compared to older individuals2.

**3. What else in her social history would you like to discuss further? What increased healthcare risks does being a lesbian/bisexual woman confer?**

Diana is a current smoker and is drinking alcohol at a level that puts her at risk. Additionally, she has a history of anxiety which is seen at higher rates in the LGBT population. Young people who are members of the LGTBQ community are at a higher risk for cigarette use. Although cigarette-use and heavy smoking have decreased broadly speaking for young people, disparities remain for youth who are sexual minorities. 15 Furthermore, members of the LGBT community self-report higher levels of smoking and poor health status in general. In communities with higher levels of approval for same sex marriage, there was a lower prevalence of smoking amongst LGBTQ individuals, lending support to the hypothesis that structural stigma contributes to smoking and cigarette use.14

In addition to substance abuse and anxiety, lesbian and bisexual women have higher rates of depression, intimate partner suicide, violence and obesity. While data on bisexual women are limited, lesbian women also have a higher rate of many risk factors for breast cancer including decreased parity, and lower use of hormonal contraception, but have lower rates of screening mammograms and clinical breast exams than heterosexual women.5-7
