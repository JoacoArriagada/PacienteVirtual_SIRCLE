**CASE ONE:**

Dawn is a 49-year-old woman with a past medical history of diabetes mellitus type 2 who presents today to establish care with you as her new PCP. You learn that she has been generally healthy in the past without major illnesses. Her family history is remarkable for diabetes and hypertension in both of her parents, but no history of cancers or autoimmune disorders. She works as a manager at a local department store. She reports drinking alcohol occasionally, mostly in social situations, and smoking approximately 1 pack per day for the past 25 years. She denies illicit drug use.

Sexual history should address sexual behavior, sexual orientation identity and gender identity. Some examples of inclusive terminology are

* To start, you could ask
  + “Do you have a partner?” or “Are you in a relationship?” instead of asking “Are you married?”
  + Next you could ask, “Have you had female partners, male partners, or both?”
  + Alternatively, you could start by asking “Are you sexually active?” and then ask, “When you have had sex, what are the genders of your partners?” If patients are confused by this question, you could ask “Do you have sex with men, women, or both?”
* At the end of the sexual history you could ask “Do you have any concerns or questions about your sexuality, sexual orientation, or sexual health?”
* Finally, you should also ask whether patients identify as transgender or have gender-related concerns. For instance, you could say “Since many people are affected by gender issues, I ask patients if they have any concerns about this. If this topic is not relevant to you, tell me and I will move on.”

**CASE ONE continued:**

Dawn reveals that she is currently exclusively sexually active with her female partner of 25 years, Louise. They have two young adult children together who were conceived using a sperm bank.

The ability to take a full history of an LGBT patient, asking questions about sexual orientation and gender identity and responding non-judgmentally to the information received, facilitates the delivery of optimal health care for several reasons.

* First, it helps to establish trusting relationships in which LGBT patients feel safe and are more likely to respond honestly to sensitive questions.
* Second, it allows us to create more positive clinical encounters, which LGBT patients will more likely want to repeat.

A study in 1998 examined lesbians' perceptions of communication with primary care providers. They found that respondents who were “out” to their primary care provider were significantly more likely to

* seek health and preventive care
* more likely to have ever had a Pap smear
* and more likely to be comfortable discussing sensitive issues.

This study is a very good example of the statistically significant association between comfort discussing sexual orientation with providers, and better care seeking behaviors and healthier lifestyles.

**CASE ONE continued:**

Screening for intimate partner violence is negative. A review of systems reveals that she recently has been having trouble with insomnia because of worrying about financial problems. She requests a referral to a therapist. She states that despite her worries, her mood is generally good, and she continues to enjoy her hobbies and her time with her family. She denies any suicidal or homicidal ideation. She scores a 2 on the GAD-2, so you do not pursue other management options for her anxiety currently. The remainder of the history and physical exam is normal. You refer her for psychological counseling and a yearly eye exam.

A few months later, Dawn returns. Upon reviewing her EHR, you notice that she attended only one session with the counselor. She states that when the therapist asked her if she had any children and she said two, he went on to ask about her children’s father. Dawn avoided the questions, completed the interview, and did not return.

The therapist should not have asked a question that assumed heterosexuality. As healthcare providers, we should try to use open-ended questions that are gender-affirming and free from heterosexist bias. We might need to modify questions that we routinely ask to achieve neutrality and remove bias.

* Do not assume that all patients use traditional labels. Several people prefer more inclusive or amorphous labels, or do not like to use labels at all.

* Do not assume sexual orientation based on appearance, sexual behavior or partner’s gender.
* Do not assume sexual behavior or orientation has not changed since the last visit.
* Finally, do not assume transgender patients are gay, bisexual or lesbian.

Communication with LGBT patients in a manner that is sensitive is not difficult if you listen to how your patients describe themselves and their partners, and then follow their lead. If in doubt on how to refer to your patient’s sexual identity or partner, ask the patient what terms they prefer. Most of the time, you can be curious without offending patients.

Sometimes people use terms for themselves that sound derogatory if used by an outside source. Again, if you are unsure if your patients would be comfortable with you using this language, ask the patient.

If you do make a mistake and/or the patient seems offended, apologizeand ask the patient what they prefer. Most patients will appreciate your sincerity and good intentions.
