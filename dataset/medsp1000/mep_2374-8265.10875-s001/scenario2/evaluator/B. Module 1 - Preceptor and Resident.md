**CASE TWO:**

Shawn is a 19-year-old man whom you are seeing in a free clinic, which services a nearby homeless shelter for youth. He has not seen a physician in about three years since his 15year old well child check, and he has been homeless for about two years. He reports that he identifies as male and has always felt he expressed himself as a male in terms of his athletic clothes, tribal tattoos and sporty haircut. He reports to you that he has been intimate with both men and women over the last two years with intermittent barrier protection, and now thinks he prefers to be intimate with men, as he finds them more attractive. Once he shared with his family his sexual orientation, he no longer felt welcome at home, and subsequently moved out. Although his family has not welcomed him, he is comfortable with his decision to be in relationships with men.

**4. According to the American Association of Medical Colleges, the human experience has been described as consisting of sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression and sexual behaviors. What is meant by the terms Gender Expression and Gender Identity?**

Definitions

* **Gender identity** is described as a person's internal sense of their own gender. Examples of gender identity can include:
  + *Cisgender* (natal sex concordant with internal sense of gender)
  + *Transgender* (natal sex discordant with internal sense of gender).
* **Gender expression** is defined as the spectrum of masculine and feminine characteristics in how one dresses, acts or presents his/herself.8 Gender expression can be masculine, feminine, or somewhere in between.
  + *Gender nonconforming* refers to people whose gender expression does not conform to society’s norm, examples can be women who wear men style of clothes, hairstyles, or men who wear more feminine types of make-up, nails, clothing.9

Gender identity and expression can be closely tied with the effect of sexuality on health, not necessarily in how the individuals present themselves, but in how the community responds9. For example, in a 2013 high school study, 74% of LBGT students reported harassment due to their orientation, and 55% due to their gender expression. 10

**5. How does Shawn seem to identify in terms of his Gender Identity vs. his Gender Expression? Is his Gender Identity cis or transgender?**

In Shawn's case, his **Gender Identity** is a male, **gender expression** is gender conforming/masculine, and his biological sex is male. As his gender identity and biological sex are the same, this is **cis-gender** (as opposed to transgender).

**6. What are the differences between sexual behavior and sexual orientation? What is Shawn’s sexual behavior and sexual orientation?**

Definitions

* **Sexual orientation** is based on how a person identifies attraction on a physical and emotional basis9. Sexologists recognize sexual orientation as referring to core erotic arousal. However descriptive terminology often may not give a full picture.
  + Sexual orientation can be described by many terms, including but not limited to: Lesbian/Gay, Bisexual, Queer, Straight, Pansexual, and Asexual11.
* **Sexual behavior** is based on a person’s sexual practices

Sexual behavior and sexual orientation do not always align and can evolve over time. For example, a man who does not self-identify as gay, may at times be intimate with men, or a woman who identifies as lesbian may have had both female and male partners11.

Sexual orientation is not a choice, but a person’s self-awareness and acceptance can be influenced by many factors including cultural environment, stage of development, and beliefs9.

**7. How would you categorize Shawn’s sexual orientation and sexual behavior?**

Shawn’s self-described sexual orientation is gay, as he finds himself attracted primarily to men. Though he was previously intimate with both men and women, his sexual behavior now would be described as MSM or men who have sex with men.

**8. How can PCPs best clarify these topics with their patients, and why is it important to cover all aspects of sexuality in history taking?**

These issues are best discussed with patients directly. Many patients aren't asked about their orientation by their physician, but this is key to understanding the health issues that can specifically affect the LGBT population. It is also important to clarify with patients their gender history, sexual practices, and preferred terminology. As demonstrated above, asking only one component of the sexuality history could give a very incomplete picture for a provider. Incorporating a patients’ entire gender history and sexual practices is essential to ensuring they are receiving all recommended screenings, as will be discussed in detail later. Knowing sexual practices for all patients will also allow providers to determine if they qualify for pre-exposure HIV prophylaxis (PrEP) based on the CDC guidelines for individuals at risk for HIV infection.16

In summary, it is best to be respectful and just ask.

**9. What are some common barriers to healthcare for LBGT patient?**

Barriers to healthcare arise from widespread individual and systemic discrimination. In addition to overt discrimination, subtle forms often exist. There improvements being made from a legal perspective, as well as education of providers, however further interventions that create equal access are needed.

According to the AAMC, many LGBT individuals, especially those who are transgender, look for clues when they arrive to healthcare offices: such as

* posted non-discrimination policies
* inclusive forms, as formats are frequently exclusive of gender and sexual minority experiences, which can discourage disclosure of gender identity, sexual orientation and behavior12
* interactions with staff
* bathrooms that are not gender specific or single user/gender neutral11.

Furthermore, LGBT individuals are twice as likely as others to lack insurance, and often (though this is changing) insurance plans may not include benefits for same sex partners, nor cover medical care to support gender transition, or fertility options for same sex couples, or other population specific treatments such as gender-affirming therapies11.

Poverty also disproportionately affects the LBGT population. For example, transgender individuals are 4x more likely than the general population to have a household income less than $10,0009.

All these factors can lead to delays in diagnosis and suboptimal care. In module 2, we will explore use of inclusive language.

**12. Why is knowledge of these barriers so essential?**

Sensitivity, education and inclusive policies are key areas of improvement needed to improve accessibility to healthcare for all patients, specifically members of the LGBT community or individuals who are gender nonconforming.

Improving access to healthcare for the LGBT population is essential due to the associated physical and mental health issues as well as higher rates of substance abuse, eating disorders, intimate partner violence, mental health issues and suicide attempts.

As providers, it is our role to improve accessibility to the healthcare system and ensure that all patients feel welcome, but especially those individuals who can still be marginalized by society. The National LGBT Health Education Center has identified several opportunities to create a welcome environment and decrease barriers for people who are LGBT including “LGTB affirmative training for staff, implementing LGBT inclusive practices, forms and data collection, incorporating LGBT health needs into clinical services; and reflecting the LGBT community through representation in both the physical environment and the workforce.” 17

To begin this process, we must first start with knowledge. This module was designed to provide the framework upon which to continue further building that knowledge in the subsequent modules in this curriculum.
