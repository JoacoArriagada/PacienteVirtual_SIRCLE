**Optimal Primary Care for LGBT Patients**

**Module 2: Cultural competencies; performing a sensitive history and physical exam**

**Learning Objectives:**

1. Elicit relevant information about sexual history, sexual practices, and gender history in preferred patient terminology.
2. Implement basic gender-neutral practices in ambulatory clinics.
3. Recognize that implicit bias about sexuality, gender, and sex anatomy may adversely affect communication.
4. Engage in effective self-reflection processes to diminish the effects of implicit bias.
5. Conduct a sensitive physical exam using gender-affirming terminology.

**CASE THREE:**

You are assigned a new patient who has established care with your clinic. Jordan is a 30-year-old patient beginning graduate school looking for a medication refill. On your standard admission paperwork, you notice that the patient has not selected "M" or "F" on the form. Past medical history is notable for asthma and current medications include albuterol, spironolactone, and monthly estradiol valerate injections. The patient is roomed, and review of vitals includes height of 5'7" and weight 174 lbs., BP 132/78, and HR 74. You enter to find Jordan is Caucasian with medium length hair, and baggy clothing.

1. **How would you ask Jordan about gender identity?**

Key messages

Asking open ended questions allows for the patient to identify preferences. You should have the patient describe how he or she would like to be addressed. It is acceptable to clarify for the sake of understanding.

Pronoun use should be tailored to the person's gender identity. For example, with Jordan identifying as a woman, the correct pronouns should be "she" and "her."

**CASE THREE continued:**

Jordan reveals that she identifies as a woman. She reports feeling "trapped in the wrong body" since she was in elementary school. While born a male, she reports that she began transitioning to a female when she was in college. She struggled with depression during this period, but for the past 5 years has been meeting with a therapist and describes her mood as good. She is beginning graduate school in psychology. She lives in an apartment with a supportive partner. She denies tobacco or alcohol use.

1. **What are some words you might use to elicit information about her partner?**

Since sexuality and gender identity are distinct, it is prudent to establish an understanding of the patient’s sexual orientation and sexual behaviors to allow for directed screening and contraceptive management.

Again, open ended questions will elicit the most information. Rather than asking a patient: “Are you married?” or “Do you have a boyfriend?” consider asking “What is your current relationship status?” or “Who are the important people in your life?” or even “Tell me more about your partner.”

This method of questioning provides an open environment and prevents assumptions that may hinder the therapeutic alliance.

1. **How would you approach the physical exam?**

You could appropriately begin by asking for permission and whether the patient wants anyone in the exam room with her. The patient might not want a physical exam.

**Preceptor: Would you perform a genital exam?**

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends against screening for testicular cancer in adult males. It would not be necessary to conduct a genital exam in this case.

Overall, using gender affirming language, which would entail saying “chest” instead of “breasts” or “genitals” and “private parts” instead of specific anatomic parts, such as “uterus, cervix or penis,” would be recommended.

Draping and examining one organ/part at a time should also be standardpractice.

In the case of a female genital exam, if a patient would like to insert the speculum themselves, you could also guide them in speculum insertion.

**CASE THREE continued:**

Jordan is in a relationship with a male who is aware of her transition. Screening for intimate partner violence is negative. The remainder of the history and physical exam are performed. The patient has not undergone gender affirming surgeries. She is happy with her breast size which has increased since taking estrogen. After age-appropriate vaccinations, the annual exam is complete. She asks, "Oh by the way, can you refill my estrogen and spironolactone? I have never been off of them and would hate to start seeing negative effects. I have an appointment with an endocrinologist in 3 weeks.”

1. **As a provider unfamiliar with transgender care, how would you respond to her request?**

Depending on scope of practice and location, a provider may be asked to refill medications, particularly in transitions of care. While this does largely depend on the provider’s comfort and knowledge of drug monitoring and side effects, writing a prescription to bridge a patient to a new endocrinologist would be an appropriate measure, especially with stable dosing and no titration of medication.

If you are interested in broadening your scope of practice to include gender-affirming hormone therapy for transgender persons, we invite you to download and use the Endocrine Society's Guidelines and Clinical Practice Mobile App, which provides an evidence-based and easy-to-access treatment guide at your fingertips. It includes updated recommendations for hormone dosing regimens and monitoring protocols as well as information on criteria for and effects of hormone therapy.

Many pharmaceutical agents are used off label and individual insurance plans may vary in terms of compensation. Regardless of role in prescribing these medications, a physician’s duty is to ensure that the patient does have options and access to providers who can continue to support their transition.

1. **What are ways to promote gender affirmation in the clinical setting?**

As a healthcare provider, basic understanding of barriers to inclusion of the LGBT community should be built into your practice setting.

Easy ways to ensure a patient population feels accepted begins with sensitively and openly identifying issues with open ended questions about gender and sexuality in neutral language.

Single stall restrooms in the clinic should be gender neutral or “all-gender” if possible.

Intake forms should not have “M” or “F” but rather be inclusive of individuals who identify as transgender.
