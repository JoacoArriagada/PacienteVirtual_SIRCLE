II. Case Objectives

a. Demonstrate recognition of the clinical diagnosis of ventricular fibrillation (VF)   
b. Demonstrate knowledge of how to treat VF, including provision of quality CPR before and between defibrillation attempts   
c. Demonstrate knowledge of how and when to use supplementary medications (e.g. epinephrine) during the treatment of VF

III. Conceptual Background

a. Given the growing amount of evidence that Pediatric residents may not be sufficiently prepared to carry out advanced life support resuscitations in accordance with published guidelines, this case is intended to provide instructors with a case suitable for mannequin or computer screen-based simulation, that could provide pediatric health care workers with supplementary training in ventricular fibrillation management. The Anesoft Pediatric Advanced Life Support Simulator was developed to provide pediatric advanced life support providers with on-demand, instructor-free, interactive training in how to conduct pediatric resuscitations in accordance with American Heart Association guidelines. In our institution, the majority of providers who regularly undergo teambased resuscitation training in the full-scale mannequin simulator laboratory use these interactive computer scenarios to prepare for their team training.

b. Performance expectations/anticipated management mistakes (Reference: Part 12: Pediatric Advanced Life Support. Circulation 2005;112; IV-167-IV-187).

i. Many providers fail to provide CPR before attempting defibrillation. Although this particular scenario occurs in an inpatient setting, it was essentially unwitnessed, making it preferable for providers to administer CPR immediately, while setting up for defibrillation. In theory, it may be reasonable to consider immediate defibrillation if one directly witnesses this kind of an arrest, in a patient with a cardiac history. However, on an inpatient hospital ward (non-ICU environment) it often takes several minutes to assemble adequate help and administer immediate defibrillation within a timeframe that will not compromise the patient’s prospects for arrest survival.

ii. Many providers fail to provide sufficient CPR between defibrillation attempts in patients with ongoing VF. Two minutes (or 5 cycles) of CPR should be administered between defibrillation attempts. The pulse should be monitored during CPR to verify adequacy of compressions, and also during CPR pauses, while the cardiac rhythm is evaluated.

iii. Many providers give epinephrine after the first defibrillation attempt. While it is not known whether this would create a survival disadvantage, current American Heart Association recommendations call for administration of epinephrine after the second defibrillation attempt. Resuscitation teams should take care not to distract themselves from providing high quality, uninterrupted CPR when called for in the treatment algorithm.

iv. Many providers fail to assess for the presence of a pulse in a patient with a narrow complex rhythm (after $3 ^ { \mathsf { r d } }$ defibrillation attempt)

v. CPR and electrical defibrillation are the keys to successful treatment of ventricular fibrillation. During the resuscitation, it is essential to frequently reassess the patient’s response to therapy. If the patient does not respond to defibrillation attempts, you must verify adequacy of CPR and appropriateness of defibrillation dosing, as well as making sure equipment is functioning normally.

You must also address the “5 H’s and 5 T’s”: Hypovolemia, hypoxia, hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hyper/hypokalemia, hypothermia, Tablets (drugs), Tamponade, Tension pneumothorax, Thrombosis (coronary), and Thrombosis (pulmonary).

vi. The recommended concentration of epinephrine in this case 1:10,000

VI. Limitations

a. This case scenario does not test knowledge of the full VF algorithm (including antiarrythmic administration). With minimal revision, the scenario can be altered to incorporate the administration of amiodarone.
