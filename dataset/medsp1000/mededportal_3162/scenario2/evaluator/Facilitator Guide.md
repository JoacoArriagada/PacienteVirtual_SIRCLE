# GENERAL SCENARIOS

2. Hillary Dee is a 54-year-old housewife from Evans, GA. Mrs. Dee scheduled an appointment with her family physician because of problems sleeping over the past 6 months. She describes waking up at night because of a “kind of burning, painful numbness” in the soles of her feet. While talking to the doctor, she is wringing her hands.


# DATA COLLECTION

Activities: Color code BLUE

# 4a. Refining Symptom Descriptions and Patient Predicaments. (In groups of 3 students, 30 minutes)

Purpose. In this activity, the purpose is to use empathy to gain an understanding of the context, timeline, meaning, and details of the precipitating event, and also to obtain detailed information about symptoms. Understanding the context of the patient’s life and the meaning of the symptom as it effects the patient’s life aids the doctor in getting the information needed to make an accurate diagnosis. Students will expand their interviewing skills by practicing empathic skills within the structure of the medical interview. Clarification of symptoms is increased through empathic responses and gathering of descriptive data in a structured format.

# Coach Tips.

• Be sure to emphasize that technical “jargon” can derail the interview.   
Have students give a sign post when starting to ask a series of rapid fire questions in the technical ‘subroutine.”   
• The purpose of this activity is to remember that empathy still exists in the gathering of specific data.

Description. Students will rotate in the “patient,” “doctor,” and observer roles. The “patient” will role-play a medical problem (provided). “Doctors” will get the “patient’s” story and be able to name the Chief Complaint (CC), obtain a History of Present Illness (HPI) and related Past Medical History (PMH) including social and family history.

1. The “doctor” should get the STORY. Use empathy to understand the patient’s feelings, the context and the personal meaning of the problem for the patient. IMPORTANT: The “story” is more important than all the details of the technical subroutine.

2. Clarify the timeline and all symptom details.

3. Practice the “technical” subroutine. Use the mnemonic OLDCARTS (onset, location, duration, character, aggravating/associated factors, relieving factors, temporal factors, severity) to clarify symptom descriptions. Be careful to avoid bombarding the “patient” with question after question. Use the skills learned to this point to obtain this information in an open-ended fashion. Observers will keep track of OLDCARTS items, using provided tally sheets.

At the end of the interview, the “doctor” retells the story—including facts and symptom details as well as the meaning for the “patient”—to the observer, “patient,” and coach. The “doctor” then receives feedback from them about what was done well and what could be done differently—to correct details, suggest other approaches to obtaining the patient’s story, suggest moments in the interview when empathic responses/approaches would have been appropriate, and to coach the “doctor” on the interview elements of CC, HPI, and related PMH.

Materials. For this activity, you will need the symptom scenarios and the OLDCARTS section of the tally sheet.

Discussion. Discuss how empathy helps in the development of the patient-physician relationship. How does empathy contribute to the “doctor’s” ability to get the facts and details? What did the “doctor” learn from the “patient’s” nonverbal behavior?

# 4b. Using Empathy to Verify the Understanding of the Symptom. (In groups of 3 students, 30 minutes)

Purpose. Students often forget that symptoms have meaning to the patient beyond simply the description in OLDCARTS.   
This activity emphasizes the need to understand the underlying life implications of the symptom for the patient.

# Coach Tips.

• One medical school reported sending students into 3rd grade classrooms to practice ex plaining medical information using words 8 year olds could understand. Practice letting the patient story lead into each of the OLDCARTS answers. How does that feel as an interviewer?

Description. Rotating the roles and the problems of the same stories, students will again walk through the collection of the symptom information. This time they should focus on the beliefs, concerns, and feelings of the patient about this symptom experience. How will it influence their life? What are they really worried about? What is the implication for their family? Are there gender, age, spiritual, economic issues that could arise?

Conduct as in Activity 4a. At the end of the interview, the “doctor” discusses the potential daily implications of this patient’s story to the other group members. He receives feedback from them about other ramifications of the story.

Materials. For this activity, you will need symptom scenarios and the OLDCARTS section of the tally sheet.

Discussion. Discuss how empathy and the shift in focus from the “technical” data gathering to the “story” gathering change the emphasis? How may this play out in the therapeutics for this patient?

4c. Middle of the Cone. (In groups of 3 students, 30 minutes)

Purpose. This activity will help students understand the concept of a cone model, moving from a very broad and general event to the specifics of feelings/emotions, symptoms, and/or fears of the patient surrounding the event.

Description. In this activity, students will once again rotate through the roles and symptom scenarios as in the last two activities. For this activity though, students can choose to either walk step-by-step with the “patient” through a symptomatic episode or walk step-by-step through the person’s day to get the “patient’s” story. If the “doctor” chooses to go step-by-step through a symptomatic episode, then he/she needs to go through using open questions and the OLDCARTS to find out what symptoms the “patient” had, what thought/feelings/emotions, what fears, what treatments, what the timeline was from the beginning to the end of the episode. For the step-by-step through the day, the “doctor” needs to begin with the night before and go through untill the appointment time using open then more closed ended questions. The “doctor” needs to be especially aware of symptoms, medications, timing of everything, food, etc.

Materials. For this activity, you will need the symptom scenarios and the OLDCARTS section of the tally sheet.

Discussion. Discuss how using the cone effect of going from the very broad idea of a symptom or disease to the minute details of an event or day helps the “doctor” understand the “patient’s” story and condition better. Also discuss the difference and similarities of using the step-by-step through the day or the symptomatic episode.



6b. Practicing Medical Interviews. (In groups of 3 students, 90 minutes).

Purpose. The purpose of this session is to practice a “first” medical interview, in preparation for the recorded interview of a Standardized Patient. Students will practice with a Standardized Patient (SP) going through opening, engagement and empathy-building aspects and gather Chief Complaint, History of the Present Illness, Family, Social and Past Medical History to the extent that time allows.

Description. Each SP has a scenario to role play which includes emotional aspects of the illness and suggestions for non-verbal behaviors. They will be joined and interviewed by a “doctor.” Observers will take notes and use the Interview Behavior Skills Checklist to gather data for the “doctor” being observed. Both “patients” and “doctors” will use non-verbal behaviors to convey status of emotion. Each student will interview a different SP. The remaining students and faculty will serve as “coaches” interrupting periodically with suggestions of different strategies to try. SPs will then go backward in the interview and begin again (rewound) so that the student can practice the suggested strategy. At the end of each interview, SPs will be rotated among exam rooms.

Materials. For this activity, you will need the Interview Behavior Skills Checklist, located in both the student pocket books as well as in the Facilitator Guide, and the SP scenarios. You will also need several SPs.

Discussion. Each “doctor” will first assess what strategies worked well and what he/she might do differently at this point. “Doctors,” in conjunction with their peer and faculty coaches, should identify areas they need to practice before Communication Lab Session 4. SPs will next provide feedback about their reactions to the “doctor” including perceptions based on “doctors’” non-verbal behavior. This will be followed by comments and suggestions from the coach.

Students may want to review their texts, the handout on the Medical History in notebooks and other medical interviewing resources prior to next activity.

6c. Celebrating Successes (individual interviews and 6 student debriefings, 120 minutes).

Purpose. The purpose of this session is to put it all together in terms of the opening, negotiating the connection, the technical subroutine of data gathering, and the basic medical interviewing structure and to practice those skills with live patients.

Description. This entire session will be spent with mock patient interviews. Each medical student will act as a physician for a 5 - 7 minute interview that will be videotaped. Feedback will occur during playback. (Allot 10 minutes per student $= 3 0$ minutes).

The objective in the interview is not to diagnose and treat but simply to connect with the patient to establish rapport and enjoy the patient’s story, to practice some of the techniques we have learned and to really listen and hear what the patient says. Students should move through the Chief Complaint and HPI parts of the medical interview to the extent possible in 5 - 7 minutes. Feel free to stop the interview if you’ve gotten stuck and begin again or add a different skill. This session is to learn, practice, and try some new communication techniques. We realize it is always difficult to receive feedback but it truly is the best way to practice communication skills.

The Interview Behavior Skills Checklist will be used by Standardized Patients following the live session. These may be useful for reference during the feedback phase. The Checklists will be given to “doctors” at the end of the feedback session.

Materials. For this activity, you will the SP scenarios and multiple SPs, the Interview Behavior Skills Checklist, and the DVD/tape recording of the student interviews to playback during the debriefing session.

Discussion. (10 - 15 minutes per student $=$ 60 - 90 minutes) PLEASE NOTE: SCHEDULES ARE TYPICALLY TIGHT WITH THIS ACTIVITY. During DVD playback and feedback time, medical students will first evaluate themselves by addressing the following questions:

1. What did you do well?   
2. What might you do differently?   
3. What would you say are the cares, predicaments, laments of your patient?   
4. What thoughts and emotions did you have as you interviewed the patient?

Following the student’s comments, the Standardized Patient will give feedback to the “doctor” addressing specifically engagement and empathy skills. What made them comfortable? How connected did they feel to the “doctor?” Did they feel as if they would be willing to share personal information and details with this person? Did they feel cared for?

Observing medical students will also give feedback related to what they thought was done well and what they might suggest the “doctor” might have tried. Coaches will address any issues that may have been left out.
