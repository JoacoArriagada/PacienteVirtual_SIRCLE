**Subgroup 3**

Michel is a 2 month old male born to Moroccan immigrant parents. His mother did not seek prenatal care prior to his delivery in the local hospital. His newborn screen was abnormal and he was placed on prophylactic penicillin by his primary care doctor during the first month of life while awaiting his formal hematology evaluation. He had prolonged neonatal jaundice that was treated with home UV light blankets and attributed at the time to breastfeeding. He has since been growing and feeding well, without any unusual problems. His physical exam is unremarkable, and his lab results are attached for your review. Both parents are well and deny any known problems with blood in the family. Review his labs and use the website cited at the beginning of the case to deduct his diagnosis. Then, use the resource of your choosing to research the following:

1. What defect (s) does he have in hemoglobin production?
2. What is the biochemical basis for this disorder(s)?
3. What is a retic count? Why is it elevated in this patient?
4. What clinical manifestations/problems can be expected? How do these change over the course of life? How do these compare with sickle cell disease?
5. Why was this infant placed on prophylactic penicillin? How long will he need to take it?
6. Should the parents undergo any further testing prior to another pregnancy?
7. What is the likelihood of having a similarly affected sibling?

Your group should be prepared to give an overview of the biochemical basis for this disease and the general genetic abnormality involved, as well as the answers to the above clinical questions, in your ICC session. Make sure you cite your references! You will then explain the diagnosis and its significance to an SP posing as the parent of this child. The remainder of the group and your faculty mentor will watch your interaction with the SP parent and offer feedback on the way you explain the infant’s test results and their significance. You will also receive feedback from the SP on the interaction.

**This patient has Hemoglobin SC Disease. That means he has one gene for sickle cell anemia and one gene for Hemoglobin C, an abnormal hemoglobin that in the homozygous state results in mild, chronic hemolytic anemia and splenomegaly. The main clinical importance for Hemoglobin C lies in the high numbers of people in West Africa who carry the gene and its clinical similarity to Sickle Cell Anemia when paired with a single sickle cell hemoglobin gene, as in this patient. Clinically, these patients retain the same vulnerability to infection by encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus, haemophilus, meningococci) and must be placed on prophylactic penicillin for the first 5 years of life,** with 23-valent pneumococcal vaccination at age 2 years and then every 5 years thereafter due to splenic dysfunction. They have chronic hemolytic anemia with splenomegaly developing in childhood and persisting into adulthood, are prone to splenic sequestration, acute chest, pain crises, acute chest syndrome, priapism and stroke. Renal papillary necrosis, sickle retinopathy, and aseptic necrosis of the femoral head are also common complications in these patients. In general, SC disease patients are more susceptible to the complications of sickling as the presence of SC in the cell promotes a higher percentage of hemoglobin in the red blood cell and this a greater degree of sickling than SS alone. More information about this disease is available in the article in Appendix I, but your students should be playing the role of teacher for the group on this patient.**

<u>**SP information for portraying a parent of this infant:**</u>

**Your baby has been normal and fine since he got over his jaundice; although you have been worried because your pediatrician told you he has a blood disease and will have to take medicine to prevent infections. You do not know what other problems the baby might have down the road—the pediatrician said you needed to wait to hear what the hematologist said, so you are pretty nervous. You have a lot of questions for the doctor.**

1. **What is the meaning of the abnormal test my baby had? It means he has Hemoglobin SC disease.**
2. **What is Hemoglobin SC Disease? A genetic problem with producing red blood cells that can cause many lifelong problems. Your baby inherited one gene for Sickle cell anemia and one gene for a different kind of anemia called Hemoglobin C disease. This means that he will have some problems associated with both diseases. Red blood cells carry oxygen to all parts of the body, so a disease that affects these cells can affect the whole body.**
3. **Will he have any medical problems as a result of this? Yes. The most immediate problem is that he cannot fight some kinds of bacterial infections, which is why he is on an antibiotic every day to prevent infection. He will need to stay on this antibiotic until he is at least five years old. He may also have problems with the abnormal red blood cells getting stuck in his veins, which can cause organ damage in the lungs, eyes, kidneys, brain, and penis. Babies with this disease need to be brought to the doctor’s office more frequently for checkups, and have to be seen by a doctor every time they have a cough, cold, or fever, or if they seem fussier than usual to make sure they do not have a more serious infection. He will also have to be seen by specialists periodically to watch for evidence of organ damage due to his disease.**
4. **Does he need any more tests? He should have his blood tested to decrease the chance of reactions to blood transfusions in the future. This makes it possible to more closely match his blood type (Extended Phenotype Blood Matching) to prevent problems in the future. He may be someone who needs lots of blood transfusions, so minimizing the risks of blood transfusion reactions is important. (This is a sophisticated point of care that some students may not get from their reading, so it’s OK if this is not covered.)**
5. **Could I have another baby with this problem or with worse problems? Yes. Your future children have a 25% chance of having the same problem as Michel. To determine the chances of your children having other problems with red blood cells, you should be tested to see if there are any other problems you may carry in your hemoglobin genes (thalassemia.) If all your other genes are normal, you have a 25% chance of having a baby with normal genes, a 25% chance of having a baby that is a carrier for Hemoglobin C disease, and a 25% chance of having a baby who is a carrier for Sickle Cell disease.**
