**Subgroup I Case Description:**

Micah is a 4 month old male. He is Mom and Dad’s only child. During prenatal testing, mom was found to be mildly anemic. (“Low blood”) Micah had an abnormal newborn screen for hemoglobinopathies and the attached follow-up hemoglobin electrophoresis was done at 4 weeks of age. Based on these results, he was referred to the hematologist for further evaluation. (The first available appointment was at age 4 months). His CBC (complete blood count) at 4 months is shown. Make sure you compare it to normal values for age from <u>The Harriet Lane Handbook</u>, which are provided as part of this case. Since birth, he has been growing and gaining weight well without any unusual illnesses. He has no family history of any severe anemia’s, sickle cell, or other known blood disorders. Mom is African American and Dad is Asian in heritage.

**This baby has a result of FAV on his newborn screen. This denotes 3 different types of hemoglobin, listed in order from highest to lowest quantity. This means he had 3 different forms of hemoglobin detected (normal is FA.) The types of hemoglobin detected are F=Fetal, A=Hemoglobin A (both of which are normal) and V=Variant. When the variant hemoglobin was examined, it was found to be Hemoglobin Bart’s, which is a tetramer of gamma globin chains seen during the newborn period. Fetal hemoglobin is made up of alpha and gamma globin chains, and since not enough alpha chains are produced in utero, the extra gamma globin chains form tetramers. These tetramers are called Hemoglobin Bart’s (named after St. Bartholomew’s Hospital in London where they were discovered.) As gamma chain synthesis stops after the birth of the baby, Hgb Bart’s is only transiently seen in the newborn period. Its presence indicates that one or more of the four genes that produce alpha globin chains are dysfunctional, causing alpha thalassemia. The more alpha genes affected, the more significant the thalassemia and clinical symptoms, and the greater the amount of Hgb Bart’s on follow-up hemoglobin electrophoresis.**

<u>**SP information for portraying a parent of this infant:**</u>

**Your baby has been normal and fine. Your pediatrician sent you to the hematologist due to an abnormality on the labs that are drawn on all babies born in the state. You are not overly worried, because your pediatrician says that if the baby had a serious blood problem he would be having symptoms and problems he hasn’t had. You are mostly interested in knowing the following:**

1. **What is the meaning of the abnormal test my baby had? (it means he probably has a form of alpha-thalassemia.)**
2. **What is alpha-thalassemia?(A genetic problem with producing red blood cells that ranges from completely asymptomatic, like your baby, to severe life threatening anemia, which your baby does not have.)**
3. **Will he have any medical problems as a result of this? (Probably not. He may have mild anemia with small red blood cells, depending on the results of testing that cannot be done until he is 9-12 months old. If he has mild anemia, the most important thing is for him to tell his doctor that he has a form of alpha thalassemia to avoid unnecessary iron dosing for presumed iron deficiency anemia)**
4. **Does he need any more tests? Yes, he needs follow-up blood tests at age 9 months.**
5. **Why can’t we get  Micah’s follow-up testing done now? Babies make a certain kind of hemoglobin when they are inside the mother that changes over to normal adult hemoglobin during the first year of life, then stays the same for the rest of their life. We have to wait for Micah’s bone marrow to complete this change over to the adult pattern to see which form of alpha thalassemia he may have.**
6. **Could I have another baby with this problem or with worse problems? Yes. If this infant has thalassemia genes, with a history of Dad’s Asian heritage, it could be possible to have an infant with more significant anemia. If alpha thalassemia is confirmed, testing of mom and dad if indicated to ensure there are no more abnormal hemoglobin genes. Micah himself also needs to pursue genetic counseling prior to procreating, to prevent a more severe form of alpha-thalassemia in his offspring.**
