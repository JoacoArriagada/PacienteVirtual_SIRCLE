# 1. **ADULT CASE SUMMARY**

# **Life-Sustaining Treatment: Ben/Benita Ward**

**Patient Information: Ben/Benita Ward**

**Your Role:**

You are a medicine intern. You are on call and admitted a patient with a chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation. While presenting the case to the senior resident, she asks you about the patient’s “code status.” You have not had that conversation with the patient yet, so you have to go back and discuss it with them. It’s been a busy day and you only had about 30 minutes to spend with Mr./Ms. Ward previously.

**Situation:**

Mr./Ms. Ward is a 65-year-old with moderate-severe COPD who was admitted for the 2nd time in nine months with a COPD exacerbation. This is the patient’s fifth admission overall for COPD. He/she developed worsening shortness of breath, and increased volume of sputum production over the past four days. History, exam, and chest radiograph are consistent with a COPD exacerbation. You started the patient on methylprednisolone, doxycycline, and bronchodilators. He/she is starting to feel better.

Mr./Ms. Ward first developed dyspnea on exertion (DOE) about 10 years ago and was diagnosed with COPD. He/she smoked two packs per day for 45 years but quit two years ago. The patient reports that their DOE has worsened every year. They can walk up a flight of stairs but is very winded at the top and stops to rest and coughs daily. The patient does not require oxygen. They have continued to work, but it is getting harder to do so.

You think Mr./Ms. Ward will turn around in the next 48 hours. However, last month you had a patient who came in with a congestive heart failure exacerbation. You thought the same thing about her, but then she had a cardiac arrest and underwent cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) before you could talk to her about her preferences. She survived but spent weeks in the intensive care unit (ICU) and suffered hypoxic encephalopathy.

Mr./Ms. Ward has a forced expiratory volume1 (FEV1) that is 30% of predicted value and has a body mass index greater than 21. If asked, would rate themself with mild-moderate dyspnea on validated scales, and has somewhat limited ability to walk a long distance in six minutes. He/she has never been intubated and does not require chronic steroids. While it is difficult to predict mortality in COPD, based on some prognostic models, his/her risk of death from any cause in the next 3 years is 10-25%.

You know that if Mr./Ms. Ward has a cardiac arrest in the hospital, his/her chance of surviving to discharge is about 15% and the chance of being alive at 12 months is about 10%. Given his/her COPD, it is less clear, if intubated, whether he/she would be able to come off the ventilator. Other than death, there are other significant risks associated with mechanical ventilation and CPR, including debility/functional impairment that does not improve, brain damage, and a host of other medical complications (infection, pressure ulcers, etc.).

PMH: COPD (FEV1 0.9L), hypertension

Home Meds: tiotropium, fluticasone, albuterol, hydrochlorothiazide

Records from the patient’s primary care physician indicate they were concerned about his/her oxygen saturation with exercise, even when at baseline, and there are no documented advance directives.

Family Hx: both parents died from myocardial infarctions

Social Hx: married, lives with spouse in Arbutus. Has two children and two grandchildren. Works as a delivery person for a linen service that supplies local hospitals and nursing homes. Completed the 11th grade. Quit smoking two years ago.

**Your Task:**

Discuss his/her “code status” or preferences for life sustaining treatment.
