# OSTE Ward Rounds Case: Pain Management Standardized Learner Instructions -- Resident

# The Scenario

Mrs. Garcia is a 68-year-old woman with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, depression, and chronic low back pain who was admitted for evaluation of chest pain six days ago. She presented to the emergency room complaining of three days of a new burning chest pain associated with anxiety - her son lost his job. Her cardiac work up was negative (troponins neg x 3, ECG no acute changes, just some LVH (left ventricle hypertrophy)). She even had a CT angiogram in the ER because she had some SOB - negative for PE or dissection. Her chest pain responded to omeprazole and your diagnosis is GERD (Gastroesophageal reflux).

She has had back pain for years: low back tightness and pain that radiates to her buttock. At home she manages it with Tylenol and gabapentin; at base line her pain in 5/10 but she has flares to 9/10 pain once or twice per week. She had an LS spine MRI 4 months ago which was significant from multilevel DJD and mild spinal stenosis, and it was not significantly changed from an MRI she had three years ago. Over the past month her pain became worse (7/10), and she started taking Motrin, which helped "a little."

Since being in the hospital she has had 9/10 back pain. She reports it became worse the night she was admitted and had to stay in the ER on a gurney for four hours awaiting a bed upstairs. She describes her 9/10 pain as a tightness across her low back that radiates to her buttock. It is better when she lays still and worse when she tries to move, change position, or walk. She denies bowel or bladder incontinence, lower leg pain, or weakness.

You initially changed her Tylenol to Tylenol with Codeine 1 TID PRN, but her pain was still 9/10. Three days ago you changed her T-3 to Percocet 1 tab QID prn thinking she would improve with the stronger medication. You view Percocet as your "go to" medication for managing pain on the wards. It is sufficiently strong to treat most pain, but short-acting so you won't get into trouble with long-lasting side effects. Her pain initially improved to 5/10 but she began having breakthrough pain again. She has been asking for additional Percocet and for the past two nights night float has been called regarding her pain.

# The Scenario, continued

When you examined her this morning she was lying in bed complaining of moderate pain but she had just received her Percocet. She complained of pain when you asked her to sit up, but she was able to do so without assistance. She needed help standing and was able to walk across her room using the walker, but complained of severe pain and soon asked to get back into bed.

# Personality

You are a 3rd year resident and are planning to go into GI. You have done well on the wards and feel fairly confident in your ability to manage a ward team. You like to teach and feel like there are certain topics you have become good at (anemia, GERD, colon cancer screening, hepatitis). You tend to be a bit “hands off” and let the interns manage issues they feel comfortable managing. You think your intern is doing a good job overall but think he may have a confidence problem. You have been encouraging him to make more decisions. You trust that he will come to you with questions.

You view attending rounds as a time that the attending teaches the intern. You don’t think the focus should be on you, and you often will check your email, and check out a bit.

A couple months ago you were on the Bellevue wards and a patient you started on morphine left the hospital and fell at home, breaking a hip. The patient said he was too “out of it” from the pain meds and just fell. You felt terrible and have avoided starting long-acting pain meds, because you feel you need to be able to monitor patients closely. You don't want to start Mrs. Garcia on a long-acting medication, as she lives alone and will be going home in a few days.

Timing Beginning

Listen to the intern's presentation. Don't say anything about his management or choice of medications until the attending brings up a concern.

Middle

If the attending asks why you placed her on PRN meds: Say “She was fine asking for medications initially. We can certainly convert her to a standing dose; we just didn't want to make her too sleepy."

If asked about the use of long acting pain meds: Say you know she should be on a long- acting pain med, but you feel it is important for her to have really close follow-up and she is being discharged in a couple days. If you feel comfortable, tell your story. (See above.)

If asked about the type of pain: Say “We discussed this and we think she has both nociceptive and neuropathic pain, a combination of muscle spasm and radiculopathy from djd."

If asked about treating her neuropathic pain: Say she is on gabapentin and “I guess we can increase it - but that will take weeks to work.”

Timing, continued

If asked about treating her nociceptive pain: Say the Tylenol isn’t enough and that she needs a narcotic. She should be on longacting medication, but you feel that is something for her PCP to manage.

End

If the attending does a good job leading the discussion, say “that was so helpful.” You are more willing to start long-acting pain medication and appreciate hearing her/his approach to pain management. You are going to make sure you speak to your team about how to manage pain at the beginning of ward months.

Personality

You are enjoying your internship. You are feeling more confident on the wards but still worry about making mistakes. You like to run everything by your resident but your current resident has been encouraging you to make more of your own decisions. You feel fairly comfortable managing pain; however, you prefer to use short-acting medications, such as Percocet, so you can quickly make someone feel better and not run into problems with side effects or addiction. You know that she should probably be on a long-acting pain medication but you are not sure which one to start or of how much to start. You would like to learn how to convert her to long-acting pain medications but you are a bit embarrassed to ask. You also think her pain can be adequately controlled by increasing the Percocet. You view pain management as an outpatient issue, especially for a patient with chronic pain, and you think her PCP can just titrate her pain medications when she is out of the hospital.

You are a team player and easy to work with. You are receptive to feedback.

Timing Beginning

“So as you know,Mrs. Garcia is our 68-year-old woman with hypertension,hyperlipidemia, back pain and GERD, and she is getting ready for d/c as soon as her home care is set. Regarding her HTN, hyperlipidemia and GERD, she is well-controlled on her outpatient medications and doing well on her PPI. Regarding her back pain, she is still having some breakthrough pain. I reminded her to ask for her pills when she needs them. I was also going to increase her Percocet to 1-2 tabs qid prn. She will need to see her PCP soon after discharge to have her pain managed. Social work says her home health aid should be in place in two days and I think we should plan to discharge her then.”

# Middle

If asked about the status of her pain: Say that the Percocet works and she is just having a little breakthrough pain. She also isn’t asking for her pills so she ends up having to wait. [The truth is that night float has been called about her pain two nights in a row, and you feel badly about this.] You plan to increase her meds to 1-2 tabs qid prn and educate her to ask for her pills.

If asked why you ordered the Percocet prn: State that you didn’t want to drug her so she just sleeps all the time.

If asked about how you manage chronic pain: Say you know she should probably be on a long-acting pain medication, but she was doing better on the Percocet and she is about to be discharged. You think increasing the Percocet will help enough until she sees her PCP.

If you feel comfortable, you admit that you are not sure which long-acting pain med to start. You thought about tramadol 50 mg tid, since she has a component of neuropathic pain, but there is a drug-drug interaction with her antidepressant (sertraline). You thought about morphine but your resident seemed hesitant and you didn’t know how much to give her. You thought about oxycodone but remembered being told that we only use that pill in people who fail morphine.

# Middle, continued

Other reasons to use Percocet:
You worry about addicting her.
You don’t want to commit the PCP to having to give her a chronic narcotic.

If asked how comfortable you feel about managing her pain: Say “not too comfortable.” You are OK with starting the Percocet but are unsure how to convert to long-acting narcotics (see above).

If asked what type of pain you are treating: Say “probably nociceptive (muscle spasm and degenerative joint disease) and possibly neuropathic (buttock pain when she raises her legs - straight leg raise) due to radiculopathy.”

If asked about her spinal stenosis: Admit to not being sure how it presents. You think it might be leg pain related to walking and you don’t recall her describing that problem. Her buttock pain occurs when she stands to walk.

# End

If the attending identifies your knowledge gaps and leads the discussion in a sensitive way, summarize what you learned. (“That helps. I really wasn’t sure what to start, but I would be willing to give her MS contin 15 mg bid & prn Percocet. I just didn’t view it as an inpatient issue, but I see what you mean….”)

If you felt exposed because of your knowledge gaps, become quiet and just say that you will change her meds to long-acting ones, as long as everyone agrees.
