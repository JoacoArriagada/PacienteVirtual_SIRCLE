# OSTE Ward Rounds Case: Pain Management Standardized Learner Instructions -- Resident

# The Scenario

Mrs. Garcia is a 68-year-old woman with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, depression, and chronic low back pain who was admitted for evaluation of chest pain six days ago. She presented to the emergency room complaining of three days of a new burning chest pain associated with anxiety – her son lost his job. Her cardiac work up was negative (troponins neg x 3, ECG no acute changes, just some LVH (left ventricle hypertrophy)). She even had a CT angiogram in the ER because she had some SOB – negative for PE or dissection. Her chest pain responded to omeprazole and your diagnosis is GERD (Gastroesophageal reflux).

She has had back pain for years: low back tightness and pain that radiates to her buttock. At home she manages it with Tylenol and gabapentin; at base line her pain in 5/10 but she has flares to 9/10 pain once or twice per week. She had an LS spine MRI 4 months ago which was significant from multilevel DJD and mild spinal stenosis, and it was not significantly changed from an MRI she had three years ago. Over the past month her pain became worse (7/10), and she started taking Motrin, which helped “a little.”

Since being in the hospital she has had 9/10 back pain. She reports it became worse the night she was admitted and had to stay in the ER on a gurney for four hours awaiting a bed upstairs. She describes her 9/10 pain as a tightness across her low back that radiates to her buttock. It is better when she lays still and worse when she tries to move, change position, or walk. She denies bowel or bladder incontinence, lower leg pain, or weakness.

You initially changed her Tylenol to Tylenol with Codeine 1 TID PRN, but her pain was still 9/10. Three days ago you changed her T-3 to Percocet 1 tab QID prn thinking she would improve with the stronger medication. You view Percocet as your “go to” medication for managing pain on the wards. It is sufficiently strong to treat most pain, but short-acting so you won’t get into trouble with long-lasting side effects. Her pain initially improved to 5/10 but she began having breakthrough pain again. She has been asking for additional Percocet and for the past two nights night float has been called regarding her pain.

# Beginning, continued

soon as her home care is set. Regarding her HTN, hyperlipidemia and GERD, she is well-controlled on her outpatient medications and doing well on her PPI. Regarding her back pain, she is still having some breakthrough pain. I reminded her to ask for her pills when she needs them. I was also going to increase her Percocet to 1-2 tabs qid prn. She will need to see her PCP soon after discharge to have her pain managed. Social work says her home health aid should be in place in two days and I think we should plan to discharge her then.”

# Middle

If asked about the status of her pain: Say that the Percocet works and she is just having a little breakthrough pain. She also isn’t asking for her pills so she ends up having to wait. [The truth is that night float has been called about her pain two nights in a row, and you feel badly about this.] You plan to increase her meds to 1-2 tabs qid prn and educate her to ask for her pills.

If asked why you ordered the Percocet prn: State that you didn’t want to drug her so she just sleeps all the time.

If asked about how you manage chronic pain: Say you know she should probably be on a long-acting pain medication, but she was doing better on the Percocet and she is about to be discharged. You think increasing the Percocet will help enough until she sees her PCP.

If you feel comfortable, you admit that you are not sure which long-acting pain med to start. You thought about tramadol $5 0 \mathrm { m g }$ tid, since she has a component of neuropathic pain, but there is a drug-drug interaction with her antidepressant (sertraline). You thought about morphine but your resident seemed hesitant and you didn’t know how much to give her. You thought about oxycodone but remembered being told that we only use that pill in people who fail morphine.

# Middle, continued

Other reasons to use Percocet:   
You worry about addicting her.   
You don’t want to commit the PCP to having to give her a chronic narcotic.

If asked how comfortable you feel about managing her pain: Say “not too comfortable.” You are OK with starting the Percocet but are unsure how to convert to long-acting narcotics (see above).

If asked what type of pain you are treating: Say “probably nociceptive (muscle spasm and degenerative joint disease) and possibly neuropathic (buttock pain when she raises her legs – straight leg raise) due to radiculopathy.”

If asked about her spinal stenosis: Admit to not being sure how it presents. You think it might be leg pain related to walking and you don’t recall her describing that problem. Her buttock pain occurs when she stands to walk.

# End

If the attending identifies your knowledge gaps and leads the discussion in a sensitive way, summarize what you learned. (“That helps. I really wasn’t sure what to start, but I would be willing to give her MS contin 15 mg bid & prn Percocet. I just didn’t view it as an inpatient issue, but I see what you mean….”)

If you felt exposed because of your knowledge gaps, become quiet and just say that you will change her meds to long-acting ones, as long as everyone agrees.
