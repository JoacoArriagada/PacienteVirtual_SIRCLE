Massive Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding with

**Advice for Implementation & Deployment:**

We successfully ran the simulated scenario with a group of emergency medicine residents. A hybrid simulation design was employed. The scenario began with a medical student who played the role of the patient; he was moulaged with artificial blood. Procedures were then performed on a Laerdal airway task trainer, which was immediately adjacent to the moulaged patient.

While running the simulation, a senior (third-year) emergency medicine resident managed the case while a junior resident, intern, and medical student participated in the resuscitation. An emergency medicine faculty member observed from behind a one-way glass and acted as the instructor. After the simulation case was completed, a debriefing was held followed by gastroesophageal balloon tamponade device instruction and practice.

We found the following to be helpful:

**Initial Simulation:**

* Case length: 15 minutes
* During our pilot testing use of a moulaged actor was visually effective and encouraged interaction between participants and the “patient”.
* The initial portion of the simulation, with the patient-actor, should be allowed to progress without instructor intervention.
* When participants decide advanced interventions are needed (i.e. endotracheal intubation), the instructor should direct participants to the adjacent training models and encourage the case to continue.
* The simulation ends when participants consider and/or briefly attempt to use the GEBT tube; or do not appropriately progress in the resuscitation after minimal prompting by the instructor (who also acted as the telephoned voice of a gastroenterology consultant from behind the one-way glass).

**Limitations:**

Our main limitation was finding a suitable model on which to practice with the GEBT device. An intubation training manikin with attached lungs and an esophagus port worked well for practicing airway management, but the plastic esophagus was small and we were unable to pass the Minnesota tube into the esophagus. To overcome this issue, we had participants perform endotracheal intubation on the airway manikin and begin to place the Minnesota tube in the manikin’s mouth. Participants were then instructed to complete the passage and deployment of the Minnesota tube using the esophagus of an open airway model (see Photos & Images). Alternatively, a commercially available nasogastric/orogastric tube training manikin could be used to practice GEBT tube placement in the esophagus and stomach.
