**Defining Treatment**

Due to her FIGO staging of IB2, which is derived from her TMN scoring of cT1b2N0M0 (see table below), Ms. Pauls will undergo chemoradation therapy to her pelvis followed by high dose rate (HDR) brachytherapy. While Ms. Pauls is nervous about beginning this process, you reassure her that you will be following up with her each step of the way and that with adequate treatment the prognosis is good as she has stage I cancer. With treatment, 80-90% of women with stage I cancer and 60-75% of those with stage II cancer are living at 5 years after diagnosis. You explain that it is crucial that this cancer was caught at this early stage because survival rates decrease to 30-40% for women with stage III cancer and 15% or fewer of those with stage IV cancer 5 years after diagnosis.

Treatment will involve many steps such as port placement and cisplatin chemotherapy as well as both external beam radiation and high dose rate tandem and ovoid brachytherapy that will more locally act on the cervix. Radiation dosing is divided in this way in order to provide adequate dosing while also decreasing side effects to local structures such as bowels and bladder.

**Treatment Course and Considerations**

She is receiving general external beam radiation up to 45 Gy to pelvis and receives 19 Gy via tandem and ovoid brachytherapy as well as multiple cycles of cisplatin chemotherapy.

The treatment for Ms. Pauls has been difficult causing persistent fatigue, mild diarrhea, pelvic skin irritation, dysuria, and increased frequency of urination. Medications for management of side effects include Compazine and Zofran as needed for nausea; ultram for pain control following port placement; dexamethasone to reduce inflammation, improve appetite, and decrease sickness associated with chemotherapy; multivitamins to avoid nutritional deficiencies; aloevesta as needed for skin integrity to counteract the effects of external beam radiation causing skin irritation; and immodium as needed for diarrhea secondary to chemotherapy and radiation.

After a slow recovery from chemotherapy and radiation, cancer is no longer seen on scans or examination. Only time will tell if the cancer will return, so Ms. Pauls must continue to follow-up for several years with gynecologic examination and follow-up scans, but for now she appears cancer free!

It is important to consider social issues and barriers to patient follow-up, especially in patients who have a previously track record of noncompliance or physician avoidance. It is also important to provide constant reassurance on the side effects of chemotherapy and radiation and ensure that she is connected to community resources to provide guidance for supports including wigs for hair loss. Another important consideration is making sure that Ms. Pauls has a support system at home and has friends and family that will be able to provide both emotional and direct support. It is also common for patients to experience depression, so it is import for her to be connected with her primary care physician or a psychiatrist during this process. Her primary care physician will be important in this collaboration as she will need to be assessed for chronic medical conditions such as hypertension and diabetes since she has not seen a provider in some time.

**Importance of Vaccination and Screening**

Since cervical cancer can have a subtle and slow onset, it is important to remember that pap smears are an effective screening method that has drastically decreased the rates of cervical cancer in the United States. Recommendations for which women should receive pap smears and how often has changed over recent years due to efforts to provide adequate screening without over-testing and subjection to unnecessary invasive procedures. Current guidelines recommend beginning pap smears at age 21 and performing them every 3-5 years depending on age, history, and type of test (cytology versus cytology with HPV co-testing). With a history of negative pap smears, testing can be stopped at age 65. Testing can also cease following a hysterectomy for benign disease such as uterine fibroids. Various algorithms are provided by the America Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Patholgy (ASCCP) for follow-up and management based on the degree of cellular changes seen, presence of HPV infection, and other factors.

It is also important to consider that new methods of HPV vaccines are a new method of primary prevention that have shown promising preliminary data on decreasing changes related to early cervical dysplasia. This case of Ms. Pauls reminds you to consider HPV vaccination in all of your younger patients for primary prevention of cervical dysplasia and cancer by preventing infection with HPV. Gardasil and Cervarix are the two current HPV vaccines on the market. Gardasil was initially approved in the United Sates in 2006 and in most states by 2008. Due to the latency period of 10-20 years between HPV exposure and cervical cancer development, it is hard to definitively show that HPV vaccination reduces risk of cervical cancer currently (2015). However, studies in the United States have shown a decrease in high-grade cervical dysplasia in some states with increased vaccination rate. The vaccine contains recombinant HPV capsid proteins that form virus-like particles (VLPs), which lead to an immune response of high levels of neutralizing antibody. The original Gardasil acts against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18. The newer nine-valent Gardasil-based vaccine also protects against other risk subtypes of 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. Gardasil is administered in a series of three shots over a six month period recommended for girls and boys age 11-12 as to precede sexarche and has been tested in ages 9-26. Cervarix is a competitor vaccine approved in 2009 that acts against subtypes 16 and 18. It is recommended for girls age 11-12. Vaccines are not expected to cause regression of established lesions which speaks to the importance of vaccination prior to sexarche.
