SESSION II: Part 1

Ms. Pauls returns to your office after 1 month to follow-up on the results of her pap smear and transvaginal ultrasound. The irregular pattern of bleeding has continued this month with spotting occurring most days and increased bleeding after intercourse, and she has noticed some low-grade pelvic pain. She has also continued to have hot flushes and difficulty sleeping. Gonorrhea and chlamydia nucleic acid amplification tests were negative. While thyroid testing was initially a concern due to her history, her currently results indicate that the activity is currently within normal limits.

Transvaginal Ultrasound showed a 8x4x6 cm uterus with exophytic anterior fundal uterine fibroid measuring 2.3x2x2.9 cm as well as an enlarged right ovary likely due to ovarian cyst. You explain that fibroids can be a cause of abnormal bleeding; however, the pattern of bleeding that would result from fibroids is more likely to be heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding with pelvic pressure and pain and less like the low-level of almost continuous spotting that she is currently having. This imaging also shows no abnormalities in the endometrium. You explain that intermenstrual bleeding is not characteristic of fibroids and needs to be investigated further.

The results of her pap smear indicate Atypical Squamous Cells of Unclear Significance (ASCUS) cannot exclude High-grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesions (HSIL). You explain that ASCUS lesions can be caused by HPV and that cancer cannot be ruled out without further testing. After consulting ASCCP guidelines, you determine that a colposcopy (a method of using a special solution and microscope to look for areas of dysplasia and take biopsies) needs to be performed for follow-up of these lesions.

SESSION II: Part 2

On colposcopy, you note the external genitalia to be without lesions. The internal examination shows a normal exocervix (outside portion of cervix). After application of acetic acid, you note multiple lesions. You note leukoplakia (white plaques) on the lip of the exocervix and a faint gray acetowhite lesion. You take multiple biopsies for pathologic evaluation.

On return of pathology, biopsies show rare atypical glandular fragments consistent with adenocarcinoma in situ. Histology of samples reveals strong p16 reactivity in all glandular epithelial cells and Ki67 reactivity at 90% in glandular cells. Since this colposcopy did not obtain adequate margins around the lesions, you proceed to an excisional procedure to remove the entire lesion. You choose Cold Knife Conization (CKC), which requires a trip to the operating room, over Loop Electrical Excision Procedure (LEEP) since there are multiple lesions spread out over the entire cervix. Ms. Pauls’ pathology from CKC indicates multiple secretions of a deeply-invasive, well-differentiated adenocarcinoma in situ.

Ms. Pauls then undergoes staging, which includes the Transvaginal Ultrasound that she previously had to assess for local extension. Combined Computed Tomography/Positron Emission Tomography (CT/PET) scans are used to assess for hypermetabolic states in locations throughout the body which would indicate extension or metastasis. The CT/PET scan shows an irregular, fungating, hypermetabolic cervix with an additional area of hypermetabolism in the uterine fundus likely associated with her fundal endometrial fibroid. The scan shows no evidence of lymphadenopathy or distant disease. She is categorized using the FIGO staging system as 1B2 (cT1b2N0M0). “T” indicates the tumor’s local spread (i.e. the size of the tumor), which in this case is a clinically visible lesion more than 4.0 cm in the greatest dimension. “N” indicates spread to lymph nodes and “M” indicates metastasis, both of which are absent in this case. Based on the completed staging, a treatment plan can be developed which may include surgery, chemotherapy (with cisplatin as a staple of the regimen), radiation therapy and could represent meaningful learning issues.
