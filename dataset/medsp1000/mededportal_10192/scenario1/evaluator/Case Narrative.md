1. **TITLE OF CASE:** North American Pit Viper Envenomation
2. **TARGET AUDIENCE:** Fellow physicians, resident physicians and medical students
3. **LEARNING OBJECTIVES:**
   1. **Primary Learning Objectives**
      1. Demonstrate recognition of a crotalidae snake envenomation
      2. Evaluate a snake envenomation victim for signs and symptoms of toxicity, recognizing local, hematologic and systemic effects
      3. Identify severity of envenomation based on clinical signs and symptoms
      4. Demonstrate knowledge of basic supportive care measures in snake envenomation
      5. Understand that snakebites may progress in severity, requiring a minimum 8 hours of observation prior to disposition
      6. Understand that fasciotomy is rarely indicated for treatment of snakebites
   2. **Secondary Learning Objectives**
      1. Recognize the importance of repeat examination of the envenomated extremity for progression of injury
      2. Know the indications for antivenom (CroFab; Crotalidae Polyvalent Immune Fab Ovine) administration
      3. Understand the elements of basic wound care in snake envenomations
      4. Recognize the utility of poison control as a useful resource in the management of snake envenomations
      5. Understand the adverse reactions (acute allergic reaction, serum sickness) that can be seen with antivenom (CroFab) administration
   3. **Critical Actions**
      1. Perform focused history and physical exam based on reported animal bite
      2. Obtain appropriate labs
      3. Initiate normal saline fluid resuscitation
      4. Provide basic wound care and initiate analgesics
      5. Start timed measurements of the extremity
      6. Initiate antivenom (CroFab) administration (+/- toxicology consultation)
      7. Admit the patient
4. **ENVIRONMENT:**

**A. Lab set up** – Pediatric Emergency Department bed in simulation center or true emergency department.

**B. Manikin set up** – The manikin will be a clothed adult size manikin. At the beginning of the simulation, there are no monitor leads on the patient, no IV access has been obtained, and the patient is not on oxygen.

**C. Props** – Available for use will be a heart monitor with leads, blood pressure cuff, and pulse oximeter. Supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula and face mask will be available. There will be a medicine cart or tray with a full complement of vasoactive agents, PALS medications, medicines necessary for sedation, rapid sequence intubation, and analgesia. In addition, a fully stocked code cart with defibrillator will be available for use, along with a selection of direct laryngoscopes and intubation supplies.

**D. Audiovisual** – (See Appendix A) Available for review when asked for by participants will be photo of affected limb and laboratory studies.

**E. Distracters** – Distracters can be added at the director’s discretion. Choices could include obtrusive or difficult family members. However, as written, there is no need for distractors as the goal is simply to manage a medically complex patient.

1. **ACTORS:**
   1. Roles can vary depending on the number of participants in the simulation session and on potential actor availability. This case can be implemented using a single participant and one facilitator/operator who can provide oral feedback via overhead audio and play additional historical or consultant roles via voice only. Realism can be enhanced by using physical actors, such as nursing staff members, but these are not required to successfully implement this case.
   2. Roles may be played by resident or faculty physicians, nurses, or medical students.
   3. Actions for the roles will be as follows:
      1. Primary physician: The main scenario participant will act as the primary physician and do the primary evaluation of the patient to include obtaining a history, conducting a physical exam, and ordering any necessary medications or other interventions. The primary physician can perform any needed procedures, or can delegate these to other providers if multiple participants are present.
      2. Secondary physicians: Other participants in the scenario will serve as collaborators, assistants for any necessary procedures, and consultants.
      3. Nursing staff: The role of the nurse will be to administer medications, verify orders, and perform other tasks as directed by the physicians. The nurse can also make observations as needed to stimulate case progression.
2. **CASE NARRATIVE:**
3. **Scenario Background**
   1. Chief Complaint: Bite to right middle finger
   2. Triage Nursing Note: 14 year old female patient complains of intense right middle finger pain with bruising, generalized weakness and vomiting
   3. Vital Signs: BP 95/45, HR 135, RR 18, O2 sat 97%, T 99.8 F oral
   4. Past Medical History: None
   5. Medications: None
   6. Allergies: No known allergies
   7. Family and Social History: Lives on farm with father, mother and 6 year old sister. Maternal grandmother with cardiovascular disease.
4. **Initial Scenario Conditions**
   1. **History given by patient and parents:** Parents reports that the patient was outside feeding the horses on the farm when she dropped her cell phone into the grassy field. She began searching for it and felt something abruptly bite her right hand about 1 hour ago. She states she couldn’t see the animal in the grass but thinks it was a snake. She returned home and gently cleaned the wound. However, the wound has developed progressive swelling, pain and bruising. They decided to come to the local ED. While on the way to the emergency department, the patient started to feel tired, weak and vomited twice.
   2. **Circumstances at symptom onset:**  (Other clues to be provided if elicited). Previously, the family has seen rattlesnakes on the farm, but no one has been bitten in the past. The patient heard a faint rattle sound just prior to today’s bite.The patient noticed paired puncture wounds at the bite site (dorsal surface of finger).
   3. **Associated symptoms (given by system as review of system is completed):**  Patient has pain and swelling that initially started on the tip of her right middle finger. It has now progressed to the whole finger with mild swelling on the dorsum of the hand. Within 30 minutes, the finger developed bruising and blisters. She is lightheaded and feels generally weak. She vomited twice that is non-bloody and non-bilious. No SOB, wheezing, or coughing. No fevers. The remaining review of systems is negative.
   4. **Initial Exam**
      1. **General**: Patient is in moderate distress secondary to pain.
      2. **HEENT**: Atraumatic and normocephalic. Pupils are equal, round and reactive to light. Conjunctive are normal. Extra-ocular movements are intact without nystagmus. Bilateral tympanic membranes are clear. Mucous membranes are moist.
      3. **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardic, regular rhythm. No murmurs or rubs noted.
      4. **Respiratory**: Breath sounds are equal bilaterally. No wheezes, rhonchi or rales are auscultated. There is no nasal flaring or retractions.
      5. **Abdominal:** Patient is non-tender. Bowel sounds are normal. Abdomen is not distended and there is no rebound. No organomegaly.
      6. **Genitourinary:** Deferred.
      7. **Extremities/Skin:** Right middle finger with puncture wounds on the dorsal aspect of the middle phalanx. Significant swelling of the entire finger extending to the dorsum of the hand. The edema is soft, but extremely tender to touch and increases with passive motion. Radial pulses felt, capillary refill < 2 seconds. Sensation and motor function intact. Ecchymosis and bullous lesions noted on the middle finger. (Appendix A, photo)
      8. **Neurological:** Patient is AAOx3. GCS 15 (verbal 5, eyes 4, motor 6). She has no gross motor or sensory deficit.
   5. **Physiology (data points should appear when patient is placed on the appropriate monitoring devices)**
      1. Heart Rate 135, sinus
      2. Blood Pressure 95/45
      3. Respiratory Rate 18
      4. Pulse Oximetry 97% on room air
5. **Scenario Branch points**
   1. Changes in patient condition:
      1. Vital Signs (VS) - The patient’s VS will initially improve with a 20 cc/kg isotonic fluid bolus. Repeat vital signs are HR 116, BP 102/65, RR 18, SO2 97%. However, the patient’s VS will decline and need further fluid resuscitation (VS BP 97/55, HR 125, RR 18, RR 18, SO2 96%). Ultimately, the patient requires antivenom (CroFab) and further fluid boluses. If antivenom (CroFab) is not given, then patient will have worsening hypotension and tachycardia.
      2. Tissue injury – The patient will have progression of tissue injury and pain on repeat examination (prior to antivenom [CroFab] administration). The pain will initially respond to analgesics, but become recurrent. Repeat exam will reveal swelling that is progressive, extending into the mid-distal forearm. The pain has spread to involve the hand and forearm (remains soft, with good capillary refill). The patient requires antivenom (CroFab) on the basis of worsening local tissue injury.
   2. Request for old records: None available.
   3. Differential Diagnosis: The physician managing this case may consider a differential including animal bites (brown recluse, black widow, scorpion), trauma, sepsis of unknown etiology, anaphylaxis or DIC of unknown etiology.
   4. Intravenous fluid administration: An IV fluid bolus is indicated by the initial VS. The patient may transiently improve with 20cc/kg normal saline. However, the patient’s VS will deteriorate shortly thereafter (BP 97/55, HR 125, RR 18, RR 18, SO2 96%) and need further resuscitation with additional fluids. The patient should receive at least 40cc/kg normal saline for fluid resuscitation.
   5. Other medical interventions:
      1. Basic wound care interventions should occur, such as updating tetanus and immobilizing the limb in a neutral or elevated position.
      2. Analgesics (but no ibuprofen derivatives secondary to impaired platelet function) can be given after stabilization of blood pressure.
      3. Antivenom (CroFab) should be given based on criteria meeting moderate or severe envenomation. Usually, antivenom (CroFab) is given under the guidance of the toxicologist with initial dosing of 4-6 vials.
      4. Pressors may be considered due to the patient’s recurrent hypotension and tachycardia, but will respond to fluids and antivenom (CroFab). Some participants may start antivenom (CroFab) alone and/or both antivenom (CroFab) and pressors.
      5. Laboratory studies will confirm a coagulopathy. Antivenom (CroFab) should be given based on severity of the snake envenomation. Blood products alone would be contraindicated.
   6. Laboratory studies: Laboratory studies can be found in Appendix A.
   7. Administration of medication: The patient may be given antibiotics for possible sepsis since infection is a consideration on the differential diagnosis. Analgesics, pressors, and antivenom (CroFab) can be considered as above.
   8. Consultation of specialist: A pediatric intensivist and toxicologist/poison center will be available by telephone consult. Neither should direct management, nor be obstructive. If the pediatric intensivist is consulted prior to diagnosis, they should be unavailable until the diagnosis of snake envenomation is made. If the poison center is consulted for management, then they should be available to confirm treatment decisions that have already occurred. They should not be available to lead/direct management.
   9. Non-recommended actions: The participant should not admit the patient directly to the floor prior to stabilization. Surgical consult for early incision/excision or possible fasciotomy is contraindicated. Corticosteroids are not indicated for this patient’s systemic inflammatory response. Icing the wound is contraindicated as it does not inactive venom and compounds tissue injury. Ibuprofen agents are contraindicated secondary to antiplatelet effects.

**VII. Instructor Notes:**

1. **Scenario flow** – Instructors can directly influence the flow of the scenario by providing the initial patient history.
   1. Key aspect of case – Identification of a snake envenomation leading to tissue injury, hematologic, and systemic symptoms is essential. Basic wound and supportive care should be initiated. Fluid resuscitation and the initiation of antivenom (CroFab) should be performed.
   2. Information for participants - The severity of envenomation in this case is not representative of most N.A. Pit Viper envenomations. This case’s clinical derangements were amplified for educational purposes. A majority of snake envenomations require Antivenom (CroFab) administration and supportive care, but death is extremely rare. There are less than 15 deaths out of the 20,000 snakebites over the last 5 years. A case fatality rate of 0.06%.
2. **Information for actors** – This case can easily be presented without formal “actors” and by using other participants to serve as the nursing staff or other collaborating physicians. Any specific actors used outside of participants should be briefed about the critical actions and anticipated flow of the case ahead of time.
3. **Scenario programming** – The settings for a high-fidelity patient simulator are fairly straightforward for this scenario and do not require specific programming. Our program typically presents this scenario with the initial vital signs as presented above, with the major branch points of fluid resuscitation, administration of antivenom (CroFab) and consultation with a toxicologist.

**VII. Debriefing Plan:**

1. **Method of debriefing** – A post-case debriefing conference is recommended immediately following the end of the scenario. Consider including some of the following elements:
   1. Open-ended questions by facilitator – Consider beginning the session with a question to the primary participant about how they felt the scenario went. This often leads to extensive participant-led discussion that will touch on many of the major issues in the case. Invite any secondary participants and/or observers to comment constructively about the case.
   2. Brief didactic review – Some possible materials for review after the scenario have been provided in Appendix B. They address several major teaching points related to the case, however, individual participants may raise additional questions.
   3. Formal participant evaluation – Our group does not use this case for formal evaluations. However, this case can easily be adapted to the standardized evaluation method used at your institution using the objectives in Section III (above).
2. **Actual debriefing materials** – See Appendix B for debriefing materials.
3. **Rules for the debriefing** – Often an open-ended question to the participants will provoke a discussion of aspects of the case management that were good, and those areas where improvement can be made. Encourage your participants to discuss the case management decisions in a non-judgmental way. When this case is used for experiential learning (without formal participant evaluation) discussions may vary as compared to situations where formal feedback or grading is anticipated. We have found that placing an emphasis on the learning that occurs from experiencing a case like this is very effective, rather than focusing on the “right answer” or whether the specific management decisions were precisely as the leader would have performed.
4. **Questions to facilitate the debriefing –**
   1. Discuss the general approach to wound care in snakebites.
   2. Discuss expected local tissue, hematologic, and systemic effects that may occur in snake envenomations.
   3. Grade snake envenomations based on clinical severity score.
   4. Discuss useful labs to send based on expected venom effects.
   5. Discuss the benefit of using timed measurements of the envenomated extremity.
   6. What are the indications for giving antivenom (CroFab)?
   7. How long should a snakebite be monitored in the ED for suspected dry bites prior to disposition?
   8. What are the side effects of antivenom (CroFab)?
   9. Discuss the role of surgical consultation and possible fasciotomy in snakebites?
