**Appendix J: Follow-up Discussion Guide - Clerkship Orientation Case**

Below are common gaps and other teaching points that came up during the Clerkship Orientation Case. These points can be reviewed by faculty and serve as discussion topics as relevant:

* Some students do not think they will encounter LGBTQ+ patients. You will! LGBTQ+ individuals are not rare and self-identification rates are growing.
* Regarding asking all patients identity questions: When we don’t ask, we’re making heteronormative and cisnormative assumptions—and we’re relying on the patient to correct our incorrect assumptions rather than acknowledging that we don’t really know.
* Many students are worried about time: asking identity questions at the start of the interview really takes very little time; a thorough sexual history can also be streamlined and is certainly worthwhile (almost all of your adult patients are sexually active, and diverse sexual practices are more [prevalent](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs//data/nhsr/nhsr088.pdf)1 than many providers realize!).
* Mx. is a gender-neutral honorific—some patients use Mx., some don’t; it’s better to ask patients how they would like to be addressed or if they use Mx. than to use Mx. universally
* When taking a history, you can wait to ask about gender identity, but you really need to confirm this before you use any gender-specific language (such as sir or ma’am) or make any sex-specific care recommendations (such as cancer screenings).
* The same goes for sexual orientation—you don’t need to (and really shouldn’t) ask this at the start of a history since you can do this later when taking the sexual history, but you would need to establish this before you gender a patient’s partner in conversation, assume a patient has a reason to discuss contraception, etc.
* An example of a good way to transition into a sexual history from this great [Fenway Guide](http://www.lgbthealtheducation.org/wp-content/uploads/COM-827-sexual-history_toolkit_2015.pdf)2 is: “I am going to ask you some questions about your sexual history. I ask these questions to all my patients because they are very important for your overall health. Everything you tell me is confidential. Do you have any questions before we start?”
* Taking an extensive sexual history may not be part of your daily routine depending on what specialty you’re in, but you still need to feel comfortable talking to patients about this; however, gender identity is something you really need to establish for every patient when providing sex-specific care recommendations and to be accurate/show respect in the medical record.
* Some students expressed concern over not yet knowing the nuances of hormone therapy, and thus being uncomfortable prescribing it—don’t worry, this activity was only meant to prime you about the concepts. Clinical skills regarding dosage, etc. (of this and many other medications) comes in residency
* Insurance coverage for hormones for gender affirming care varies; see [UCSF CoE](http://transhealth.ucsf.edu/trans?page=guidelines-insurance)3 regarding insurance coverage for services that may be denied when there is a mismatch between the gender marker and the billing service
* See tables in the eQuality Toolkit4 for more details about gender-affirming surgery (page 16) and cancer screening based on sex assigned at birth (page 20)
* Mistakes happen, and there’s no need to get tripped up. If you misgender a patient or patient’s partner, you should acknowledge, correct, and move on: “I referred to your partner as ‘she’ before, but I realize now that your partner is male. We were discussing his ….” You can apologize if you think it’s necessary (“I’m sorry for that assumption.”) but don’t linger on the error—each patient’s response will be different, but excessively apologizing or explaining your assumption can be perceived as trying to make you feel better vs. the patient.
* Some students express concerns about addressing cisgender patients who may not be receptive to questions about gender identity: most patients are not hostile and do not take offense to gender identity questions, especially when the topics are introduced and normalized as part of a history that you ask all patients; if you experience any touchiness, acknowledge that you understand and move forward quickly with other history questions. Remember—cisgender patients have important answers to all of these questions too. (One additional clarification: questions about sexual practices should be comprehensive in the sexual history for both cisgender and gender minority patients—you really need to know what goes/went where for everyone in order to make appropriate care recommendations).
