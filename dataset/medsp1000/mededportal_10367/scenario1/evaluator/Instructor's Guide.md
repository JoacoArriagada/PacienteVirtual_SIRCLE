# Resource Files and Use of Resource Files

The case submission contains four elements to help direct the simulation experience. The first element is the case narrative, which includes all pertinent case information, interpretations of the case information, and anticipated case branch points. The second element is Appendix A, a PowerPoint presentation that contains pertinent case lab information, x-ray, and ECGs to be used during the case simulation. The third element is Appendix B, a PowerPoint presentation that contains teaching points for the debriefing session. Finally, an evaluation form is included, that contains recommended actions.

# Purpose and Education Objectives

Deep T inversions in the anterior leads (Wellens’ Syndrome) is a sign of life threatening coronary artery occlusion. The purpose of this simulation experience is to present a Wellens’ Syndrome case that allows medical students, emergency medicine residents, and emergency medical service providers to test their knowledge base and critically evaluate the management of this type of acute coronary syndrome: a Wellens’ Syndrome (deep T wave inversions in anterior leads) that develops into a STEMI during management.

Primary Learning Objectives

a. Describe and identify typical ECG findings of ACS including STEMI and NSTEMI   
b. Identify anterior t wave inversions as sign of critical LAD disease   
c. Create an evaluation and treatment plan for patients with acute coronary syndrome   
d. Order repeat ECGs for patients with suspected ACS   
e. Assess prior ECGs for patients with chest pain

Secondary Learning Objectives a. Communicate clearly with team members b. Divide and assign tasks appropriately within the team c. Perform a focused history and physical exam

# Conceptual Background

Wellens’ Syndrome is an infrequent but potentially life threatening presentation that requires an adequate knowledge base and critical thinking to manage. This simulation case allows real-time, hands on training for participants to practice managing this type of acute coronary syndrome.

# Practical Implementation

The simulation programming is not difficult, but the instructor should be familiar with their simulator software. Approximately 30 – 45 minutes is required for this simulation, 15 minutes to do the case, and approximately 15 – 30 minutes for debriefing and discussion. The debriefing

session should be conducted in a nonjudgmental manner, while focusing on identifying knowledge gaps and critically evaluating the case management.

# Deployment and Limitations

Pilot testing was performed using 4 Emergency Medicine residents at various levels of training. Following pilot, case participants had excellent feedback in regards to the topic, delivery, complexity, and improvement of knowledge. As Wellens’ Syndrome and associated ECG findings are relatively uncommon, participants, especially novices, may not be familiar with the ECG findings associated with this syndrome. Expected discussion topics include a basic overview and significance of Wellens’ Syndrome, ECG findings, and pathophysiology. The significance of the syndrome are demonstrated and emphasized by the STEMI that developed during the case and also by reviewing the cardiac catheterization findings of a proximal LAD lesion with $1 0 0 \%$ occlusion.

# Effectiveness and Significance

This case was developed to review and teach PGY1-PGY3 emergency medicine residents, medical students, and emergency medical service providers the significance of Wellens’ Syndrome and the ensuing associated acute coronary syndrome (STEMI). A group simulation case scenario was chosen to promote communication, clinical decision making, and to critically evaluate those management decisions.

Participants provided feedback of the case scenario during piloting. The combination of participation in the simulation scenario, interactive feedback session that focused on debriefing questions, and review of the brief PowerPoint lecture addressed all of the listed objectives for the learners. The case was definitely effective in familiarization and teaching about Wellens’ Syndrome. Additionally, most of the participants identified a general lack of comfort in recognizing Wellens’ ECG changes (even when the management decisions were correct), secondary to lack of familiarity. In this regard, the case was received well as it is an opportunity to critically review and discuss the Wellens’ phenomenon.

# Lessons Learned

Since this case is based on a real case that presented to our emergency department, minimal changes have been made to the case during deployment. This case provides a unique twist to Wellens’ Syndrome in that the patient truly went on to develop a STEMI during the emergency department management. Cues such as suggestions to re-evaluate the patient for decline in status and to re-evaluate the ECG rhythm were added to assist beginner learners. During deployment at our institution, knowledge gaps included: lack of familiarity of Wellens’ Syndrome ECG findings and the significance of the syndrome, inappropriate (late) consultation to cardiology, failure to appropriately initiate acute coronary syndrome management and cardiac catheterization in a timely manner.
