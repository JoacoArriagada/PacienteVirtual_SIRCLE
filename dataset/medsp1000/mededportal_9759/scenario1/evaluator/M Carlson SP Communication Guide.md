**Depression in the LGBT Patient:**

**A Standardized Patient Encounter**

**Standardized Patient Communication Guide**

Benjamin H. Huang, David A. Milling, Sarah Dimiduk, Harsha K. Nair, Karen H. Zinnerstrom

This document is a guide for Standardized Patients and Monitors on giving useful and constructive feedback to examinees on the Standardized Patient Post-Encounter Checklist and the Monitor Encounter Checklist. There are sections on both of these forms for communication skills, English proficiency, and general comments. Please note that while the document is addressed to the Standardized Patient, Monitors will also benefit from the information herein.

Standardized Patients should construct comments based on interaction with the examinee, rather than the treatment/diagnoses that they feel the examinee should have recommended/made . The examinees are constantly evaluated on clinical knowledge; this is an opportunity to give them some feedback about their clinical skills. Standardized Patients should try to explain not only that they felt that an examinee did well or poorly, but should also expound upon *why*.

* **HIV**: The examinee should have attempted to alleviate your fears somewhat, while giving practical information about the way that HIV screening works and/or about treatment options in the case that you have been infected. How did the examinee respond to your concerns about HIV infection? Were they acknowledged? Did you feel comforted? Were safe sex and treatment options discussed?
* **Autonomy**: At 17 years of age, you are mentally an adult, and want to be a part of the decision-making process for your treatment. The examinee should assure you that everything you speak about in this visit will be completely confidential, even though they may encourage you to speak to your family. Were you told that your answers were confidential and that your parents would be involved only if you wished them to be? Were you treated like an independent adult, fully capable of making decisions?
* **Sexuality**: Though this may be a sensitive subject, the examinee needs to be able to approach this topic confidently and without making you feel ashamed or judged. Did the examinee make you feel like you could speak openly about your sexual orientation? Did the examinee bring up this topic, rather than making you bring it up?
* **Counselor and Comforter**: Though this is a simulated appointment, the examinee must act compassionately, as if you were a patient in real emotional distress. You are portraying a depressed 17 year old young man with no one to turn to. Did the examinee show empathy? Did you feel like things were going to get better because of the examinee’s help?
* **Interview**: The doctor’s appointment should feel easy, yet productive. It should follow a logical order of progression, and not feel scattered. There should never be long awkward silences, but neither should you feel bombarded with questions and rushed. Did the examinee allow you to answer completely without interrupting? Were you examined in a logical manner while the examinee talked you through each procedure?

Finally, consider the overall approach from this examinee. Was this examinee someone you would wish to have as your physician in a year’s time? Were they knowledgeable, yet approachable? Did they make you feel as if you could tell them anything and that they would keep it confidential? Were you comforted? What is one word you could use to describe the examinee?
