Appendix D: Faculty Reference Material

Differential diagnosis of fever in a young infant

The guidelines surrounding workup and management of fever are based on an assumption that the infant is well-appearing, which is not the case in the current scenario. Nonetheless, a review of these issues is usually helpful for the students.

Fever may be the only sign of serious bacterial infection in young infants. For infants under 28 days of age with a fever and no source who are well-appearing, the standard of care is to obtain blood, urine (catheterized specimen), and CSF cultures and admit to the hospital on intravenous antibiotics pending culture results, usually for 48 hours.1 In well-appearing infants 1-3 months of age, there is no standard of care but there are some guidelines (Philadelphia, Boston, Rochester criteria) for assessing whether an infant is at low risk of SBI and could be safely managed without antibiotics and/or an LP and/or admission to the hospital (see table 1). Over 3 months of age, a child will most likely have other clinical signs if there is sepsis or meningitis. However, since signs of a urinary tract infection may be hidden, it is important to check a urine culture for well-appearing children aged 3 months to 3 years with fever and no source.2 Sepsis or meningitis should be considered in a child of any age who is ill-appearing.

Potential organisms and choice of antibiotic agents for a young infant with possible meningitis

The most common bacteria causing meningitis during infancy are as follows:3,4,5,6

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| age | Most common bacterial pathogens | Optimal antibiotic agents |
| < 28 days | E.coli  S. agalactiae (Group B Streptococcus)  Listeria (<5%) | Ampicillin  Cefotaxime (when available) or Ceftazidime |
| 29-59 days | E. coli  S. agalactiae  S. pneumoniae  N. meningitides | Ceftriaxone  Vancomycin |
| > 60 days | S. pneumoniae  N. meningitides | Ceftriaxone  Vancomycin |

Antibiotics in the newborn age group should provide coverage for Listeria if a child is under 3 weeks of age with Ampicillin. Coverage for E.coli may be accomplished either with Gentamicin (unless meningitis is suspected) or with a 3rd generation cephalosporin like Cefotaxime (if available) or Ceftazidime Ceftriaxone should not be used in neonates because it potentially displaces albumin from bilirubin.7

The standard antibiotic combination to treat suspected meningitis in infants over 28 days is ceftriaxone and vancomycin. 3,4,5 Ceftriaxone covers both S. pneumoniae and N. meningitides as well as neonatal pathogens (other than Listeria) and vancomycin covers resistant S. pneumoniae. (For infants 1-2 months of age, the ceftriaxone covers Group B Streptococcus and E.coli as well). Decadron treatment is somewhat controversial but is a potential treatment to decrease inflammation surrounding bacterial meningitis caused by H. influenza and S. pneumonia. 3,4,5

HSV should be considered in newborns with suspected meningoencephalitis; providers should consider sending cerebrospinal fluid for HSV PCR and intravenous acyclovir should be initiated pending results. HSV infection should be suspected in newborns with sepsis syndrome, negative bacteriologic culture results, severe liver dysfunction, or consumptive coagulopathy. 8HSV also should be considered in neonates with fever, a vesicular rash, or abnormal CSF findings, especially in the presence of seizures or during a time of year when enteroviruses are not circulating in the community.8

Timing of antibiotic administration in suspected meningitis

Antibiotics should be provided as soon as possible after blood and CSF cultures are obtained in a child with suspected meningitis. If there will be a significant delay in obtaining CSF (for example if the child will have head imaging performed prior to the LP, or if the child is medically not stable enough to tolerate the procedure), it is acceptable to administer antibiotics prior to the LP.4,5 Cell count may be helpful if a valid culture is not available, but it could be falsely negative (as in early infection prior to an inflammatory response).9,10

If antibiotics are administered prior to the lumbar puncture, it is imperative to draw blood cultures prior to giving antibiotics.4,5 A urine culture should be considered as well, since urosepsis may be the inciting cause of bacterial meningitis in young infants and the urine will be rapidly sterilized with antibiotic administration.

Interpretation of CSF results, including the analysis of a bloody tap

In suspected meningitis, cerebrospinal fluid should be sent for gram stain, culture, cell count, protein, glucose, and possibly viral studies (HSV, enterovirus PCR).4 The following gram stains results are indicative of bacterial infection4:

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| Gram stain | Bacteria (typical) |
| Gram positive diplococci | Streptococcus pneumoniae |
| Gram positive cocci | Streptococcus agalactiae (Group B Strep) |
| Gram negative diplococci | Neisseria meningitidis |
| Gram positive rods | Listeria monocytogenes |
| Gram negative rods | Escherichia coli |

Cell Count:

In bacterial meningitis, there are often >1000 WBC per microliter, with a predominance of polymorphonuclear leukocytes, but if it is early in the course of infection, there may not be a significant immune response yet and it is possible that there are few to no cells. 9,10 When a lumbar puncture yields bloody CSF, there is no formula for correction that has been shown to be consistently accurate. An increased number of WBC is abnormal and is concerning for meningitis. 3,4,5,6,11

The CSF glucose level is generally less 40 mg/dL or less than 0.66 of the serum glucose level (obtained at approximately the same time) in bacterial meningitis and is not generally affected by a traumatic tap. 3,11The protein level is generally over 200 mg/dL in bacterial meningitis.4

Differential diagnosis for seizure in a young infant

In a young infant with seizure, it is imperative to consider reversible causes including hypoglycemia and hyponatremia. Fever will lower the seizure threshold in a child otherwise prone to epilepsy. A mass or CNS bleed could cause a seizure.

Common etiologies for seizures in children (all ages) are:12

Infectious (meningitis, encephalitis, febrile)

Metabolic (hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypoxia, inborn error of metabolism, uremia)

Traumatic (intracranial hemorrhage or contusion)

Oncologic (tumor)

Toxic (hypoglycemic agents, lead, isoniazid, tricyclic antidepressants)

Acute management of a seizure in a young infant

The initial drug of choice to abort a seizure is lorazepam 0.05-0.1mg/kg IV. If there is no IV access, diazepam PR (0.5-1.0mg/kg) or midazolam IM (0.2mg/kg) may be used.13 Other options are buccal (0.4-.5mg/kg) or intranasal (0.2mg/kg) midazolam4. Repeat doses may be required. It is crucial to have airway and respiratory support available since benzodiazepines may cause sedation and respiratory depression. After 10 minutes, if the seizure has not been controlled, phenytoin or (preferably) fosphenytoin may be used 15-20 mg/kg IV.13 Levetiracetam has emerged as a safe and effective alternative second line agent at a dose of 10-40 mg/kg.14

Criteria for febrile seizures

Students will commonly think that the seizure in this case is a febrile seizure. It is instructive to review the criteria for simple febrile seizures.

Criteria for a simple febrile seizure: 15,16

Fever

Age 6 months- 5 years

Non-focal

Duration less than 15 minutes

No recurrence within 24 hours

No signs of CNS infection

Previously neurologically normal

If a child meets all of the criteria for a simple febrile seizure, no labwork, imaging, or treatment is indicated and the child does not require hospitalization. Febrile seizures recur in about one third of children, but they are benign and are not associated with cognitive impairment. There is a low risk of developing epilepsy (2.4% vs 1.4% in general population). 15,16

Oxygen delivery and respiratory support options

There are multiple options for delivering continuous oxygen for infants and children.17

Nasal cannula- 4-6 L/min- final oxygen delivery 30-40%

Simple mask- difficult to predict or measure FiO2

Partial rebreathing mask- FiO2 35-60%

Non-rebreathing mask- valves incorporated into face masks so that exhaled CO2 is not breathed in- deliver up to 100% FiO2.

Hi-Flow nasal cannula- 6L titrated up to 20L- 100% FiO2- provides positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to help keep alveoli open18

Bag mask: to be used if child not breathing spontaneously or gas exchange not adequate.

Rate for infants 20-24 per minute.

Self-inflating bags deliver 60-90% FiO2.

Anesthesia bags deliver 100% FiO2, but require experience to use appropriate amount of pressure.

Risk factors for child abuse

Clinicians should provide anticipatory guidance about normal crying behaviors in infants and screen for adult partner violence as well as maternal depression or drug abuse, conditions that may place children at risk for abuse. In taking a history, certain elements that are concerning for intentional trauma include vague explanations, change in important details, explanations inconsistent with injury, or different explanations from different witnesses.19
