**Ideal Scenario Flow**

*Provide a detailed narrative description of the way this case should flow if participants perform in the ideal fashion.*

Students enter the room and recognize an infant who is minimally responsive. They immediately check airway, breathing, and circulation for the baby and ask for the baby to be placed on a monitor. They recognize that he is a 5 week old infant with fever and lethargy and ask for intravenous access and blood to be sent for culture and other labs. They request a 20cc/kg fluid bolus using isotonic fluid without dextrose. They ask the mother some focused questions, including “what is wrong?” and “when did he first seem sick?” They also take a focused medical and birth history, including screening for infectious risk factors. They consider the differential diagnosis of fever in a young infant and request urine catheterization and equipment for a lumbar puncture. They also consider the possibility of neglect or abuse. Antibiotics are ordered and the students explain to the mother why a lumbar puncture is indicated, also considering that it may need to be deferred due to clinical instability. The infant develops a generalized tonic clonic seizure before the students start the lumbar puncture. The baby demonstrates cyanosis and decreased respiratory effort, so oxygen is applied and bag-mask ventilation commences. The oxygen saturation improves with this management, but his seizure continues. Students administer lorazepam and the seizure stops following one or two doses, with subsequent improvement in respiratory effort to baseline, with normal oxygen saturation. Students choose to either perform a lumbar puncture or to defer it until the infant is more stable. They administer antibiotics and possibly acyclovir. Throughout the case they provide clear explanations to the mother, who is distraught, and eventually communicate the likely diagnosis of bacterial meningitis.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

*Provide a list of management errors or difficulties that are commonly encountered when using this simulation case.*

1. Students proceed with intubation: during seizure and respiratory depression, an important learning point is that if oxygenation can be maintained with BMV, it obviates the need for intubation, and that when the seizure aborts, respiratory drive returns to normal. By increasing the oxygen saturation after BMV is initiated, students will usually not proceed to intubation.
2. Failure to recognize the need for anti-seizure medications: many students have learned that one can wait for several minutes before treating a seizure, but in a patient with cyanosis it is important to draw up anti-seizure medications and abort the seizure to improve ventilation. The nurse can prompt them by asking if they would like medications drawn up?
3. Inadequate experience performing lumbar puncture: students have varying levels of experience and ability in performing lumbar punctures. Students could review the instructions on performing an infant lumbar puncture prior to the session. The facilitator can provide direct instruction on the procedure either during the case or during the debriefing/skills station practice.
4. Simulated Parent challenges students’ ability to perform procedures: since students may have little to no experience performing urine catheterization, bag-mask-ventilation, or lumbar puncture, the parent may question their ability. It should be clarified to the Simulated Parent and the students prior to the case that in reality they would have a more experienced person helping with the procedures giving guidance but in this exercise it is acceptable to attempt procedures with little prior instruction.
