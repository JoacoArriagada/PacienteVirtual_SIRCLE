**Screening for Cervical Cancer**

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) systematically reviews the evidence of effectiveness of a wide range of clinical preventive services—including screening, counseling, and chemoprevention (the use of medication to prevent diseases)—to develop recommendations for preventive care in the primary care setting.

This fact sheet presents highlights of USPSTF recommendations on this topic and should not be used to make treatment or policy decisions.

January 2003

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

What Screening Is Recommended by the USPSTF?

- The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) strongly recommends screening women for cervical cancer if they are sexually active and have a cervix.
- The USPSTF recommends against routinely screening women older than age 65 if they have had adequate recent screening with normal Pap smears and are not otherwise at increased risk for cervical cancer.
- The USPSTF recommends against routine Pap screening for women who have had a total hysterectomy for benign disease.
- The USPSTF concludes that the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against new technologies (such as ThinPrep®) in place of conventional Pap tests.
- The USPSTF concludes that the evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against human papillomavirus (HPV) testing as a primary screening test for cervical cancer.

The Task Force concludes that screening should begin within 3 years of the start of sexual activity or age 21, whichever comes first, and should be done at least every 3 years. The risk for cervical cancer and the yield of screening decline through middle age.

For women older than 65 who have had normal Pap smears, the benefits of continued screening may not outweigh the potential harms, such as false-positive test results and invasive procedures. The Task Force also concludes that the yield of detecting vaginal neoplasms is too low to justify continuing screening after a total hysterectomy.

Most cases of cervical cancer occur in women who are not screened adequately. Clinicians, hospitals, and health plans should develop systems to identify and screen women, including older women, who have had no screening or who have been screened inadequately in the past.

**Ask-Teach-Ask**

“Ask-teach-ask” is a way to check patient comprehension and provide corrective information along the way.  We recommend that you use this in any situation where new health information is brought up such as a new diagnosis, a change in labs, or a new medication is prescribed.  In this scenario, the provider can directly elicit a patient’s concerns, assesses their baseline level of knowledge, and provide new or corrective information as needed.  Several iterations might be needed before all of a patient’s concerns are addressed.  This technique may greatly increase both patient retention and comprehension.  The following dialogues illustrate this method.

<u>Example:</u>

*Provider: Mr. Tempe, I noticed that your blood pressure is still high today.*  

*Patient: It is? I’ve been taking my pills just like you said.*

*Provider: Ok.  Well, let’s see if we can figure out what’s going on.  We both want you to be as healthy as possible.  Can you tell me what a normal blood pressure should be? (ASK)*

*Patient:  Well, there’s 2 numbers and the first one is usually bigger.  I think maybe something like 100 and 70 or something.  I’m not sure really.*

*Provider: That’s ok.  You’re close.  There are 2 numbers. We want the first one to be less than 120 and the second one to be less than 80.  Your blood pressure today was 153/95 so that’s too high for both numbers.  (TEACH)*

*Patient: I promise I’ve been taking those pills. They make me pee all the time.*

*Provider: I’m hearing you’re feeling frustrated that maybe the pills aren’t working completely, and with your side effects.  Let’s see if maybe we need to change the pills or maybe the dose.  Tell me which pills you’ve been taking?  How have you been taking them? (ASK)*

**“Teach Back” or “Show Me” (also referred to as “Closing the Loop”)**

Although the ask-teach-ask method helps identify and correct a patient’s unique concerns, it may not provide an adequate assessment of patient comprehension or retention of the treatment plan.  Since research has shown that most patients leave their doctor’s appointment with very little understanding of what was covered and what they are supposed to do, teaching providers to check comprehension before the end of a visit (or “closing the loop”) is a valuable skill.  Asking the patients to use their own words (or demonstrate a specific behavior) is an effective way to assess comprehension.

<u>Example</u>*: “Ms. Stratton, I want to make sure that I’ve done a good enough job in describing how and when you should use your asthma inhaler.  Can you tell me how and when you will use the inhaler after you leave our visit today? Can you show me?”*

Ms. Stratton explains how and when she will use her inhaler, and then demonstrates how she will use it.  If her explanation and demonstration are not consistent with your treatment plan, then you repeat the process until she understands the information and skills correctly.

We recommend that at minimum you use “closing the loop” at the end of every visit in order to make sure the patient understands the plan – i.e. what they are supposed to do before their next appointment (e.g. change meds, see specialist, change diet, call social work, etc).  You can read more about “closing the loop” at [http://www.webmm.ahrq.gov/case.aspx?caseID=53](http://www.webmm.ahrq.gov/case.aspx?caseID=53)<u>&nbsp;</u> or by pulling the articles in the attached reference list.

**Looking Ahead:**

Research has shown that medical non-adherence is incredibly common and may have very serious health consequences.  “Ask-teach-ask” and “Closing the loop” may improve patient comprehension and retention but they may not identify and address other obstacles to adherence such as finances, family stress, or stigma.  “Looking ahead” is a technique that asks patients to identify potential obstacles to future medical adherence before they leave the office.  Provider and patient can collaboratively consider solutions to adherence obstacles.  Remember that medical adherence includes adherence to medications, adherence to medical procedures (e.g. wound cleaning, checking blood glucose), adherence to appointments/referrals, adherence to screening/diagnostic tests (e.g. colonoscopy, mammogram), and adherence to health behaviors (e.g. diet, exercise).

*Provider: (after successfully using “closing the loop”) Ms. Tesla, you are exactly right about the changes we’d like to make with your anti-seizure medication.  What might get in the way of filling this prescription in the next day or two?*

*Pt: Well, I work from 8am-6pm every day and the pharmacy near my house closes right at 6 so this might take a while.*  

*Provider: Oh.  That does make it tough. Are there any other places you could get it filled?  I’m happy to call it in wherever is most convenient for you.*

*Pt: There’s a Safeway with a pharmacy near work.  If you call it in there I can go during my lunch hour tomorrow.* 

*Provider: Terrific.  We’ll do that.  Any other things that might get in the way?*

*Pt: As long as it is covered by my insurance, I think it’s fine.*

*Provider: Good.  I’ll double check that before I call it in.  One last thing before you go.  I know for most folks including me, it is tricky to remember to take something every single day.  Tell me what might make you miss a dose?*

*Pt: Well, I’m pretty good about taking my meds.  I need them.  But I do sometimes forget if I am running late for work.  I just rush out and don’t realize it until I’m already at work.*

*Provider: Totally understandable.  Is there any way you could keep an extra pill bottle with a couple of back-up doses in your desk at work or maybe in your purse?  That way you could still take it after you get to work.*  

Since there are often multiple obstacles, it is important to elicit and address as many as practical.  Depending on time, the provider might just offer potential solutions instead of collaboratively developing each idea.  If the patient is unable to see any potential obstacles, you might want to draw from the list of most common reasons and solutions for non-adherence.  Ask the patient if any of these apply.
