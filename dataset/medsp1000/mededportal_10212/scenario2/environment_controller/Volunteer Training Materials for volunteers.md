# Sessions 1 & 2: Background for Family Member Volunteers Patient Case, Roger Crosby

Patient background:

Roger Crosby is a 75 year old man with a history of COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) diagnosed at age 60. He has had 2-3 admissions (not ICU) for COPD related conditions (trouble breathing) over the past 5 years. He is in fair to good health. He uses oxygen at night or when he needs to walk long distances. He has osteoarthritis in both knees that often causes considerable pain when walking. Smoked 1PPD for 50 years; quit at age 65. He still drives short distances to the grocery store and senior center. He drinks one beer every night. He is able to walk up one flight of stairs before needing to rest.

Emotion, personality, interests:

Roger is a stoic man and doesn’t like to complain. He doesn’t like to burden anyone. He is a content person who adapted well to a new living situation when he moved to a senior living facility in West Roxbury. He is social, likes to play cards and bingo and talk. He plays poker on Monday nights with the same group of guys for the past 15 years. Roger loves to play piano and taught his grandson, Matt, to play. He especially loves ragtime and ballads. He talks about his grandsons a lot. He loves watching movies, especially comedies. His favorite movies are “Caddyshack,” “Blazing Saddles,” and Steve Martin in “The Jerk.”

# Education and work:

Roger finished high school and apprenticed with a plumber who was a friend of his dad’s. He wanted to be an engineer but his father died (in a car accident) when he was finishing high school and he felt responsible for helping his mother since he was the oldest of the three children.

Spirituality and religion:

Roger is Catholic. He goes to church, mostly for social reasons. He was close to the priest in their old neighborhood in South Boston but since he moved he doesn’t have much connection.

Family and social situation:

Roger’s wife, Maureen, died 10 years ago from a pulmonary embolism. He is a retired plumber who grew up in South Boston. Five years ago he moved into senior housing in West Roxbury. He has many friends there and seems happy in his living arrangement. He has two younger sisters, Teresa and Catherine and one son, Michael. Michael learned plumbing from his father.1

# Current Medical Information:

Roger was with a neighbor, who became concerned that he was having difficulty breathing and called 911. The neighbor also called Teresa, who visits Roger often. He was very sick when he got to the Emergency Room. He was unable to breathe and got placed on a breathing machine. It is unclear whether Roger was getting worse over the last days or weeks.

Crosby Family: Sisters, Teresa and Catherine & son, Michael

Sister: Teresa McLaughlin

Teresa McLaughlin is a 64-year-old woman2 who is Roger’s youngest sister. She lives about 45 minutes away from Roger, in Rhode Island, and tries to see him often. She speaks to him on the phone at least weekly. She is the closest with him of the siblings. She has always looked up to him.

Concerns and attitude about Roger’s illness:

Teresa understands that he will not be the same if he survives and thinks it would be a mistake to be too aggressive want to let things take their course. Teresa is worried about Roger’s comfort, and also about finances if rehab needed. Teresa remembers that Roger was relieved that his wife Maureen died quickly, without prolonged suffering or tubes, but he has not specifically expressed what he would want for himself. He has not spoken with his family about advance care planning. Teresa has a hard time imagining losing her brother.

# Education:

Teresa received a baccalaureate degree in dental hygiene locally at the [name of local school] in [city]. She feels indebted to Roger, who helped make it possible for her to go to school after their father died. She retired last year from her job as a dental hygienist in Rhode Island.

# Spirituality:

Like the rest of the family, Teresa is Catholic. She is not deeply religious but appreciates the traditions of their Catholic upbringing and turns to the church at stressful times like this. She wonders if it might help to contact the priest who Roger felt some connection to as they struggle with this illness and decisions that come up.

Family and social situation:

Teresa is married to John McLaughlin, who she has known since high school. He has had poor health since he had a stroke last year, though he has improved over the last few months so she now feels more comfortable leaving him for the day. She is concerned about leaving him alone for long periods, though, which has made it hard to visit her brother as much as she would like. Teresa and John do not have children, but she is close with her nephew, Michael, and his children.

Social:

Has friends through church, which is her main support system.

Sister: Catherine Crosby

Background, personality, emotion

Catherine is Roger’s 68-year-old sister who lives in New York. She is single. She is less connected with the family but still concerned. Catherine is affluent and can afford to pay for whatever is needed. She has already been providing some financial support to Roger, paying for his TV and computer.

Catherine is a bit assertive and demanding, in a way that may make her sister Teresa a little embarrassed or uncomfortable.

Concerns and attitude about Roger’s illness:

Catherine might be nonchalant about the situation at first, not really grasping the severity of Roger’s illness. The resident will have to be very clear and honest for Catherine to take it seriously. If the resident is clear about Roger’s life threatening illness, she will exhibit emotion in these ways:

Catherine understands he is very sick but thinks he would want “everything” done. She may not be able to explain what “everything” means, if asked, but she just feels afraid of giving up on him, or making it appear like she’s giving up. She may be trying to compensate for being somewhat removed from family-she clearly has emotion (guilt, defensiveness, remorse) about not seeing him for the past 8 months, which may compel her to be more “aggressive” with his care.

Catherine knows a pulmonary specialist at Mt. Sinai Hospital in New York and will request having him involved for a second opinion.

Emotions that may sound like cognitive comments or questions: “He’s a fighter. He would want to fight this!” “He was a plumber - he always thought you could fix anything - that’s what he would do - fix it.” “You can help him get better, right?”

If resident mentions any improvement in Roger’s condition, reply, “So he’s not worse, right? That’s good!”

Education and work:

Catherine finished high school and attended Northeastern University, where she studied communication. She works in New York City in the HR department at an outpatient adult rehabilitation center, Rusk Rehab at NYU Medical Center. She is not divorced.

Spirituality and religion:

Catherine is not religious but tries to respect that her family (particularly Teresa) relies on the church for support.

Other stresses:

Concerned about being away from work for too long. Roger’s illness is making her more aware of her disconnection from family, which she is probably compensating for by being less willing to accept that he may be dying.

Main concerns:

Roger’s illness is shocking to her. She hasn’t been around for the past 8 months so she hasn’t seen the decline in his energy and ability to get around as Teresa has. She has been a bit removed, busy with work and travels, so this is a difficult surprise. She thinks Teresa is being a bit resigned to the negative, when Teresa expresses uncertainty about what Roger might want to endure.

# Social support:

Catherine has an active social life in NYC and travels frequently with friends. She has her differences with her siblings but she still loves them and feels grounded when she does visit.

Michael is Roger’s only child, a 40-year-old-man. Michael lives locally in [town/city]. He sees his father two or three times each month. Michael took over his dad’s plumbing business and he works with his wife, Angela, running it. He feels close to his father and tries to help him as much as he can with errands and chores. He has always admired his dad’s diverse interests, and shares his interest in movies and political cartoons. Michael has two children (Matt, 11 and Jake 8) who see their grandfather ‘Poppy” a lot and adore him. Poppy taught his oldest grandson how to play piano and they play songs together.

# Education:

High school and worked with his father to train as a plumber.

Religion:

Catholic. Michael and his wife, Angela, are involved in the same church (St. Stephen’s) that his family attended when he was growing up. Their sons are in the youth program at the church.

Major concerns:

Michael is worried about how his children will deal with loss. “I’m worried about the kids. What should I tell them?”

Michael feels very concerned about having to make decisions with his two aunts and he is not sure what his father would want. He is confused about whether his Dad will be able to talk, hear and think after all this treatment. Michael often becomes quiet and disengaged during discussions - he’s really sad, anxious and not sure what to do.

# Other stresses

Michael works hard in his plumbing business. Financially he is getting by, but things are a little tight. He really wants to be in the hospital more to be with his dad but he needs to keep working. He’s worried about taking too much time off, and having to pay $12 for parking every time he visits. (If the resident offers to arrange for him to speak with social work about these issues, he will be relieved.)

# Social support:

# Tips for family volunteers:

When you are asked questions like “what is Roger like?” “What would he say is important to him?” or “describe a typical day before he became ill” you should pause and think as you would if someone asked you that about your own family. It would not be natural to have your answer immediately available with a list of qualities or activities or values.

If asked about what he is like, reply “He’s a great guy. Everyone loves him. “ Do not offer information about life style/functional status unless the resident asks. Even though he has had breathing problems you think he has been basically doing fine - he takes good care of himself. He is “quite active.” He’s happy and social. You don’t really grasp the severity of the situation (unless they are very explicit).

If the resident makes it clear that this is situation is grave it would be natural to show emotion: “This is scary”! If the resident asks about your fear or validates it you can add, “I’m afraid he might die. Do you think he’s going to die?”

Ways to show emotion:

• Nonverbal: look down, get quiet, look away, turn away or inward, head in hands, sad facial expression, taking frantic notes, using tissues, become quiet and detached. • Verbal: express frustration, anger, anxiety, overwhelm, exhaustion, guilt, denial, hopeful, etc • “How did we let this happen?” “I should have brought him in earlier”

Avoid filling the silence-if one family member pauses it is natural for another family member to speak. However, it is interesting to see what the resident does with the silence. Create silent moments to allow the resident to pause and acknowledge your emotion.

Toward the end of the meeting you ask:

“Can I see him?” Will he know me? Hear me? Be able to talk?”

Jargon: If they use medical jargon you can appear confused, seem overwhelmed or more detached, or ask them to explain it.

The second meeting is 10 days later, Mr. Crosby’s condition has not changed, he has failed an attempt at extubation 3 days ago and the resident is meeting with the family to share this news and outline the available options. When asked what you know, Catherine responds that she knows thinks that Roger is stable so this is a good sign.

In discussing regarding comfort care or care to prolong life ask questions: Teresa is concerned, “What will you do? What will it be like for him? Even though Teresa asks these questions she believes that he would want comfort care. Catherine thinks he would want to fight but is not upset with Teresa for different point of view. “He has a lot of life left! He likes his life!” Michael is torn - “I don’t know.” I don’t want him to suffer but I don’t want to lose him.” Teresa wants to make sure he is comfortable and is concerned about tubes, machines. Catherine wants to make sure everything is being done - “Can’t you do anything else?” This is Harvard - you’ve got all the best resources!” Michael is conflicted about what is best and not sure what to do about aunts disagreeing. Though close to father he feels aunts would know better than he about what Dad would want.

Potential challenges to introduce:

If they are not clear about severity of illness you can say “do I need to call my family right away?” or “I don’t want to tell Michael (Roger’s son) until after Matt’s piano recital on Saturday. It would just be easier to let them have the recital and then deal with Roger being in the hospital.”
Asking for 2nd opinion (this addresses trust-building-they should be happy to include anyone the family wants to include in the discussions of Roger’s care)
