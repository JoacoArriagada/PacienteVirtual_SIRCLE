# SESSION II: DISCUSSING TRANSITIONS IN GOALS OF CARE

# GOAL

To teach effective approaches and specific language to use in leading family meetings to discuss patients‘ goals.

# LEARNING OBJECTIVES

a. Describe important elements of a meeting to discuss goals of care (eliciting family understanding of patient goals and values, describing patient prognosis, responding to emotion).
b. Understand that open-ended questions are better than lists of options to understand what is important to patients and to explore their values and goals.
c. Demonstrate skills in: • Explaining prognosis (disease, specific causes for decline [multi-system organ failure, sepsis] and likelihood of death, if appropriate) in clear, simple terms. • Eliciting family understanding of patient’s goals and values. • Pausing after family expression of emotion and expressing validating statements.

# MATERIALS/HANDOUTS

One copy of attendance sheet, faculty session plan, and clinical case for role-play. Multiple copies of Communication Guide and Responding to Emotion handouts.

# 0:10-0:30 min (20 min)

# Elements 7 . 9 of the Communication Guide

Hand out the Communication Guide and explain that these are some of the key behaviors you are going to practice. Remind residents that the Guide contains a lot of good examples of language and phrases that can be very helpful to them, and suggest that they take the Guide with them after class and refer to them before they meet with families.

# Element 7: Responding to emotion and expressing support

Distribute the “Responding to Emotion” handout. Ask: “Why is it important to recognize and respond to family emotion?”

Discuss with the residents how to identify and respond to emotion during conversations with family:

1. Recognize it.
2. Pause, allow silence, and listen for emotion. Do not talk through emotional reactions.
3. Validate the emotions expressed by acknowledging them verbally and explore them further.

Remind residents that nonverbal behaviors have an impact on communication as well. Moving your chair closer, reaching out and touching a shoulder or arm, offering tissues are all examples that convey empathy and support.

# Element 8: Eliciting patient’s goals and values

Discuss why it is important to elicit the patient’s goals and values from the family members when patients cannot speak for themselves. Ask them if this is something they have done and what has worked for them. Look at the section of the Guide called “Language Matters,” and talk about ways to ask about what is important to patients.

Ask: Have you ever heard a doctor ask, “Do you want us to do everything?” “Do you want us to be aggressive?” “We want to withdraw care.” Have residents reflect on how these questions or phrases might make family feel.

An alternative to these questions should be linked to what is known about the patient’s goals and values. Ask about what the patient might “think “and “feel” when exploring these, instead of asking family what they would want.

Talk about how to avoid leading the family members to think that they need to make decisions for the patient. Emphasize that it is important to understand patient goals and values before discussing treatment options.

# Element 9: Summarize, check in, next steps

Here you can talk about the importance of restating key points and checking in with how family members are feeling. Often family members can’t take in all the information that has been discussed, so it is very helpful to ensure that they understand what has been discussed and have an opportunity to clarify information or correct any misunderstandings. Families also need to have a sense of what will happen next and when and how they will receive updates.

It is also important to inform family members about resources available in the hospital such as social work and chaplaincy, and make sure they know whom to call if they have questions.

Additional points/phrases you can suggest to residents for explaining patient illness and possible clinical course:

Explain, “He is very sick. It is possible he may not survive this. We will know more in the next 48 hours.”
Reassure families that the team is doing everything they can to take care of the patient and to make him or her as comfortable as possible.
If family asks: “Is she going to die?,” answer the question as honestly as you can.
If a family members asks “What you would do if this was your Dad?”, frame your response around your Dad’s values and goals.

# 0:30-0:45 (15 min) ROLE PLAY

Describe the patient case and invite questions about the clinical information, prognosis (refer to case description provided for role play).

Explain that there are volunteers in the waiting room who will be acting as family members in the roleplay. Let residents know that volunteers are former patients or family members of former patients, and they have been trained to do role-play to simulate an actual family meeting.

# 0:45-0:55 (10 min)

# DEBRIEF ROLE PLAY

Thank the resident and invite them to return to their seat. Invite feedback by asking the resident:

1. How do you feel about the experience?
2. What did you feel went well?
3. What do you most want feedback on--OR--what did you find most challenging?
4. Ask resident if they have specific questions for the family members.
5. Ask each resident to paraphrase and then give feedback on their assigned element. Limit your feedback to only one or two key points.
