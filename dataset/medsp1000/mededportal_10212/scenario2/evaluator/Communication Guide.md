# ICU COMMUNICATION GUIDE

1. Focus your attention on the patient and family

2. Introduce yourself and make a connection

3. Establish agenda

4. Find out what they know

5. Explain illness in clear, simple terms

6. Respond to emotion with empathy

7. Listen actively, listen for cues

8. Understand patient’s goals

9. Summarize, check in, next steps

Skills

# 1. Focus your attention on the patient and family

·Put away phone/pager, and papers, if possible · Sit or stand at level of patient/ family member · Maintain eye contact with each family member · Adjust your voice so your tone is calm and serious · Speak slowly

# Useful Phrases

# 2. Introduce yourself and make a connection

· Take a moment to establish rapport · Explain who you are, your role, the team · Ask their names and relationship to patient · Ask how they like to be addressed · Acknowledge that this is a difficult time and ask how they are doing

I am _ _, part of the team of ICU doctors who are working together to treat [patient]. How does your mother like to be addressed?   
This can be pretty overwhelming. How are you holding up?

# 3. Establish agenda

· Ask what they want to discuss   
· Exception: if there is bad news to share, deliver   
it at the beginning of the meeting   
· Explain your goal for the meeting   
· Restate their concerns and summarize shared   
agenda   
· Explain if there is a time limitation   
· Ask patient/family to stop you if you use   
language they don’t understand   
· Separate giving bad news and making major decisions

We wanted to meet to update you on your father’s condition. Before I start, can you tell me what you most want to discuss today?

I am here to let you know how things are going and what we might expect over the next few days.

We have 20 minutes for a discussion now. If we need to talk more, then we can meet again later.

It’s my job to explain things clearly. If I use language that sounds confusing, please stop me so I can explain things better.

# 4. Find out what they know

· Ask their understanding of the illness · Explain why you are asking, so the family does not think you are unprepared or feel frustrated that they have to repeat information

· What are their expectations?

I reviewed the chart and spoke with the doctors who were caring for your wife and now I would like to talk with you directly. Just so I know where to begin, can you tell me your understanding of her condition?

Tell me how you think things are going.

# ASK BEFORE TELLING

Opening with a question helps in making a connection and focusing the conversation. Depending on the information you will share, ask family members about:

What they want to make sure to discuss

# 2

# TELL: SHARING INFORMATION

. Tell the most important message first   
. Begin with a simple, general statement of how sick s/he is (think: what two things do they really need to know?)   
. Do not use jargon   
. Be honest and direct   
. Use short phrases   
. Use diagrams if appropriate   
. Pause frequently—Allow time to process information.   
. Give prognosis, if known, or share frustration about uncertainty. Try to identify what (and when) will indicate improvement or worsening.

3

# ASK AFTER TELLING

• Be sure to check family understanding and/or reaction after sharing news • Validate the complexity or difficulty of the news (e.g., “I have shared a lot of information with you--let me stop now and see what questions you might have”)

# 5. Explain illness in clear, simple terms

ASK--Always ask a question before sharing information. Listen to response without interrupting, and reflect back.

TELL—Be brief and clear when sharing information ASK--Check in with a follow-up question

· Observe reactions and respond to emotion.

· Remember there are often emotions in questions and statement—respond to emotion first.

· Answer honestly if family asks “is s/he going to die?”

· Avoid terms like “exacerbate,” “pressors,” and “extubate”

· Use drawings or diagrams when helpful

· Ask permission to move on in the conversation or to make suggestions

# 6. Respond to emotion and express support

· Notice emotion, pause and listen

What have you been told about your brother’s condition?

What is your sense about how your mom is doing? How are you feeling?

Your brother is very sick. His lungs are not working.

While we continue to provide maximum support to your dad, there is the possibility that he will not recover. I wish we had a clearer answer about what is going to happen. If the treatment is working, we would expect to see some definite improvement in 2 to 3 days.

These are the things we’ll be watching to know if she’s improving.

I have shared a lot of information with you. Let me stop now and hear what questions you have.

How are you feeling about what we have discussed?

# Useful Phrases

· Use nonverbal responses to emotion: pause, offer tissues, comforting gestures, such as a touch on the shoulder or arm, if appropriate

· Use verbal responses to emotion by validating emotion.

· Avoid premature reassurances. Instead, explore their emotions and provide support.

· Support hope

While we hope for the best, many people also want to prepare for what might happen if the treatments don’t work. Would that be helpful for you?

Would it be okay to talk about some of the treatment options?

# 7. Listen actively: listen for cues and explore them

# Useful Phrases

· Allow patient/family to speak without interruption · Use open-ended questions and allow time to respond · Reflective listening: reflect what you observe or hear with a statement   
· To explore their thoughts further, use “tell me more” · Invite questions

I’m going to summarize so we’re all on the same page--please tell me if I got it right.

You mentioned that your brother is “a fighter.” Tell me more about that.

I gave you a lot of information. What questions do you have?

# 8. Understand patient’s goals

· Get to know your patient as an individual. What was life like before they became ill? What are their values, interests, and important relationships?

· Explore what the patient would consider a good quality of life   
· Instead of listing treatment options, use open questions to understand goals   
· Avoid “what do you want” and instead, ask “what would s/he think”   
· Understand values and goals before discussing specific treatments   
· When patients or family ask for specific treatments or express their goals, find out more by asking "tell me more about that" or ask "why do you feel she/ he would make that choice?”   
· Explore and discuss likely outcome of treatments they are describing

Tell me about your mother. What kinds of things does she like to do?

What would your father say is most important to him?

What have been your sister’s biggest concerns?

What kind of outcomes would be acceptable to your dad? What situations would he want to avoid?

Imagine your mother sitting in her ICU room watching everything we’re doing. What would she say?

What do you think will happen if we do XX?

Thank you. This is really valuable information.

# 9. Summarize, check in, next steps

• Briefly summarize what was discussed • Allow family to correct or add information • Explain what will happen next • Agree on a time for a follow-up meeting • Encourage family to use hospital resources (clergy, social work) and to attend to their own well being • Emphasize support—that they are not alone

So, just to review, we talked about how we will continue the medications your wife is on for now, and we will see whether there are any changes in the next day or two. If we see that things are getting better or worse before then, we will contact you right away.

This is very hard on you. You may not be thinking about yourself but you need to take care of yourself as well. Are you getting any sleep?

I am here all night and you can always reach me if you want to talk. Just ask the nurse to page me.

Research has shown high rates of anxiety， depression, & symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder among patients and family members of patients in the ICU. Providing clear information, establishing goals of care consistent with patients’ values, and offering emotional support can diminish distress and improve quality of care.

Diagrams can aid understanding-- draw pictures on a whiteboard or paper when family is interested in further explanation

EXPLAINING CAUSES FOR BREATHING DIFFICULTY

![](images/c34667dfaed31182f6fe8443319555cb18a8b6fdd04d25996c0fbba07a722942.jpg)

When there is fluid in the lungs it is hard to breathe and get enough oxygen to the blood and the organs. Sometimes this is from an infection like pneumonia and sometimes it is a fluid buildup from a heart condition.

# EXPLAINING SEPSIS

![](images/3e587e47db32f8f659aeb558e0eafdd1cc8e7687dec2cabe6ffcc28c7c04b903.jpg)

Sepsis is a severe reaction to an infection, and it involves inflammation throughout the body. We need to treat this with antibiotics to see if we can get the infection under control and give fluids and medication to keep the blood pressure from dropping dangerously low.

![](images/6b737e071f18db9ce8670390f39e0461753b718d0427157f1628eed730cc18ba.jpg)

Often, recovery doesn’t follow a straight line, and we are likely to see ups and downs over the coming days. Sometimes people can return to the condition they were in before they were hospitalized, sometimes they improve but not to the level they were before, and sometimes their condition gets worse. We should have a better idea which way things are going over the next few days.

![](images/ef34fb0f43a9cd77251281915ace56ebf1f43b72fa032b8c9daa43385afe5fad.jpg)

Would it help if I explained what was going on in her lungs? ARDS, or acute respiratory distress syndrome, is a serious lung condition that leads to low oxygen levels in the blood. When you breathe, airs goes through your mouth and nose into your windpipe, and this branches off into each lung and ends in tiny air sacs called alveoli. These look kind of like bunches of grapes throughout the lungs. These sacs fill with air when you breathe, and small blood vessels in the lungs pick up oxygen from here and carry the oxygen throughout the body to all the organs and tissues. With ARDS, fluid builds up in the air sacs and then the body is not able to get the oxygen it needs.

# Language matters: Commonly-used terms and suggested alternatives

# AVOID	THIS	LANGUAGE

# TRY	THIS	INSTEAD

We need you to make a decision. What do you want us to do? These phrases place burden and guilt on family and prioritize the values and goals of the family, not the patient.

“What do you want?” asks family to make decisions and doesn’t help us understand the patient’s perspective. What people want is for their loved one to get better.

I need you to help me understand what is most important to your mom so that we can give her the best care possible consistent with her values.

What would your brother think about all this?

Some people in this situation would say “if I can’t live in my home, then I wouldn’t want to go through all this”

Would you like to hear what we might suggest? From what you have told me about your dad, it sounds like…

Do you want us to be aggressive? Do you want us to do everything?

It is hard to say “no” to these questions. No one wants “nothing” done for their loved one.

Reframe the question to contrast between whether the patient would prefer everything done for their maximum comfort and dignity or care aimed primarily at survival. Reassure family that the patient will be provided with the best care.

We will provide your sister with the best possible care.

We need to discuss whether life support machines would be what your dad would choose if he could discuss his medical situation at this point.

From what you have told me about your husband, it sounds like he really did not want to go through more invasive tests and might prefer to focus on his comfort.

We should talk about withdrawing care We may withdraw the use of life support machines, or treatments that are not working, but we never withdraw caring for a patient.

Based on what's important to your wife it makes sense to stop drawing blood for lab tests. We will continue to do everything we can to make her comfortable.

# We hope he will recover.

“Recover” means very different things to different people. If families use this term, be sure to discuss what this means to them.

Your sister’s pneumonia is better, but this last illness has made her lung condition worse and she probably won’t return to the condition she was in before she was hospitalized. She can breathe without the ventilator now, but probably won’t be able to live on her own anymore.

What does “recovery” mean to you?
