# Session 3: Simulation Patient Case: Background for Family Member Volunteers Patient Case: Roger Crosby returns with GI bleed

Patient background:

Roger Crosby is a 76 year old man with a history of COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) diagnosed at age 60. He was admitted to the ICU for 3 weeks with a severe COPD related failure of his breathing, with pneumonia as well. There was a painful series of family discussions about whether he would recover to a life he would choose, or whether he would even be able to come off the ventilator. The family (particularly Roger’s HCP, his sister, Teresa) decided he should have a chance at recovering because while they were still deciding he started to improve his breathing after 2 weeks of little progress. He had a tracheostomy and went to a nursing facility that handles ventilators, where he improved over 6 weeks, and finally went home with some home health support.

It has been six months since he returned home. He has been doing fairly well. He can walk 4 blocks, stopping a few times to catch his breath, but tries to do it every day. He goes to lunch every week with friends he has made in his assisted living community, which he has come to enjoy. He uses oxygen at night and during his walks. He uses oxygen at night or when he needs to walk long distances. He has osteoarthritis in both knees that further limits his walking ability.

# Current Medical Information:

Roger was at his weekly lunch with his buddies this morning at around 11 at Bertucci’s. He got up to use the bathroom and was found unconscious in the bathroom. Paramedics found him in atrial fibrillation (family members will most likely be unfamiliar with that term and instead will say that they were told there was something wrong with his heart or heart beat). He regained consciousness within a couple of minutes and said that he had “a lot of blood when he thought he had to go to the bathroom.” He was brought to BIDMC ED where he had 2 more episodes of bloody stool. He was lightheaded, sweaty, lethargic.

Teresa got to the hospital within 2 hours of being called. She is very concerned because he wasn’t very interactive and he had a tube in his nose, which seemed very uncomfortable and there were so many doctors and nurses running around. Now Roger is back up in ICU. The family doesn’t really know what is going on except that he is losing blood from his bowels and his heart beat has been abnormal.

Crosby Family: Sisters, Teresa and Catherine & son, Michael

Sister: Teresa McLaughlin

Teresa McLaughlin is a 64-year-old woman who is Roger’s youngest sister. She lives about 45 minutes away from Roger, in Rhode Island, and tries to see him often. She speaks to him on the phone at least weekly. She is the closest with him of the siblings. She has always looked up to him.

Concerns and attitude about Roger’s illness:

Teresa understands that he will not be the same if he survives and thinks it would be a mistake to be too aggressive want to let things take their course. Teresa is worried about Roger’s comfort, and also about finances if rehab needed. She is the health care proxy but there is an understanding that family makes important decisions together. (Do not mention HCP unless asked.) Teresa remembers that Roger was relieved that his wife Maureen died quickly, without prolonged suffering or tubes, but he has not specifically expressed what he would want for himself. He has not spoken with his family about advance care planning. Teresa has a hard time imagining losing her brother.

Sister: Catherine Crosby

Background, personality, emotion

Catherine is Roger’s 68-year-old sister who lives in New York. She is single. She is less connected with the family but still concerned. Catherine is affluent and can afford to pay for whatever is needed. She has already been providing some financial support to Roger, paying for his TV and computer.

Catherine is a bit assertive and demanding, in a way that may make her sister Teresa a little embarrassed or uncomfortable.

Concerns and attitude about Roger’s illness:

Catherine might be nonchalant about the situation at first, not really grasping the severity of Roger’s illness. The resident will have to be very clear and honest for Catherine to take it seriously. If the resident is clear about Roger’s life threatening illness, she will exhibit emotion in these ways:

Catherine understands he is very sick but thinks he would want “everything” done. She may not be able to explain what “everything” means, if asked, but she just feels afraid of giving up on him, or making it appear like she’s giving up.

Emotions that may sound like cognitive comments or questions: “He’s a fighter. He would want to fight this!” “He was a plumber - he always thought you could fix anything - that’s what he would do - fix it.” “You can help him get better, right?”

# Roger’s son: Michael

Michael is Roger’s only child, a 40-year-old-man. Michael lives locally in [town/city]. He sees his father two or three times each month. Michael took over his dad’s plumbing business and he works with his wife, Angela, running it. He feels close to his father and tries to help him as much as he can with errands and chores.

Major concerns:

Michael is worried about how his children will deal with loss. “I’m worried about the kids. What should I tell them?”

Michael feels very concerned about having to make decisions with his two aunts and he is not sure what his father would want. He is confused about whether his Dad will be able to talk, hear and think after all this treatment. Michael often becomes quiet and disengaged during discussions - he’s really sad, anxious and not sure what to do.

# Family concerns:

Despite the difficult situation Roger’s sisters and son/daughter were in during his last critical illness, they have not extensively discussed what happened with Roger and therefore don’t really know how he felt about going through that illness and long recovery. He seems fairly happy but he is a stoic man and he is definitely not as energetic or active as he was prior to that illness. The family is relieved at first that this illness doesn’t seem to be about his failing lungs. They feel stressed to be back in this familiar place where they experienced a lot of confusion, anxiety, sadness.

# Meeting # 1

The resident will explain the “GI bleed” and you should be unfamiliar with that term. They may also explain the “atrial fibrillation,” which should also be foreign. You should not easily appreciate how sick he is. You will be relieved that this is not a recurrence of the breathing problems he had last admission, not necessarily appreciating that this may be just as bad, or worse.

If asked about what he is like, reply “He’s a great guy. Everyone loves him.” Do not offer information about life style or functional status unless the resident asks.

Ways to show emotion:

• Nonverbal: look down, get quiet, look away, turn away or inward, head in hands, sad facial expression, taking frantic notes, using tissues, become quiet and detached. • Verbal: express frustration, anger, anxiety, overwhelm, exhaustion, guilt, denial, hopeful, etc.

Jargon: If they use medical jargon you can appear confused, seem overwhelmed or more detached, or ask them to explain it.
