# SESSION 1: THE INITIAL FAMILY MEETING

# GOAL

To teach communication skills and strategies to build relationships and establish trust with families.

# LEARNING OBJECTIVES

At the end of the session the learner will be able to:

1. Describe why good family communication is important to families, patient care, and physicians
2. Demonstrate (elements of Guide indicated in parentheses): • Introducing self and learning family members’ names and relationship to the patient (1 & 2) Creating a shared agenda for a family meeting (3) Using Ask-Tell-Ask style of conversation: ask questions before sharing information, then check in to explore reactions and assess understanding (4, 5) Explaining critical illness (e.g. disease, clinical status including severity of illness), in clear and simple terms (6) Pausing after family expression of emotion and expressing validating statements (7)

# MATERIALS/HANDOUTS

Single copy of sign-in sheet, session plan, and case description for role-play. Multiple copies (handouts) of reflective writing worksheets and Communication Guide.

# 0:05-0:15 (10 MIN)

# KEY ELEMENTS OF AN EFFECTIVE INITIAL FAMILY MEETING

Recap/emphasize goals of the initial family meeting with the group:

What are the most important goals in an initial family meeting?

Building relationship and establishing trust

Providing honest and clear information-what is going on with their loved one and what may happen next (including whether or not the patient is imminently dying, whether a few hours or days are needed to be able to predict what will happen, and what is being done to care for them)

# 0:15-0:30 (15 min) Explore elements 1-6 of Communication Guide and Ask-Tell-Ask

Hand out the Communication Guide. Use the Guide to illustrate behaviors essential to all initial interactions (Elements 1-6).

Let’s talk now about specifics (the How) (Refer the Communication Guide).

# Element 1: Focus attention on the family

Ask-What does it mean to focus your attention on the family?

Talk about verbal and non-verbal communication.
Discuss voice, tone, pace of speech.
Example: if you have all the appropriate phrases and explanations but you sound cheerful and rushed when the situation is somber and shocking for them, you will have a disconnect with family and they will not really hear you. Adjust your speaking style to the situation.

Ask-What about physical space and how you use it?

In meetings with the medical team, don’t have team on one side and family on other.
If you are alone (as in the role play), it is best to seat yourself so you are facing all family members.
Sit so that you are at eye level with the people you are speaking with.
It is ideal to be be close enough to the key family member to be able to reach out and touch him/ her as part of a response to emotion, if the situation warrants.

Cover the following points:

Identify yourself and your role on the ICU team. Note that families often don’t understand levels of training or how an ICU works. The resident can say that they are a physician here as part of their training (families usually understand this is a teaching hospital) and emphasize that they work as a team. If they have questions about the resident’s experience, they should say they always have a senior physician on the team, a supervising faculty physician, who is always available to you and the rest of the team 24 hours a day.
Find out who is who in the family: names, relationship to patient, how they would like to be addressed.

# Element 2: Introduce yourself and make a connection

Ask--How often do you introduce yourself, explain your role, sit down and ask open-ended questions? This is essential in making connections and developing relationships with families.

# Element 3: Establish agenda

Talk about the importance of establishing an agenda with the family, and how common it is to forget to do this. Talk about how they should share their agenda and ask family what they wish to discuss. If there is not enough time to cover all items, suggest that they let the family know how much time is available and then let the family know that they will schedule another time soon to cover remaining questions and issues.

# Element 4: Find out what the family knows

Ask residents if they have done this and why it is important (getting on the same page, correcting any misunderstandings, building on existing knowledge). Discuss how they might do this in a way that does not sound like a quiz and does not sound like you do not know anything about the patient; for example, “I have read your father’s chart, and just so I know where to start, can you tell me what you understand about your father’s condition?” or “Can you tell me what the doctors have already told you about your sister’s illness?”

# Element 5: Listen actively

Talk about the importance of active listening and using open-ended questions. Describe how studies show that doctors often do most of the talking, and that family satisfaction is correlated with the amount of time they are given the opportunity to talk. Remind residents not to interrupt family members, and to be active in exploring family members’ thoughts and feelings using phrases such as “Tell me more about your concerns.”

# Element 6: Explain illness in clear, simple terms using Ask-Tell-Ask

Introduce Element 6 and briefly introduce Respond to Emotion (Element 7), indicating that this will be explored further in Session 2. Talk about how to use Ask-Tell-Ask to explain illness or to share other kinds of information.

Describe Ask-Tell-Ask as a useful model to use whenever they are sharing information. You can summarize the sequence as follows: Ask a question before you share information, explain one piece of information, then check in with exploring questions. Provide simple, clear explanations, use diagrams when appropriate, avoid jargon (like “pressors,” “exacerbation,” “extubate”). At the same time, try to avoid making the information too simplistic, especially with more highly medically literate families. Check in after you share information and pause periodically to allow family time to absorb new information, and simplify or elaborate on the information based on feedback you receive from the family.

When you do this, pay attention to family member emotion, such as crying, worry, anger, confusion. Pause when you observe emotion, make eye contact, and express empathy by responding to the emotion (e.g., say “I know this must be hard to hear.”)

# 0:30-0:45 (15 min) ROLE PLAY

Describe the patient case and invite questions about the clinical information, prognosis (refer to case description provided for role play). Explain that there are volunteers in the waiting room who will be acting as family members in the role-play. Let residents know that volunteers are former patients or family members of former patients, and they have been trained to do role-play to simulate an actual family meeting.

# 0:45-0:55 (10 min)

# DEBRIEF ROLE PLAY

Invite feedback by asking the resident:

1. How do you feel about the experience?
2. What did you feel went well?
3. What do you most want feedback on--OR--what did you find most challenging?
4. Ask resident if they have specific questions for the family members.
5. Ask each resident to paraphrase and then give feedback on their assigned element. Limit your feedback to only one or two key points.
