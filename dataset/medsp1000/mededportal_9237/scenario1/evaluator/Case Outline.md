1. Providing Medication Instruction to an Older Adult Caregiver
2. Target Audience: pediatric residents, 3rd and 4th year medical students, nurses, nursing students
3. Learning Objectives
   1. Primary
      1. Improved skills in providing education and instructions regarding asthma medication instruction.
      2. Effective history taking and communication with an older adult caregiver.
   2. Secondary
      1. Recognition of misunderstanding regarding proper administration of asthma medications.
      2. Increased awareness of grandparents and older adults as primary caregivers of pediatric patients.
   3. Critical Actions Checklist
      1. The learner turns off the T.V. before beginning to gather the history.
      2. The learner introduces him/herself to the standardized historian.
      3. The learner maintains eye contact with the standardized historian while gathering the history.
      4. The learner sits at the same level as the standardized historian to gather the history.
      5. The learner speaks in a slow, clear voice
      6. The learner asks about the standardized historian’s relationship to the patient during the interview.
      7. The learner asks if the standardized historian is the primary caregiver. If completed, the learner asks if the custody arrangement is formal or informal.
      8. The learner asks why the patient is in the standardized historian’s custody.
      9. The learner asks about the standardized historian’s medical history as well as the patient’s.
      10. The learner asks about what medications are in the home. If completed, the learner asks where the medications are stored.
      11. The learner inquires about patient’s current asthma medications and dosages.
      12. The learner establishes that the standardized historian is not correctly administering the patient’s medications.
      13. The learner provides guidance regarding how to properly administer the patient’s medications.
      14. The learner discusses possible asthma triggers.
4. Environment
   1. Lab Set-Up: Pediatric Primary Care Clinic
   2. Mannequin Set-Up: young child mannequin, sitting in chair or stroller, or in exam bed
   3. Props – new patient chart, complete with triage sheet and blank notes, or as applicable for participating institution’s clinic setting; chair for the case learner to sit in, but placed in such a manner that the learner must move it to an ideal location for communicating effectively with the standardized historian.
   4. Distracters – If applicable for participating institution, TV on in background. Other distracters (cell phone, video game, etc.) may be incorporated, as desired by participating institution.
5. Actors
   1. Roles: standardized historian to play the role of the older adult caregiver (preferably age 65 or greater), voice for child mannequin
6. Case Narrative
   1. Scenario Overview
      1. Four year-old child presents as new patient with complaint of breathing problems. His caregiver accompanies him, and will be the standardized historian. The standardized historian is the patient’s grandmother or grandfather, who is experiencing various issues associated with normal aging.
      2. Chief Complaint - “breathing problems.” This may be revealed to learner prior to starting the case or written on the triage sheet.
      3. Medical History – if asked, the patient has history of asthma. The patient has reported to the ER twice in the past six months. He has never been hospitalized or intubated. He currently uses his albuterol inhaler 3 times a week, and coughs 2 nights a week. He tires more easily than his friends when playing.
      4. Current medications – The patient is supposed to be taking albuterol as needed, fluticasone 88 mcg inhaled twice daily. However, the standardized historian does not remember the medication regimen that was previously prescribed. She is currently giving a puff of each albulterol and fluticasone when the patient is symptomatic. The standardized historian is on amlodipine, glipizide.
      5. Family/Social History - If asked, the standardized historian tells learner that he/she is patient’s grandparent, and is primary caretaker of patient. If asked, the custody arrangement is informal, and the standardized historian is taking care of the patient because the patient’s mother has drug abuse problems. The patient does not attend daycare. The standardized historian has a history of type II diabetes and hypertension. She smokes cigarettes in the home with the patient.
   2. Learner Instructions: Prior to beginning the simulation, the learner(s) will be told that a four year old child presents as a new patient with chief complaint of “breathing problems.” The learner should be informed that the primary goal is to elicit a history from the caregiver and provide appropriate medical management and guidance. The learner should be informed that a physical examination is not necessary. This information is included in the separate document, “Learner\_Instructions,” which can be given directly to the learner(s), if desired.
   3. Scenario conditions – *Standardized historian is dealing with various issues associated with normal aging, such as minor hearing loss, visual changes, and cognitive changes, such as increased reaction time and decreased short term memory. She does not respond to the learner’s questions if they are not posed with a clear, slow voice, or if the learner is not seated at eye level across from the standardized historian. When asked long or difficult questions, the standardized historian may take a minute to think about the answer before she gives it. She should still appear competent to take care of the patient. She will be seated in a chair next to the patient’s chair/stroller.*
      1. If asked, standardized historian gives history that patient was diagnosed with asthma approximately 6 months prior. At that time, he/she was experiencing “attacks” 2 times a week and 3 times at night each month. His/her previous doctor prescribed two inhaler medications at that time. According to the standardized historian, the medications initially seemed to be working. They worked so well that she decided he was getting better, and “cut back” on the medicine. Now that the attacks have gotten worse, she is giving the inhalers more often, but his attacks are still occurring 3 times a week. If questioned further, the standardized historian tells the learner that she has been administering both inhalers at the same time, when the patient experiences an attack. If questioned regarding why she did not return to the original medication schedule, she admits that she has difficulty telling the two inhalers apart, and cannot read the pharmacist’s instructions for the medication, because the print is too small. Otherwise, the patient is healthy and happy.
      2. The standardized historian reports that she, herself, is in relatively good health. She has type II diabetes and hypertension, both of which she keeps under control. She reports that all medicines are kept in a high kitchen cabinet, out of reach of the patient.
      3. The goal is for the history to be elicited from the standardized historian. The learner may ask the patient questions, to which the mannequin should respond. The answers should be short, and not very informative, further promoting the learner to take the history from the standardized historian.
      4. Common questions posed to the standardized historian and suggested answers: (please note a separate file, entitled “Script,” is also attached.)
         1. Does Johnny live with you? “Yes – he stays with me all the time.”
         2. Why does he stay with you? “His mother has drug abuse problems. She is in and out of rehab and does not have a permanent residence. He has lived with me the majority of his life. He has no contact with his father.”
         3. Do you have formal custody? “Yes”
         4. What medical problems does Johnny have? Or How has Johnny been doing? “He is pretty healthy overall, but he has asthma.”
         5. When was he diagnosed with asthma? “Well… [pause] …he was diagnosed… hmm maybe six months ago. He had been to the ER a couple of times with wheezing, and he was coughing a couple nights a week. He had trouble keeping up with other kids when playing.”
         6. Has he ever had to stay in the hospital? “No”
         7. How many times has he been to the ER with wheezing? “I think maybe twice since being diagnosed. He hasn’t been in several months.”
         8. How is his asthma treated? “He has two of those ‘puffer things.’ I can’t remember their names. One of them is that ‘buterol’ and I think the other one starts with and “f.”
         9. Do you know the doses of his medications? “Oh no – I can’t keep those straight.”
         10. How does he take his medications? “Well, when he was first diagnosed, he took one of them every day. Then he didn’t have attacks anymore. I thought his asthma must be getting better, so he quit the puffers for a while.”
         11. And how is his asthma doing now? “Now his attacks are bad again. He is wheezing, let’s see, about 3 times a week.
         12. And what do you do when he has an attack? “I give him those puffer things. A puff of each one.”
         13. And does that help? “Sometimes it does, and sometimes it doesn’t. If it doesn’t help within a few minutes, I give him another puff of each.”
         14. If asked why she did not return to original medication regimen: “Well, I couldn’t really remember which one he was supposed to take everyday. They look alike. I have instructions from the pharmacist, but the writing is so small…”
         15. Have you noticed if anything triggers Johnny’s asthma? “I don’t know – I have never thought about that before…” Do his symptoms seem to get worse with exercise, or being outdoors? What about cigarettes – does anyone in the home smoke? “Well I smoke. I didn’t know that might affect his wheezing.” Have you thought about quitting smoking? “Actually, I have. My own doctor has finally convinced me, and I am already smoking less than I used to. It is something that I am going to keep working on.”
         16. And how is your health? “I’m pretty healthy. I have high blood sugar and high blood pressure.”
         17. Do you take medications? “Yes – I take glipizide and amlodipine.”
         18. And where do you keep medications in the home? “Oh I know to keep them away from little Johnny. They are in a high cabinet in the kitchen.”
7. Instructor’s Notes
   1. The primary goals of this case are improved skills in providing medication instructions and effective communication with an older adult caregiver. For this reason, the patient should be stable, and appear well, either sitting in a chair or on the exam table. The mannequin can answer questions if they are posed to him, but they should be relatively non-informative (Ex. How often do you feel sick like you can’t breath? “I don’t know…” Has it been getting worse? “maybe…” Can you keep up with your friends? “I like to play with friends.”)
   2. Tips to direct the standardized historians – the standardized historian(s) should be advised to act like a well-functioning, capable older adult caregivers, who are experiencing mild hearing, visual, and/or cognitive deficits that would be typical for a person their age. If the learner is not speaking in a slow, clear voice, the standardized historian should ask him to repeat the question, or act as if she does not hear him. If asked about when to give the patient his two medications, the standardized historian may state that she cannot read the pharmacist’s directions because the print is too small, or that she cannot adequately read the labels on the medications. When asked certain questions regarding the timeframe of the patient’s attacks, the standardized historian may take a minute to remember the answer. The deficits should not be so pronounced that the standardized historian’s ability to care for the patient is in question. The goal is for the learner to recognize that the standardized historian is not administering the medications correctly, and provide guidance regarding asthma triggers and treatment. When receiving the medical advice and/or instructions on how to properly give the patient his medications, the standardized historian may request that the directions be repeated. If asked to repeat back the instructions, the standardized historian may initially miss a step, but after further guidance, should act as though she fully understands and is competent to correctly administer the medication.
   3. Optimal management path – By the end of the exercise, the learner should have established that the standardized historian is the primary caretaker for the patient. The learner should understand why the standardized historian is the caretaker, and if the arrangement is formal or informal. The learner should know the standardized historian’s medical problems and medications, as well as where medications are stored in the home. The learner also should recognize that the patient is currently being prescribed the proper asthma medications, but they are not being administered correctly due to caretaker misunderstanding. The learner should address this problem, and offer solutions so he/she is confident that the standardized historian will give the patient his medications as prescribed. Examples may include, but are not limited to, writing down instructions for the standardized historian, taking the time to differentiate the two medications, and/or having the standardized historian explain to the learner how and when he/she is going to give the medications. The learner should also provide guidance and advice regarding identifying and avoiding any asthma triggers, such as smoke exposure, allergies, stress, or exercise.
   4. The exercise should end after approximately 15 minutes, after the learner has elicited a history and provided anticipatory guidance regarding asthma and asthma medications. In the interest of time, the learner should be informed prior to beginning the case that a physical exam is not necessary. However, if the learner proceeds through a physical exam while simultaneously continuing the interview, the exercise should continue until the learner appears to be finished with the interview and finished providing any anticipatory guidance regarding asthma medications and triggers.
8. Debriefing Plan
   1. Debriefing
      1. Should occur with the group with review of the video, if indicated.
      2. Knowledge support available should include algorithm for asthma treatment
   2. Key Teaching Points (These are also included in a separate document entitled “Teaching\_Points.”)
      1. Older Adult Health Literacy
         1. Health literacy refers to an individual’s ability to comprehend medical information and make decisions regarding their health.
         2. Health literacy encompasses the ability to listen, follow directions, communicate with healthcare professionals, understand medical terminology, and understand cultural norms.1
         3. Older adults have the lowest rates of health literacy skills among all adult age groups.
            1. According to the 2003 National Assessment of Adult Literacy, 71% of adults age 65+ had difficulty understanding facts written in prose form, 80% had difficulty understanding information in charts and tables, and 68% had difficulty understanding quantitative tasks.2
         4. Factors contributing to decreased health literacy in older adults include visual impairments, hearing impairments, cognitive changes, and a disconnect from E-health tools.
      2. Effective communication with older adult caregivers1,2
         1. Limit background noise. Always turn off the TV if there is one in the room.
         2. Speak slowly and clearly, but do not shout. Instead, try lowering your voice, because age-related hearing loss is associated with loss of ability to hear higher frequencies.
         3. Seat yourself across from the older adult and maintain eye contact.
         4. Let the light fall on your face. This will allow any older adults who read lips a better view of your face.
         5. Rephrase sentences into shorter statements, if necessary.
         6. Have the older adult repeat back instructions to make sure they understand.
         7. Provide literature that is “easy to read,” with large font, adequate margins, has sharp contrast between text and background colors, and uses plain language.
         8. Repeat information if the older adult does not understand. You may need to repeat the information several times for older adults, who have decreased cognitive skills.
         9. Personalize the information when possible, and highlight short-term benefits of following instructions. Older adults are much more likely to follow your orders when they can visualize an attainable, concrete, positive endpoint.
         10. Give instructions in the positive sense (“You must do this,” instead of “You should not do that.”) Older adults are more likely to interpret familiarity as truthfulness.
         11. Talk about important topics face-to-face. While sending caregivers home with literature is also important, older adults are more likely to remember instructions that you discuss in person.
         12. Follow up! An older adult caregiver may leave your office understanding what they need to do, but that does not mean they will be able to successfully complete their tasks at home. Emotional and environmental influences at home may cause confusion and non-adherence.
      3. Guidance regarding proper use of asthma medications and avoidance of asthma triggers
         1. Misunderstanding of inhaled corticosteroids, on the part of children’s caretakers, is associated with increased rates of non-adherence. The risk of misunderstanding is lower if the patient has seen a specialist or graduated from high school.3
         2. One study found that 23% of parents of children with persistent asthma misunderstood the purpose of inhaled corticosteroids.3 Another study reported that 40% of children with persistent asthma did not use a long-acting controller medication on a regular basis.4
         3. National Heart Lung and Blood Institute has created a standard of asthma treatment, which includes the recommendation that all children on long-acting inhalers, and/or their parents, receive counseling on how to properly take these medications, including the importance of taking them in the absence of symptoms.
         4. Latinos and African Americans are less likely than whites to use prescribed long-acting asthma controllers. This may be due to cultural beliefs, parental perceptions of asthma, and/or lack of health literacy.
         5. Children whose parents develop an asthma management plan with their healthcare provider are 6 times more likely to use long-term control medication, when compared to those children whose parents do not have an asthma management plan. Enrollment in an asthma education program and seeing a healthcare professional for non-urgent care in the last six months were also associated with increased adherence.
         6. Common asthma triggers include allergens (dust mites, animal hair, cockroaches, mice, pollens), infections in the lungs or sinuses, irritants (cigarette smoke, sudden changes in weather, strong fragrances), exercise, and emotional stress. Discuss the need to identify and avoid asthma triggers with caregivers.
         7. A spacer should always be used with an inhaler. Inhalers should be shaken before each use. Caregivers should use a calendar to keep track of the number of daily doses used, so they will know when the inhaler is empty. Alternatively, physicians may prescribe an inhaler with counter.
   3. Questions to Facilitate Debriefing
      1. What are your impressions of this encounter? Did this office visit go well? Why or why not?
      2. What do you think is the purpose of this simulation case?
      3. Was it challenging eliciting a history from this caregiver? If yes, what was challenging?
      4. Was it difficult trying to explain how to properly administer the patient’s asthma medications? If the learner did not attempt to explain how to properly administer the medications, he should be asked during the debriefing how he would attempt to explain the medication regimen.
      5. What barriers might the older adult have to successfully administering the patient’s medications?
      6. What techniques might you use to ensure that the older adult knows how to properly administer the medications?
9. Pilot Testing and Revisions
   1. Pilot testing – This case has been presented to eight pediatric residents since the initial case in July 2011.
      1. During the pilot testing, the following answers were given to the debriefing questions offered above.
         1. Was it challenging eliciting a history from this caregiver? Most participants thought eliciting the history was challenging. This was attributed to the standardized historian’s lack of knowledge regarding the patient’s condition and medication regimen, the standardized historian’s inability to understand several questions posed by learner(s), and the learner’s difficulty keeping the standardized historian’s attention.
         2. Was it difficult trying to explain how to properly administer the patient’s asthma medications? Most learners felt it was frustrating trying to explain the medication regimen. This was also attributed to difficulty keeping the standardized historian’s attention.
         3. How would you attempt to explain the medication regimen? Multiple learners mentioned explaining the purpose and mechanism of action of each medication, so that the standardized historian may understand the importance of administering fluticasone on a regular basis. One learner suggested the standardized historian keep the fluticasone inhaler beside the patient’s toothbrush, so the standardized historian/patient would remember to use it twice daily.
         4. What barriers might the older adult have to successfully administering the patient’s medications? Learners mentioned that it may have been several years since the standardized historian raised her own children, and may have no previous exposure to asthma and its treatment. The standardized historian also has her own medication regimen to remember.
         5. What techniques might you use to ensure that the caregiver knows how to properly administer the medications? Several learners mentioned writing out the instructions, using large font, using the read-back method, or having the standardized historian complete an asthma education class.
      2. During pilot testing, learners were asked to complete a confidential survey regarding the usefulness of the case. Six learners completed this survey. One hundred percent of those surveyed thought the topic was of value and would be applicable to their profession. One hundred percent of learners also thought the case and debriefing session were valuable learning experiences, and would recommend the program to others. Topics that learners listed as having enjoyed learning about included complications of second generation caregivers, review of important history questions for asthma evaluation, use of the “repeat back” method, and tools for explaining asthma medications. Suggestions for improvement included having practice asthma inhalers and aerochambers available for teaching demonstration during the simulation case. Another learner included that it would also be a valuable learning experience to develop a simulation case regarding providing medication instruction to non-English-speaking caregivers.
      3. This case was initially written as a well-child check up. The intent was for the learner to elicit the patient’s current symptoms and medical history during the interview, and then proceed with providing medication instructions and guidance regarding asthma triggers. However, it was felt that not providing the learner with a chief complaint may result in the learner spending a large portion of time examining the patient and providing further age-appropriate anticipatory guidance. While this would be expected practice for a well-child visit, these tasks were not part of the critical actions checklist, nor are they necessary to demonstrate the learning objectives. The case was changed to a sick visit prior to pilot testing.
   2. Performance Expectations and Management Mistakes– The performance expectations are that the learner will be able to effectively provide guidance regarding asthma medications to a standardized historian with limited health literacy skills. The patient will remain stable throughout the scenario, so no direct medical management should occur. The learner may mistakenly change the patient’s medications, or believe the standardized historian to be incapable of caring for the patient.
   3. A Critical Actions Checklist is included in a separate file. This may be completed by an observer watching the case in real time or by video review. A learner evaluation form is also included in a separate file for learner evaluation of the case.
