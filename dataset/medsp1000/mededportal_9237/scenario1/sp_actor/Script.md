**Script: Providing Medication Instruction to an Older Adult Caregiver**

Your Role: the grandparent to 4-year old Johnny, who has a history of asthma. You have brought him to the pediatrician for breathing problems. Due to misunderstanding, you have been incorrectly administering Johnny’s asthma medications. You are experiencing age-related changes that are typical of someone in the 60s or older (difficulty reading fine print, minor hearing loss, decreased short term memory, increased reaction time). These changes may affect the learner’s ability to communicate with you.

**Tips for your role:**

* Do not respond to the learner’s questions unless they are posed in a slow, clear voice, and the learner is maintaining eye contact.
* If the learner does not ask questions in a slow, clear voice while maintaining eye contact, you may ignore the question, answer a different question, say “What?”, etc.
* When asked questions about recent history, it is OK to “stumble” over your answer. You may act confused, give one answer, then retract it and give a different answer.
* At one point in the scenario, the learner may try to give you instructions regarding the administration of asthma medications. The first time the directions are given, you may act like you do not completely understand the directions. Or, if asked to repeat back the instructions, you may miss a step in the sequence. Once the learner explains the instructions again, you can act like you fully understand.
* The goal is for you to portray a typical older adult who is experiencing common age-related changes in hearing, vision, and cognition. You should still seem capable to care for the child.

**Possible questions and answers:**

* + - 1. What medical problems does Johnny have? Or How has Johnny been doing? “He is pretty healthy overall, but he has asthma.”
      2. When was he diagnosed with asthma? “Well… [pause] …he was diagnosed… hmm maybe six months ago. He had been to the ER a couple of times with wheezing, and he was coughing a couple nights a week. He had trouble keeping up with other kids when playing.”
      3. Has he ever had to stay in the hospital? “No”
      4. How is his asthma treated? “He has two of those ‘puffer things.’ I can’t remember their names. One of them is that ‘buterol’ and I think the other one ends in ‘-asone.”
      5. Do you know the doses of his medications? “Oh I can’t really keep those straight.”
      6. How does he take his medications? “Well, when he was first diagnosed, he took one of them every day. Then he didn’t have attacks anymore. I thought his asthma must be getting better, so he quit the puffers for a while.
      7. And how is his asthma doing now? “Now his attacks are bad again. He is wheezing, let’s see, about 3 times a week. When he gets an attack, I give him a puff of each puffer. But sometimes that doesn’t help. If it doesn’t help within a few minutes, I give him another puff of each.”
      8. If asked why you did not return to original medication regimen: “Well, I couldn’t really remember which one he was supposed to take everyday. They look alike. I have instructions from the pharmacist, but the writing is so small…”
      9. Have you noticed if anything triggers Johnny to have an attack? “Well I don’t really know. I never thought about it. What do you mean?” Sometimes things such as smoke, allergens, or exercise may induce an asthma attack. Is Johnny around anyone who smokes? “Oh, I smoke…” Did you know that your smoking may be contributing to Johnny’s wheezing? “I guess I never really thought about it…” Have you thought about quitting? “I have thought about quitting – my own doctor has been nagging me for years. I am already smoking less than I used to, and I am going to keep working on it.”

There are other questions that learners may ask.

If asked why Johnny is in your care, say that his mother has drug abuse problems, is in and out of rehab, does not have a permanent residence, and that he has lived with you the majority of his life. He has no contact with his father.

If asked whether you have formal custody, say no. The custody arrangement is informal.

If asked about your health history, say that you have high blood sugar and high blood pressure, and that you take glipizide and amlodipine.

If asked where medications are kept in the home, say they are in a high cabinet in the kitchen.
