4. Environment
   1. Lab Set-Up: Pediatric Primary Care Clinic
   2. Mannequin Set-Up: young child mannequin, sitting in chair or stroller, or in exam bed
   3. Props – new patient chart, complete with triage sheet and blank notes, or as applicable for participating institution’s clinic setting; chair for the case learner to sit in, but placed in such a manner that the learner must move it to an ideal location for communicating effectively with the standardized historian.
   4. Distracters – If applicable for participating institution, TV on in background. Other distracters (cell phone, video game, etc.) may be incorporated, as desired by participating institution.
5. Actors
   1. Roles: standardized historian to play the role of the older adult caregiver (preferably age 65 or greater), voice for child mannequin
6. Case Narrative
   1. Scenario Overview
      1. Four year-old child presents as new patient with complaint of breathing problems. His caregiver accompanies him, and will be the standardized historian. The standardized historian is the patient’s grandmother or grandfather, who is experiencing various issues associated with normal aging.
      2. Chief Complaint - “breathing problems.” This may be revealed to learner prior to starting the case or written on the triage sheet.
      3. Medical History – if asked, the patient has history of asthma. The patient has reported to the ER twice in the past six months. He has never been hospitalized or intubated. He currently uses his albuterol inhaler 3 times a week, and coughs 2 nights a week. He tires more easily than his friends when playing.
      4. Current medications – The patient is supposed to be taking albuterol as needed, fluticasone 88 mcg inhaled twice daily. However, the standardized historian does not remember the medication regimen that was previously prescribed. She is currently giving a puff of each albulterol and fluticasone when the patient is symptomatic. The standardized historian is on amlodipine, glipizide.
      5. Family/Social History - If asked, the standardized historian tells learner that he/she is patient’s grandparent, and is primary caretaker of patient. If asked, the custody arrangement is informal, and the standardized historian is taking care of the patient because the patient’s mother has drug abuse problems. The patient does not attend daycare. The standardized historian has a history of type II diabetes and hypertension. She smokes cigarettes in the home with the patient.
   2. Learner Instructions: Prior to beginning the simulation, the learner(s) will be told that a four year old child presents as a new patient with chief complaint of “breathing problems.” The learner should be informed that the primary goal is to elicit a history from the caregiver and provide appropriate medical management and guidance. The learner should be informed that a physical examination is not necessary. This information is included in the separate document, “Learner\_Instructions,” which can be given directly to the learner(s), if desired.
   3. Scenario conditions – *Standardized historian is dealing with various issues associated with normal aging, such as minor hearing loss, visual changes, and cognitive changes, such as increased reaction time and decreased short term memory. She does not respond to the learner’s questions if they are not posed with a clear, slow voice, or if the learner is not seated at eye level across from the standardized historian. When asked long or difficult questions, the standardized historian may take a minute to think about the answer before she gives it. She should still appear competent to take care of the patient. She will be seated in a chair next to the patient’s chair/stroller.*
      1. If asked, standardized historian gives history that patient was diagnosed with asthma approximately 6 months prior. At that time, he/she was experiencing “attacks” 2 times a week and 3 times at night each month. His/her previous doctor prescribed two inhaler medications at that time. According to the standardized historian, the medications initially seemed to be working. They worked so well that she decided he was getting better, and “cut back” on the medicine. Now that the attacks have gotten worse, she is giving the inhalers more often, but his attacks are still occurring 3 times a week. If questioned further, the standardized historian tells the learner that she has been administering both inhalers at the same time, when the patient experiences an attack. If questioned regarding why she did not return to the original medication schedule, she admits that she has difficulty telling the two inhalers apart, and cannot read the pharmacist’s instructions for the medication, because the print is too small. Otherwise, the patient is healthy and happy.
      2. The standardized historian reports that she, herself, is in relatively good health. She has type II diabetes and hypertension, both of which she keeps under control. She reports that all medicines are kept in a high kitchen cabinet, out of reach of the patient.
      3. The goal is for the history to be elicited from the standardized historian. The learner may ask the patient questions, to which the mannequin should respond. The answers should be short, and not very informative, further promoting the learner to take the history from the standardized historian.
      4. Common questions posed to the standardized historian and suggested answers: (please note a separate file, entitled “Script,” is also attached.)
         1. Does Johnny live with you? “Yes – he stays with me all the time.”
         2. Why does he stay with you? “His mother has drug abuse problems. She is in and out of rehab and does not have a permanent residence. He has lived with me the majority of his life. He has no contact with his father.”
         3. Do you have formal custody? “No”
         4. What medical problems does Johnny have? Or How has Johnny been doing? “He is pretty healthy overall, but he has asthma.”
         5. When was he diagnosed with asthma? “Well… [pause] …he was diagnosed… hmm maybe six months ago. He had been to the ER a couple of times with wheezing, and he was coughing a couple nights a week. He had trouble keeping up with other kids when playing.”
         6. Has he ever had to stay in the hospital? “No”
         7. How many times has he been to the ER with wheezing? “I think maybe twice since being diagnosed. He hasn’t been in several months.”
         8. How is his asthma treated? “He has two of those ‘puffer things.’ I can’t remember their names. One of them is that ‘buterol’ and I think the other one starts with and “f.”
         9. Do you know the doses of his medications? “Oh no – I can’t keep those straight.”
         10. How does he take his medications? “Well, when he was first diagnosed, he took one of them every day. Then he didn’t have attacks anymore. I thought his asthma must be getting better, so he quit the puffers for a while.”
         11. And how is his asthma doing now? “Now his attacks are bad again. He is wheezing, let’s see, about 3 times a week.
         12. And what do you do when he has an attack? “I give him those puffer things. A puff of each one.”
         13. And does that help? “Sometimes it does, and sometimes it doesn’t. If it doesn’t help within a few minutes, I give him another puff of each.”
         14. If asked why she did not return to original medication regimen: “Well, I couldn’t really remember which one he was supposed to take everyday. They look alike. I have instructions from the pharmacist, but the writing is so small…”
         15. Have you noticed if anything triggers Johnny’s asthma? “I don’t know – I have never thought about that before…” Do his symptoms seem to get worse with exercise, or being outdoors? What about cigarettes – does anyone in the home smoke? “Well I smoke. I didn’t know that might affect his wheezing.” Have you thought about quitting smoking? “Actually, I have. My own doctor has finally convinced me, and I am already smoking less than I used to. It is something that I am going to keep working on.”
         16. And how is your health? “I’m pretty healthy. I have high blood sugar and high blood pressure.”
         17. Do you take medications? “Yes – I take glipizide and amlodipine.”
         18. And where do you keep medications in the home? “Oh I know to keep them away from little Johnny. They are in a high cabinet in the kitchen.”
7. Instructor’s Notes
   1. The patient should be stable, and appear well, either sitting in a chair or on the exam table. The mannequin can answer questions if they are posed to him, but they should be relatively non-informative (Ex. How often do you feel sick like you can’t breath? “I don’t know…” Has it been getting worse? “maybe…” Can you keep up with your friends? “I like to play with friends.”)
   2. Tips to direct the standardized historians – the standardized historian(s) should be advised to act like a well-functioning, capable older adult caregivers, who are experiencing mild hearing, visual, and/or cognitive deficits that would be typical for a person their age. If the learner is not speaking in a slow, clear voice, the standardized historian should ask him to repeat the question, or act as if she does not hear him. If asked about when to give the patient his two medications, the standardized historian may state that she cannot read the pharmacist’s directions because the print is too small, or that she cannot adequately read the labels on the medications. When asked certain questions regarding the timeframe of the patient’s attacks, the standardized historian may take a minute to remember the answer. The deficits should not be so pronounced that the standardized historian’s ability to care for the patient is in question. When receiving the medical advice and/or instructions on how to properly give the patient his medications, the standardized historian may request that the directions be repeated. If asked to repeat back the instructions, the standardized historian may initially miss a step, but after further guidance, should act as though she fully understands and is competent to correctly administer the medication.
   4. The exercise should end after approximately 15 minutes, after the learner has elicited a history and provided anticipatory guidance regarding asthma and asthma medications. In the interest of time, the learner should be informed prior to beginning the case that a physical exam is not necessary. However, if the learner proceeds through a physical exam while simultaneously continuing the interview, the exercise should continue until the learner appears to be finished with the interview and finished providing any anticipatory guidance regarding asthma medications and triggers.
