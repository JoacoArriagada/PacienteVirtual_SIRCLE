**Standardized Patient Case:**

**RYAN HIGGINS**

**Identifying Case Factors:**

Presenting complaint: Brought to ER by his friend from base housing.

Length of patient encounter: 20 minutes

**SP Demographics:**

Name: Ryan Higgins

Gender: Male

Age: 22

Ethnicity: Any

Setting (i.e. clinic, ER, etc.): ER

SP Opening Statement: "My buddy brought me in."

History of present illness: 22 year-old man, active duty E4 Army corporal, brought to the emergency room at Womack Medical Center, Fort Bragg (NC), by his friend, Johnny, (who is also in the Army) after his wife called the Johnny expressing concern for the patient's safety. She told the friend that her husband had called her cell phone earlier in the night and left a message for his 2 year old daughter, saying "I'm sorry I've been such a bad father, and I love you." She tried calling him back, but he did not answer so she called Johnny. She told him that she was concerned that the patient has been "acting different" lately. The patient acknowledges that he probably called but says he does not remember what he said because "I had had a few beers." The patient was "just sitting on the couch, drinking beer" in his on-base apartment when his friend came by, told him that his wife had called and was frightened for him, and asked him to come with him to the emergency room. The patient says he had gone to work earlier in the day which was uneventful, had come home and then began drinking.

His wife took their daughter and moved out of the apartment to stay with friends a week ago because of the patient's recent behavior. The patient's older sister, with whom he was very close, died in a car accident three months ago. He has been acting different over the last few weeks-angrier, less interactive and drinking more alcohol. She tried to get him to seek help but he refused.

The patient felt sad when his sister died, but "thought I was doing okay." But he has struggled since a few weeks after the funeral. He got into a fight with his mother over funeral plans-his sister had expressed to him a desire to be cremated, but his mother insisted on a burial, and he relented. He feels guilty over this, and has not spoken to his mother or his younger siblings since the funeral.

The patient has not been sleeping well-a few hours of restless sleep per night. He has trouble falling asleep and staying asleep. He does not feel refreshed in the morning. He denies nightmares.

He has not been eating well-"pit in my stomach" but had been forcing himself to eat dinner with his wife and daughter every night until the last week. He denies significant weight loss. He feels somewhat anxious all the time over the last 2 weeks, and has had a "few" episodes of intense anxiety over the last week-his heart races, he is short of breath, he sweats and he feels tingling all over his body. These last for about a minute, then resolve. He had never had similar episodes prior to the last week.

He has been drinking more heavily recently. Before his sister's death, he drank 1-2 times per week, not on weeknights, usually "a couple beers" on Friday night and a 6-pack over the course of Saturday-"while watching the game" he would get a bit "buzzed" but "never really drunk." He has never had problems as a result of his drinking-no DUIs, no shakes or other withdrawal symptoms, no problems at work. Since her death, his drinking has increased-now drinks pretty much every night-probably a twelve-pack-for the past month, sometimes to the point of passing out. His last drink was about 2 hours ago just before his friend arrived. Tonight he was drinking slowly but steadily for several hours, but he is not acting overtly drunk now. He claims that he can't really remember calling his wife's cellphone or what he said because he'd been drinking. The truth is that he does not want to talk to the provider about it. If the provider continues to ask, he acknowledges that "yeah, I probably did call" and "my friend told me I worried my wife."

He is angry with his wife for moving out, but still loves her. He is upset at his wife for calling his friend -"So embarrassing"-but at some level also knows he needs help and that she wants that for him. He has never hit his wife, and had never really yelled at her until the week before she moved out. "We'd been having problems, but it's probably my fault." "She tries, but doesn't really understand." He denies thoughts of harming her, his daughter or anyone else. He acknowledges, "I've been so angry lately." He wants to get back together, but worries that "she may want a divorce, and I can't really blame her." When she left she said she didn't want their daughter around his drinking and yelling, and told him he needs to get himself together before she would come back.

He loves his daughter and feels guilty that he was not around when she was an infant-he was deployed when she was born, and for the first 6 months of her life. He feels guilty that he missed early milestones, and worries that he does not measure up to everything he would like to be as a father.

He has been going to work which involves significant physical fitness and weapons training. His job is basically to do whatever his sergeant says including tasks to get ready for the next deployment. Daily activities include physical fitness training, weapons training, cleaning weapons, doing inventory and getting trained on computer software. No one has mentioned any problems at work, but he says he doesn't think he's been paying attention very well.

He has thought about suicide, including having thoughts driving his car off the road. He is "not sure" how close he's gotten to killing himself, but "I'm just not sure I can take it anymore." He only vaguely remembers calling his wife's cell phone to wish his daughter goodbye: "I don't know what I said." He blames the lack of memory on the drinking, but he also does not really want to talk about the content of the call.

He has a gun in the house, but hasn't used it in awhile and isn't even sure where it is or whether he has any bullets.

He had been captain of the unit softball team, but gave up those duties around the time of his sister's death. "I don't even feel like playing anymore." He also enjoyed following football and baseball and had online fantasy teams, but "that all just seems like a waste of time."

Past medical history: No medical problems. "I work hard to stay in good shape." No prior psychiatric history other than ADHD as a child, and he successfully treated with Ritalin. No prior visits to a psychiatrist. No prior psychiatric hospitalizations. No prior suicide attempts or suicidal ideation. "The only other time when I felt real down was when I was homesick during bootcamp." He is not aware of any allergies. No history of head injury.

Family medical history: Didn't know his father-"into drugs real bad." Mother has emphysema, had cervical cancer-"women's cancer." Younger half-brother (10) has cerebral palsy. Not aware of any suicides in the family-"but I don't know half of 'em."

Social history: Grew up in rural West Virginia. Father left house when patient was an infant. He and his sister Tiffany (3 years older) were raised by a single mother who "had a different guy in the house every week or so when I was little." She married patient's step-father ("Bill") about 12 years ago and had 2 more children: Payton (10, with cerebral palsy) and Crystal (7, healthy). Very close with Tiffany growing up-"She was the only one I could trust."

A couple of his mother's boyfriends "hit me sometimes" when he was growing up-"Mom never did anything about it." His stepfather was not abusive and over the years he has gotten along with him pretty well.

Finances were always limited as a child-"we had enough to eat and stuff to wear, but that's about it."

He had some disciplinary problems in elementary school-"they put me on Ritalin for a while"-but behavior improved by middle school and no longer on medication by high school. B student in high school. Had several friends in high school, but "don't really keep in touch anymore." Excelled at sports, especially baseball.

He enlisted in military right after high school-he has been enlisted now for 3 years-as "my way out of a dead-end town" and "to earn some money." He is an infantry soldier with 82nd Airborne, stationed at Fort Bragg (N.C) where he met his wife. Married for 2 years; daughter, "Raleigh," is 2. He has been deployed once- his daughter was born during this deployment. He saw combat and killed enemy combatants. He did not get injured, but one of the soldiers in his unit was killed by an IED. He "was kinda close" to the soldier who died, but "got over it because I had to. We all had to." He does not experience flashbacks or nightmares about his combat, and only occasionally thinks about it. He does not easily startle, nor does he avoid places that might remind him of combat. He previously enjoyed being in the Army, but has begun questioning his career choice over the last month.

No legal problems currently or in the past other than a couple of speeding tickets.

He began drinking around age 15, mostly on weekends. He does not smoke. "I've never tried a drug in my life."

Physical examination findings (if indicated): Healthy, athletic, well-nourished, angry-appearing to sad-appearing at times, young man.

Information sharing/communication challenges: Angry and irritable at first, but masking an inner sadness and desperation. Initially a bit defensive but calms down and opens up to interviewer fairly quickly provided reasonable amount of empathy. Very formal, even when angry: "Yes, ma'am...No, sir."

Patient's response to special interviewing techniques:

"Have you ever tried to cut back on the amount of alcohol you drink?" "Not really-I haven't really had a problem with it."

"Have you ever felt angry when someone suggests you drink too much?" "No one's ever really said that except my wife lately. I was angry in general, not about that really."

"Have you ever had any guilty feelings over your drinking?" "Not really."

"Do you ever have a drink first thing in the morning, an eye-opener?" "Not really-I don't really need to drink."

"Have you ever thought about being dead or that life is not worth living any more?" "Yeah, I guess. It's hard to describe. It just sometimes seems like it's not worth it anymore."

Have you ever had any thoughts about committing suicide, hurting yourself or taking your own life? "Yeah, I guess I have."

Have you thought about a plan? "I've thought about smashing my car into a tree."

Have you actually tried to harm yourself? "No."

Do you have access to weapons? "I'm infantry, so yeah." "I have a gun at home, too, but I haven't used it in awhile."

How often do you have these thoughts? When last? "I guess I've been having those thoughts a lot-pretty much every day here lately."

How close have you come to acting on it? "I don't know. Sometimes I don't want to, but sometimes I find myself thinking it's the right thing to do."

What has prevented you from harming yourself so far? "My daughter and my wife. But now they might be gone."

How do you think other people might react if you were to kill yourself? "I don't really know."

What would happen if you were to go back home tonight? "I don't know, I'd probably try to go to sleep."

Can you tell me that you would not try to harm or kill yourself? "I just don't know anymore."

Have you had any thoughts of harming or killing anyone else? "In Afghanistan, yeah-that's my job. But no one here."

What's the worst thing you've ever done when you lost your temper? "Yelled, I guess. I don't really lose my temper."

Have you heard anything that other people don't hear or seen anything that other people don't see? "No, I'm not crazy."

Have you ever had times when you felt like you were in a really good mood? "Yeah, when I first met my daughter."

Have you ever had a time when you felt like you were on top of the world? "Not really."

Have you ever had the experience when your mind is racing or that you didn't need any sleep? "No."

I'd like to speak with your wife about what's been going on. Would that be okay with you? "I guess so."

Patient is oriented to person, place, date and time (knows his name, where he is, the date, etc). Patient can complete all items on a mini-mental status exam-can remember objects, can count backwards by 7s.

Patient says he is willing to consider treatment, if offered, including "maybe" coming into the hospital and "maybe" starting medicine, "as long as it doesn't affect my job."

Patient presentation (affect, appearance, position of patient at opening (i.e. sitting, laying down, holding abdomen etc.): When clinician enters, patient is sitting in chair, looking down, punching hand in fist angrily/nervously, taking deep breaths, nostrils flaring a bit. He appropriately responds when clinician introduces him/herself (e.g. shakes hand if offered, looks up and introduces himself when prompted). He is initially resistant to the idea of psychiatric treatment-worried that it means he is weak, or what it will mean for his military career. He remains seated for the interview, often looking down at floor, but occasionally making meaningful, heartfelt eye contact.

Specific body type/physical requirements for SP: healthy-appearing, well-nourished young man in reasonable physical condition (i.e, he is active duty soldier).

Props (i.e. test results, medication list, pregnancy pillow): None.

Make-up/moulage (please include application guidelines if available): Look as if patient has been tired, crying somewhat. Wearing clothes that suggest he was taken out of his house urgently-perhaps a t-shirt and shorts/sweat pants.

Standardized patients are trained on the importance of not volunteering checklist items. Standardized patients are also trained to answer compound/list questions ("have you had any fevers, chills, nausea, vomiting, headaches...") by choosing either the first or last item in the list and replying specifically, instead of giving a general "yes" or "no" answer.
