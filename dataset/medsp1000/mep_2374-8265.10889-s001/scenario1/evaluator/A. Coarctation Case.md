| **PATIENT NAME:** Isabella  **PATIENT AGE:** 3 weeks old  **CHIEF COMPLAINT:** shortness of breath and poor feeding | |
|  | |
| **Brief narrative description of case** | Isabella is a 3-week-old, ex full-term infant who presented to the ED with a 2-day history of respiratory distress and one-day history of poor feeding. In triage, the patient was noted to be febrile, tachycardic, tachypneic, and with transient pulse oximetry reading of 95% on room air per report. The ER assessment was that of bronchiolitis; a partial sepsis workup was also initiated. Patient is now admitted to the PICU as she seemed more distressed after urinary catheterization.  Overall learner goals are to identify a left ductal-dependent congenital cardiac lesion as a potential underlying etiology for an infant with fluid-refractory shock, understand and initiate prostaglandin therapy for treatment of known or suspected ductal-dependent lesions, and anticipate the respiratory consequences (i.e., apnea) of prostaglandin administration. |
| **Primary Learning Objectives** | Primary Objectives  1. Demonstrate appropriate initial recognition and management of neonatal shock (i.e., ABCs, fluid resuscitation, supplemental oxygen and sepsis evaluation)  2. Identify a neonate with critical coarctation of the aorta (i.e., by diminished or absent femoral pulses and peripheral persistent hypoxia) and demonstrate the correct management for a patient with hemodynamic compromise as a result of a ductal-dependent lesion utilizing prostaglandin  3. Anticipate the respiratory consequences (i.e., apnea) of prostaglandin administration by preparing for intubation utilizing appropriate techniques  Secondary Objectives  4. Establish the need for Cardiology and/or Cardiothoracic Surgery in the overall disposition of a patient with ductal-dependent lesions  5. Utilize techniques (i.e., “Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation” aka SBAR) and closed loop communication to work as a highly effective team unit during resuscitation |
| **Critical Actions** | Assess and manage the patient’s airway by verifying patency, evaluating the presence and effort of breathing, and initiating appropriate oxygen supplementation as needed (via nasal cannula, facemask, high-flow nasal cannula, CPAP or BiPAP)  Initiate cardiopulmonary monitoring (if not on monitor already) and correctly identify the presence of sinus tachycardia, tachypnea, persistent hypoxia, and hypotension  Establish IV access; if peripheral IV cannot be established rapidly ( < 90 seconds) then intraosseous access must be obtained  Initiate fluid resuscitation in the setting of neonatal shock; appreciate that the patient continues to have persistent tachycardia, tachypnea, and hypoxia despite subsequent fluid resuscitation  Verbalize a differential diagnosis for neonatal shock including sepsis, ductal dependent lesions, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and metabolic disorders.  Identify the neonate with critical coarctation of the aorta by diminished/absent femoral pulses, persistent hypoxia, and discordant blood pressures of the upper and lower extremities  Initiate the correct therapy for a ductal dependent congenital cardiac lesion by starting continuous infusion of prostaglandin E1 (with a dose of 0.025 to 0.05 mcg/kg/min and titrating as needed to a maximum dose of 0.1 mcg/kg/min)  Anticipate the respiratory consequences (i.e., apnea) of prostaglandin administration by preparing/performing intubation utilizing appropriate technique; recognize that the obtunded or moribund patient may need no sedative medications to accomplish intubation.  Involve Cardiology and/or Cardiothoracic Surgery in the care of the patient who will need eventual corrective cardiac surgery  Other actions that may be taken throughout this case are:  - Initiating inotropic support (with epinephrine, dobutamine or dopamine)  - Drawing labs (e.g., ABG, CMP CBC,) or obtaining imaging (e.g., CXR) to help establish underlying cause of patient’s acute presentation  - Administering glucose in a patient with history of poor feeding and hypoglycemia  - Administering bicarbonate in patient with metabolic (lactic) acidosis  - Broadening antibiotic coverage in the setting of treatment-refractory shock |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

*The nurses initially enter the room to find a patient in significant respiratory distress. After initial assessment (ABCs) of baby and conversation with mother (standardized patient), the nurses call in the pediatric residents to admit the patient and communicate pertinent medical information. The pediatric residents complete a focused history and physical examination. They place the patient on a bedside monitor and recognize that the patient is tachycardia, hypoxic, and hypotensive. Supplemental oxygen is provided via facemask and respiratory therapy is paged/called to bring CPAP. IV access is obtained/attempted. Upon recognition of shock (poor perfusion), the Pediatric ICU Fellow is called and pertinent medical information is communicated once more using SBAR. An IV fluid bolus is ordered. The patient’s respiratory distress does not resolve, and hypotension is refractory to IV fluids. Lab work shows leukocytosis with a left shift and an elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis. CXR shows clear lungs but mild cardiomegaly. A differential diagnosis is established and should include congenital cardiac lesions. Upon realization of discordant pulses and blood pressures in the upper and lower extremities, participants should consider critical coarctation of the aorta as the most likely underlying etiology in this patient’s thus far treatment-refractory shock. A continuous infusion of Prostaglandin E1 should be initiated for this presumed ductal-dependent lesion. Due to the risk of prostaglandin-induced apnea, the team prepares should the patient need to be intubated and started on mechanical ventilation. Cardiology/Cardiothoracic Surgery should be informed given that urgent corrective surgery will be the patient’s ultimate treatment.*

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. *Difficulty arriving at diagnosis of critical coarctation of the aorta: We found that some of our learners had difficulty establishing aortic coarctation as the leading differential. Some learners did not attempt to obtain blood pressures in both the upper and lower extremities. Some learners were unable to palpate lower extremity pulses (as designed for the simulation) but incorrectly concluded that this was an artifact of the manikin.*
2. *Failure to consider the need for intubation in the setting of Prostaglandin administration: We found that some of our learners were not aware of prostaglandin-induced apnea. As such, after prostaglandin administration, the learners were not immediately prepared, and intubation was delayed as a result.*
3. *Failure to recognize that unresponsive neonates can be intubated without medication: We found that some of our learners began to ask for medications to initiate rapid sequence intubation once it was deemed necessary to intubate the baby. This is may be unnecessary in minimally responsive babies and may delay time-sensitive intubation and compromise hemodynamics further.*
