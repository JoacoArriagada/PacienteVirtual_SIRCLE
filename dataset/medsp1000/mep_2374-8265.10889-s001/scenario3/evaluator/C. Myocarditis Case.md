| **Brief narrative description of case** | Enrique is a 5-year-old boy with no significant past medical history who presents with a 4 day history of progressively decreasing activity, exercise intolerance, and decreased urine output. In the ED, patient was noted to be moderately dehydrated on exam. He was admitted to the general Pediatric floor for rehydration in the setting of a suspected viral illness. While on the floor, the nurse called a rapid response because patient looked ill; a fluid bolus was started, and the PICU was called to evaluate.  Overall learner goals are to recognize cardiogenic shock, to initiate appropriate therapy for a patient with cardiogenic shock secondary to myocarditis specifically by limiting fluid administration and providing early inotropic support, and to appreciate that patients with myocarditis can be tenuous and prone to dysrhythmia/arrest with or without intubation. |
| **Primary Learning Objectives** | Primary Objectives   1. Distinguish cardiogenic shock as a separate entity from other types of shock 2. Initiate early inotropic support and to limit fluid administration in the setting of cardiogenic shock 3. Demonstrate appreciation that patients with myocarditis can be tenuous and prone to dysrhythmia/arrest with or without intubation.   Secondary Objectives   1. Demonstrate understanding of the effects of positive pressure ventilation on left ventricular function 2. Utilize techniques (i.e., “Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation” aka SBAR) and closed loop communication to work as a highly effective team unit during resuscitation |
| **Critical Actions** | Assess and manage the patient’s airway by verifying patency, evaluating the presence and effort of breathing, and initiating appropriate oxygen supplementation as needed (via nasal cannula, facemask, high-flow nasal cannula, CPAP or BiPAP)  Connect patient to cardiopulmonary monitor (if not on monitor already) and correctly identify the presence of sinus tachycardia and tachypnea with careful attention to the development of PVC’s  Establish IV access; if peripheral IV cannot be established rapidly then intraosseous access must be obtained  Consider initiating fluid resuscitation in the setting of potential shock; appreciate that the patient continues to have persistent tachycardia and worsening respiratory distress (i.e., tachypnea, retractions, and hypoxia) after fluid resuscitation. Careful reassessment for development of hepatomegaly, new gallop, new JVD, or new crackles.  Abort fluid administration and verbalize a differential diagnosis for shock worsened by fluid resuscitation including cardiogenic shock (i.e., myocarditis)  Obtain chest x-ray in the setting of decompensating patient with worsening hypoxia; appreciate cardiomegaly, pleural effusion, and increased pulmonary vasculature suggesting underlying cardiac etiology of patient’s condition  Administer inotropic medication (e.g., epinephrine, milrinone, dobutamine, dopamine,) to help improve cardiac contractility/function  Administer diuretics (e.g., Lasix) to help relieve fluid overload in the lungs  Given persistent symptoms, prepare for rapid sequence intubation; select appropriate sedative/paralytic agents (e.g., Etomidate; Fentanyl/ ±versed; rocuronium). In anticipation of hypotension with sedative drugs or in response to decreased venous return, phenylephrine should be available at the bedside.  Recognize multiple runs of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia on the monitor secondary to myocardial irritability and vasoactive medication; select appropriate antiarrhythmic agent (e.g., lidocaine, amiodarone); defibrillator pads should be placed on the patient or, at the least, made available at the bedside.  Other actions that may be taken throughout this case:  - Considering drawing labs (e.g., BMP, lactate, blood culture) or obtaining other studies (EKG, ECHO) to help establish underlying cause of patient’s acute presentation  - Consulting Cardiology in the care of patient with suspected myocarditis and subsequent cardiogenic shock |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

*The nurses initially enter the room to find a patient in mild respiratory distress. After initial assessment (ABCs) of patient and conversation with mother (standardized patient), the nurses call in the pediatric residents to assess the patient and communicate pertinent medical information. The pediatric residents complete a focused history and physical examination. They place the patient on a bedside monitor and recognize that the patient is tachycardic and tachypneic. The pediatric resident attempts fluid resuscitation by ordering a NS bolus. The patient’s tachycardia and tachypnea worsen, and the oxygen saturation starts to drop. As such, the Pediatric ICU Fellow is called, and pertinent medical information is communicated once more. Upon hearing that the patient’s status worsened with a fluid bolus, the fellow discontinues fluids, starts supplemental O2, and orders a chest x-ray and labs. The chest x-ray shows cardiomegaly and pleural effusion, which confirms the Pediatric ICU Fellow’s suspicion that the patient is in cardiogenic shock. Inotropic medications (e.g., epinephrine, dobutamine, milrinone) and diuretics (e.g., furosemide) are given, but the patient’s clinical status does not improve. Eventually, the patient’s blood pressure has dropped so low that the Pediatric ICU Fellow begins vasoactive medication (e.g., epinephrine). The blood pressure improves, but the patient’s respiratory status is still worsening and the frequency of PVC’s increase. The Pediatric ICU Fellow should call for antiarrhythmic (e.g., lidocaine or amiodarone) and the defibrillator in anticipation of progression to a fatal arrhythmia. Consequently, the Pediatric ICU Fellow performs rapid sequence intubation, preparing for cardiovascular collapse (administer phenylephrine, increase epinephrine ± fluid administration) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (amiodarone at bedside and defibrillator pads on). At this point, the simulation will end.*

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. *Delay in recognition of cardiogenic shock: Some of our learners had difficulty establishing a timely diagnosis of cardiogenic shock. As such, some learners continued to provide aggressive fluid resuscitation, which only serves to worsen this particular patient’s clinical status. Additionally, this delayed the prompt initiation of inotropic medication support.*
2. *Failure to recognize the need for vasoactive medication/intubation: Some of our learners experienced significant delays and hesitation in initiating vasoactive medication/intubation. In this scenario, the simulation technician will continue to drop the blood pressure and oxygen saturation in an attempt to provoke the team towards vasoactive administration/intubation.*
3. *Failure to recognize the potential untoward consequences of vasoactive medication:**Some of our learners did not anticipate that these treatments might heighten the excitability of the myocardial tissue in myocarditis and thus lead to dysrhythmias. As such, some learners did not call for antiarrhythmic agents or for the defibrillator.*
