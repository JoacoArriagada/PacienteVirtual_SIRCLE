# Appendix B: Guide to Station B – Atrial Fibrillation case

I. Title: Appendix B: Guide to Station B – Atrial Fibrillation case

II. Target audience: medical students in clinical years

III. Learning objectives

A. Primary 1. Recognize the clinical risks of atrial fibrillation 2. Perform an initial workup of new-onset atrial fibrillation 3. Manage atrial fibrillation at initial presentation in the context of validated risk scores 4. Practice prioritizing tasks and interventions in a time-limited scenario

B. Secondary

1. Recognize the characteristic atrial fibrillation pattern on an electrocardiogram   
2. Communicate reasoning within a team where opinions on optimal management may differ   
3. Communicate a plan with a patient for a likely but unconfirmed diagnosis   
4. Formulate a plan in the context of conflicting external advice

C. Critical Actions Checklist

1. Ask patient past medical history questions to fully determine CHADS2-VASc score   
2. Recognize tachycardia amid flurry of other information   
3. Request electrocardiogram (EKG)   
4. Initiate medication to control tachycardia immediately

IV. Environment

A. Lab Set Up – primary care physician (PCP) office   
B. Manikin Set Up – either live actors or a manikin may be used 1. The manikin should be pre-programmed with the vital signs listed below. Of note, the manikin should have an irregular pulse and heart rate to reflect atrial fibrillation. The manikin may also have symptoms of a URI, which the patient also endorses. Otherwise, no additional settings need to be programmed. 2. The standard simulation room should have either a one-way observation mirror through which faculty and staff operating the control panel/laptop with manikin software can observe participants, or a camera (web-cam) for which the faculty and staff can observe the participants from behind a partition. Faculty and staff may speak “for” the manikin through a microphone at the control panel, which will sound from a speaker in or near the manikin’s mouth, allowing the manikin to “answer” participants’ questions. The simulation room itself is set up to resemble a standard outpatient clinical office.

V. Actors

C. Urgent Care Physician 1. May be played by a resident, student, or actor 2. Serves to distract the learners by calling them midway through the scenario and encourages them to cardiovert the patient

VI. Case Narrative

A. Scenario Background Given to Participants (must be asked for) 1. Chief complaint a. Asymptomatic tachycardia, discovered at urgent care center b. Cold-like symptoms, for which she originally presented to urgent care

2. Past medical history

a. Mild claudication upon heavy exertion, no treatment   
b. Hypertension, treated with ACEI but poorly controlled   
c. No diabetes   
d. No prior CVA/stroke/TIA   
e. No CHF   
f. s/p hysterectomy in 1998 for post-menopausal bleeding (benign etiology)   
g. s/p appendectomy in 1956

3. Meds and allergies a. Medication: Lisinopril 10mg once daily b. Allergy: NKDA

4. Family/social history

C. Scenario branch points

1. Changes in patient’s condition

a. Patient’s blood pressure and symptoms remain stable throughout scenario.   
b. Accordingly, it is not anticipated that the patient will receive medications or therapies except in accordance with the learners’ final plan, which ends the scenario.   
c. Patient expresses anxiety about her unclear diagnosis and plan, prompting learners to counsel her based on the diagnosis they agree upon.

2. At three minutes into the scenario, students should have elicited a history of present illness (HPI) and brief past medical history (PMH). Seeing the patient’s EKG is the next crucial step for this scenario.

a. If by this point they have not asked for an EKG:

(i) The nurse (scenario confederate) will ask if an EKG would be helpful, and then provide the students with the EKG taken at the urgent care clinic (without a formal read).   
(ii) Because an objective of the simulation is to learn to manage atrial fibrillation, students should be prompted to request the study that would help identify that condition.

b. If the students have asked for an EKG:

(i) The nurse will provide an EKG taken at the urgent care clinic (without a formal read).

3. At five minutes into the scenario, with the aid of the EKG, students should have identified atrial fibrillation as the patient’s cardiac abnormality, and begun formulating a plan in accordance with this.

a. If they have not identified her primary problem as atrial fibrillation:

(i) The urgent care doctor (scenario confederate) will call the learners and suggest immediate cardioversion for the patient’s confirmed atrial fibrillation. (ii) This event will prompt learners to make the binary decision of whether cardioversion is appropriate or not, which may catalyze their plan formation. b. If they have identified her primary problem as atrial fibrillation: (i) The urgent care doctor will call the learners and ask what the plan is, because he is worried for the patient’s safety. He will push the plan of urgent cardioversion.

4. At ten minutes into the scenario, students should have a complete assessment and plan.

a. If they have not developed an assessment and plan:

(i) The nurse will tell the students that their clinic is starting, and they must electronically document their assessment, plan, and initial laboratory studies to be performed (if any). Before they leave, they must explain the assessment and plan to the patient, based on their collective discussion. b. If they have developed an assessment and plan: (i) The nurse will ask the students for any labs they wish to order. In addition, they must explain the assessment and plan to the patient, based on their collective discussion.

VII. Instructors Notes

A. Tips to keep the scenario flowing   
1. As outlined in Section VI B 2-4, the confederates will recognize specific times during the scenario at which point they will intervene to keep the scenario flowing. Should the learners get off-track, these safeguards will return them to the salient teaching points.   
2. A primary goal of the station is to practice decision-making and prioritization under time constraints, so difficulty in completing each portion of the scenario thoroughly is to be expected. B. Tips to direct actors:   
1. Patient a. Ms. Heffernan is initially not overly concerned about her symptoms, but she is worried about the urgent care doctor’s strong reaction to her EKG. As the scenario progresses, Ms. Heffernan should display increasing anxiety about the gravity of her newlydiscovered condition.

b. For any specific questions outside the scope of the information provided above, answer “I don’t know” or “I don’t remember.” c. Aside from the URI symptoms above, Ms. Heffernan is totally asymptomatic.

2. Nurse

a. The nurse should remain largely in the background, speaking up only to provide relevant information (i.e. vitals, EKG), to direct students according to the branch points as outlined in Section VI B 2-4, and to instruct them on the scenario’s limitations (i.e. no echocardiogram machine available).   
b. For any specific questions outside the scope of the information provided above, answer “I don’t know”.   
c. No other imaging or diagnostic tools are available aside from those specifically listed above.

3. Urgent care doctor

a. The doctor should be relatively aggressive in his phone conversation with the students, urging them to cardiovert the patient for her safety. Although this is the wrong option of management, the doctor should sound firm, in order to test the knowledge, understanding and conviction of the learners. b. The urgent care doctor should limit his comments to the tachycardia, brushing aside comments about other complaints or data by saying “I’m not worried about that”. He should focus on the (erroneous) concern that sustained atrial fibrillation will degenerate into a life-threatening arrhythmia. C. Scenario programming 1. The scenario is linear in structure, and so the potential for error paths is low. The branch points above are intended to continually re-direct the students in the scenario in order for an assessment and plan to be the scenario endpoint.

VIII.Debriefing Plan

A. Method of debriefing 1. Ideally, all of the students (from multiple separate scenario iterations) will congregate to debrief as a large group (see the primary Instructor’s guide and Appendix D). This is primarily a verbal debrief.   
B. Actual debriefing materials 1. Students’ scenario responses—their written differentials, plans, and next steps—will be the main aid during debrief. The electronic responses will be displayed via projector for the whole group to see (see the primary Instructor’s guide for details). 2. Appendix H—a copy of the patient’s vital signs and original EKG from the scenario 3. Appendix E—a list of the all the laboratory test values and final reports of imaging that students may have ordered during the scenario 4. Appendix F—an outline of the major questions raised (with answers)—is to be provided at the conclusion of the debrief for students’ review.   
C. Rules for the debriefing 1. The instructor will review the case scenario for one minute to refresh the students on the details. 2. Following this, a free-form question and answer session will take place, guided by the questions below. 3. To conclude, the instructor will briefly highlight the key learning points of the exercise.   
D. Potential questions to facilitate the debriefing 1. What are the reversible causes of atrial fibrillation? 2. Should our patient, Ms. Heffernan, have been cardioverted? Why or why not? 3. What imaging/labs are necessary in the initial workup of atrial fibrillation? 4. What is the CHADS2-VASc score? 5. What are the three initial treatment goals of newly-discovered atrial fibrillation?
