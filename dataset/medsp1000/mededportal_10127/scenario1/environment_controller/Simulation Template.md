**Hydrofluoric Acid Burn Simulation Case**

An emergency medicine high-fidelity simulation scenario

1. **TITLE:** Hydrofluoric Acid Burn
4. **ENVIRONMENT**
   1. **Lab Set Up** – Community Emergency Department bed in a simulation center or actual department.
   2. **Manikin Set Up** – The manikin will appear as a 32-year-old clothed male who will be positioned in a supine position on the bed. He will have one 20-gauge peripheral IV line placed in the left hand (by EMS).
   3. **Props** – The following items should be readily available either at the bedside or rapidly upon request by the learners:
      1. Cardiac monitor with the ability to monitor telemetry, pulse oximetry, and blood pressure
      2. Supplemental oxygen with nasal cannula and non-rebreather mask
      3. Airway equipment to allow for oxygen delivery and endotracheal intubation
      4. Equipment needed for establishing peripheral and central intravenous access
      5. ABG kit
      6. IV fluids and tubing
      7. Fully-stocked code-blue crash cart, specifically including:
         1. Regular Insulin and D50
         2. Sodium Bicarbonate
         3. Albuterol with nebulizer
         4. Kayexalate
         5. Calcium chloride
         6. Calcium gluconate
         7. TUMS with mortar/pestle to prepare calcium carbonate gel
         8. KY Jelly to prepare calcium carbonate gel
      8. Water and basin for decontamination of the hand
   4. **Audiovisual** – (See Appendix A) As learners complete a physical examination of the affected hand, the photo of hydrofluoric acid exposure to the skin should be shown to the learners. Other items will be available to the learners only if they request them. These items will include: initial and serial EKGs, chest x-ray, and laboratory results.
   5. **Distractors** – Nursing staff can resist the need for proper decontamination using water since no material can be visualized on the skin. The nurse can suggest that everyone “just wear gloves” instead.
5. **ACTORS**:
   1. **Roles** – The number of participants in the scenario can range from a single provider to several providers and support staff. The ideal number of actors are:
      1. One lead physician who will direct the care of the patient
      2. One support physician who will help with the care of the patient
      3. One to two nurses who will carry out the instructions of the lead physician
      4. One paramedic to provide the initial radio report to the team
      5. One phone consultant (ICU physician, burn surgeon, and toxicologist)
   2. **Who may play them** – The treating team (physicians and nurses) may be played by any of the learners. The role of the paramedic and all phone consultants should be played by an individual competent in hydrofluoric acid toxicity and management and may include a senior resident, attending physician, or the simulation case instructor.
   3. **Actions for each role**:
      1. Lead physician – This individual will serve as the main participant and will direct all scenario activities. This individual should directly perform a focused history and physical examination and provide direction for testing and treatment. This individual can delegate tasks and procedures.
      2. Supporting physician – This individual will assist the lead physician in assessing and treating the patient. This individual may also collaborate with the lead physician on clinical decision making, though the lead physician should still be recognized as the authority throughout the scenario.
      3. Nurse – This individual will be responsible for obtaining IV access, attaching monitoring equipment, and providing medications as directed by the lead physician. The nurse can also participate in clinical decision making, though the lead physician should still be recognized as the authority throughout the scenario.
      4. Paramedic – This individual will provide an initial radio report prior to the patient arriving at the hospital to allow staff to prepare for the patient’s arrival. The paramedic will also be available for about 2 minutes after patient arrival to answer questions about the prehospital care but will then leave and be unavailable for the rest of the scenario.
      5. Phone consultants – This individual will provide phone (not bedside) consultation to the learners as requested. Phone consultants should not be available until the learners have at least thoroughly attempted an appropriate history and physical examination, cardiac and laboratory evaluation, and initiated some form of treatment. Consultants should provide answers only to focused and specific questions or they can redirect and prompt the team towards appropriate assessment methods and treatment modalities.
6. **CASE NARRATIVE**
   1. **Scenario Background**
      1. Chief Complaint: Pain to right hand after chemical spill at a glass etching plant
      2. Medic Report (given freely): “Hi Doc. This is paramedic Smith – I have a 5 minute ETA with a 32-year old male patient who is coming from a local glass etching plant who appears to have spilled a chemical on his right hand. He is a new employee and we met him in the parking lot where seemed to be in a lot of pain. He’s unsure what chemical he spilled on himself other than it may contain fluoride of some sort. Vitals are stable, but he sure is in a lot of pain. I’m just now getting a line but we should see you in less than 5.” *When asked about decontamination, the paramedic should state he didn’t think he needed to decontaminate the patient on-scene.*
      3. Vital Signs: HR 112, BP 150/95, RR 18, SpO2 96% RA, T 37 oral
      4. Demographics: 32 y/o male weighing 70kg
      5. Past Medical History: Asthma
      6. Medications: Albuterol HFA inhaler
      7. Allergies: NKDA
      8. Family/Social History: Maternal grandmother with cardiovascular disease and father with history of alcohol and substance abuse
   2. **Initial Scenario Conditions**
      1. **History Given by patient**: Patient reports that he was at work when one of the employees tripped and fell, spilling the chemical he was carrying. The patient reacted by reaching out to catch the container and the chemical spilled and splashed his right hand. He noted the container had the word “fluoric” on it but cannot recall the full name of them chemical and is unfamiliar with the chemical’s use. He estimates about 500cc spilled from the container and he thinks he was only splashed on his hand. He does not think he inhaled any of it. Within 2 minutes he began to run cool water over the exposed skin, but after about 10 minutes developed a severe burning sensation on his right hand where the chemical contacted his skin. The patient has only been working at this plant for two days so he is unfamiliar with the chemicals used at the plant. He currently is in severe distress due to pain and repeatedly asks the physician to help with the pain.
      2. **Associated symptoms (given during review of systems):** The patient complains of severe pain to the right hand, but denies paresthesias to the distal hand. Remainder of the review of systems is negative.
      3. **Initial Exam**
         1. **General:** The patient is a well-developed 70kg adult male who is moaning in pain and appears very uncomfortable
         2. **HEENT:** Pupils are equal. Normal vision. Moist mucous membranes.
         3. **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardic. No murmurs
         4. **Respiratory:** Breath sounds are clear and equally bilaterally. No wheezes, rhonchi or rales
         5. **Abdominal:** Soft, non-tender, non-distended. Normal bowel sounds
         6. **Genitourinary:** Normal appearing
         7. **Neurological:** Alert and oriented x3. Non-focal examination of gross motor function and sensation. + Chvostek’s sign. + Trousseau’s sign
         8. **Skin:** Right hand appears moderately erythematous without signs of deep burns on examination. There are areas of hypopigmented and edematous tissue over the index finger, which appear to reflect about 1% of his body surface area. No crepitus. No lacerations or signs of blunt trauma are present
      4. **Vital Signs**  - as noted above in “scenario background”
   3. **Scenario Branch Points**
      1. **Decontamination.** The learner should ask if the patient was decontaminated at the scene prior to ED arrival. The learner should specifically ask about the method of decontamination and duration of decontamination. Appropriate decontamination would include removing all clothing and jewelry, running clean water over the exposure site for 30 minutes. Since the patient did not get appropriate decontamination with water for the full 30 minutes at the scene, he should complete decontamination in the ED. If all potentially contaminated clothing and/or jewelry is not removed, then the nurse (and anyone else who touches the patient’s hand or contaminated clothing either with their bare hands or when wearing latex gloves) will develop a burning sensation on their hands. Instructors can consider then removing the provider who touched the uncontaminated patient’s hand and replacing them with another unaffected provider. If the group is small, the instructor can advise the team that they are now noting a burning sensation in their hands to prompt decontamination. The use of a chemical-resistant glove can be used to safely touch the patient prior to adequate decontamination if the learners ask for such gloves.
      2. **Pain.** If the learners give rapid and aggressive intravenous opiate analgesia then the patient will report improvement (but not resolution) of his pain. If the patient is re-dosed with opioids he should become sleepy, not minimal improvement, ask as “isn’t there anything else you can do?” as a prompt for topical calcium gel. If calcium gel is applied in a glove or intra-arterial calcium is provided, the patient will note complete resolution of pain. If, however, no IV opioids are given then the patient will become more agitated and refuse to answer questions until his pain is addressed.
      3. **Hypocalcemia:** A point-of-care calcium should be requested within 1-2 minutes of recognition of hydrofluoric acid as the chemical of exposure. Its results should be provided within one minute of request and a low calcium should prompt administration of at least 2 amps of either calcium gluconate IV or calcium chloride (which must be given through a central line). If only one amp is given, the instructor should state that a nurse walking by comments “last time we had someone from that plant, we ended up cleaning out our supply of calcium!” If electrolytes are only sent to the laboratory but not performed as a point-of-care, the patient should become hypotensive (BP 100/55) and develop carpal spam. If the patient is still not treated with parenteral calcium, the patient should become more hypotensive and if still not treated, should develop a wide-complex tachycardia and progress to cardiac arrest.
      4. **Hyperkalemia**: A point-of-care potassium should be requested within 1-2 minutes of recognition of hydrofluoric acid as the chemical of exposure. Its results should be provided within one minute of request and a high potassium should prompt for standard treatment of hyperkalemia with calcium (which will also help address hypocalcemia), nebulized albuterol 10mg, regular insulin 10 units and D50 1 ampule IV, sodium bicarbonate 50mEq IV, and kayexalate 15grams PO. If hyperkalemia is not treated, the patient should become hypotensive (BP 100/55) and should develop a wide complex tachycardia. If he remains untreated he should progress to cardiac arrest.
      5. **Consultation of specialists:** A toxicologist from the poison center, a critical care physician, and a burn surgeon will be available by telephone, but only after the patient has had a history and physical examination obtained, labs resulted, and some form of treatment at least attempted.
      6. **Disposition of the patient:** The patient should be admitted to either a burn ICU or a standard ICU with the ability to have a burn surgeon bedside consultation. If the team attempts to admit the patient to the floor, the hospitalist should be very uncomfortable with the admission, ask questions on management outside of the ED, long-term prognosis, and ultimately suggest a critical care admission.
7. **INSTRUCTOR NOTES:**
   1. **Scenario Flow** – The instructor can interject the following information:
      1. If learners progress through the scenario without identifying the hydrofluoric acid, the instructor may call the ED as the patient’s plant manager. The plant manager should tell the lead physician that the hydrofluoric acid is 60% (industrial strength) and that he knows even a small amount could be deadly if not treated quickly.
      2. If learners do not recognize the potential for hypocalcemia and hyperkalemia, the patient should become hypotensive and the instructor can either show a wide complex tachycardia on telemetry.
      3. If the learners are less advanced learners who have no knowledge of hydrofluoric acid toxicity, consultants can be more open and provide guidance at an earlier time.
  1. **Scenario Programming** – No advanced setting will be required for a manikin or high-fidelity simulator, other than manipulation of vital signs and the telemetry waveforms.
