VIII. Debriefing Plan

A. Method of debriefing – After each case, the group of students in the lab will join the other three groups in the observation area for a large group debriefing. As designed, the students will receive immediate feedback regarding the correct answers/actions for each question incorporated into the scenarios from the faculty facilitator in the lab. The debriefing session will serve to address the three aspects of students’ participation.1

1. As a person – Ask the members of the team providing hands-on care how they felt about the simulation in order to get their personal reactions.
2. In the role they simulated – Ask the care team members to describe their thought process leading to the actions taken. Since their roles are limited by their clinical experience to taking a history and performing an exam, they can address the history elements they

chose to ask about, their findings on exam, their interpretation of physiologic parameters which led to the decisions they made, and the actions they recommended. Allow the peripheral learners in the classroom, who viewed the simulation, to give their feedback and to ask questions of both the students who participated in the simulation and the faculty facilitator.

3. As a student – Determine which questions the students had problems with or found confusing during the scenario. Discuss these items while relating them to the accompanying readings, the course didactic sessions, and the learning objectives for the simulation.

B. Rules for the debriefing

1. One person speaks at a time.
2. Be respectful of other students.
3. Any disagreements regarding potential alternative correct choices for the questions asked during the simulation will be addressed in front of the group. If there is ongoing concern after initial discussion, the students will submit a written request for consideration of an alternate answer outside the debriefing.

C. Questions to facilitate the debriefing

1. How did you feel when you first approached the patient?
2. What factors led to your conclusions regarding the patient’s underlying problem?
3. What factors led to your recommended course of action?
4. What would you do differently the next time?
5. How did your curricular material on traumatic and mechanical respiratory problems help prepare you for this simulation?
6. What did you learn from this simulation?

IX. Pilot Testing and Revisions

A. Numbers of participants – Sessions were tested by senior emergency medicine residents and faculty for content, flow, and timing prior to presentation of the student laboratory. Subsequently, five, two-hour, sessions were conducted in a single day with groups of 12 to 24 secondyear medical students per session (total of 100 students). During each session, the students were divided into four groups per session. One group participated in a hands-on experience in the lab for one of the four cases while the remaining three groups observed by an audiovisual connection in an adjacent room (Figure). At the end of each debriefing, a new group rotated to the lab for their hands-on experience with another one of the four cases.
B. Performance expectations, anticipated management mistakes – As second year medical students, we do not expect them to know advanced

diagnostic procedures or all of the specific aspects of therapy. A faculty preceptor will be in the lab with the students to focus the students’ efforts and redirect them as needed to complete each case. The questions presented to the students will be based on the material provided previously in the course. Any mistaken diagnoses will be addressed real-time with the immediate feedback from the instructor. C. Evaluation form for participants – The students will be given a standardized evaluation form at the end of the simulation session to provide their feedback (Appendix E).

RESUME SCENARIO: Prompt students to order a CXR if they haven’t done so. Discuss how the film findings correlate with the pathophysiology of aspiration pneumonia and the findings on physical exam. (The views show shadowing in the right lung, radiating from the right hilum and consistent with a right lower/middle lobe infiltrate. This correlates with rales heard on auscultating the right lung with normal breath sounds on the left).

PAUSE SCENARIO: Question 2: What is the most appropriate pharmacologic treatment for this patient? (Answer – A broad spectrum antibiotic combination that includes coverage of anaerobes) Guide a brief student discussion to help them evaluate the special conditions (aspiration pneumonia [providing a mix of aerobes and anaerobes] and an infection acquired in a healthcare facility [with increased risk for resistant organisms]). Once all the tasks are completed, each can briefly share what changes he/she saw.

Next direct the students to answer Question 4: What is the most like cause of this patient’s hypoxia? (Answer - Aspirated fluid disrupts surfactant and initiates an inflammatory response leading to V/Q mismatch). Guide a brief student discussion to help them arrive at the answer if not offered initially.
