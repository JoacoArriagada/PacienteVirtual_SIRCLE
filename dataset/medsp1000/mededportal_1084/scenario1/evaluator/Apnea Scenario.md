# Pediatric Scenario – Apnea due to Intoxication

# Objectives:

1. Recognize apnea as a presentation of drug ingestion.   
2. Manage apnea in a child.   
3. Understand the appropriate use of antidotes available for certain medication overdoses.

Target Audience: Emergency medicine or pediatric residents, paramedics (although rapid sequence intubation is not consistently within a paramedic scope-of-practice)

Scenario: 6 year old is brought to the emergency department by an ambulance having been found unresponsive at his grandmother’s house. He is stable en route.

Further history if asked: No history of trauma. Grandmother is his normal after-school babysitter. (If subject asks specifically about drugs in home, respond with “An uncle in the home is newly in a methadone treatment program.”)

Past medical history is unremarkable.   
Meds: None   
No allergies.   
Family and social history: as above, otherwise non-contributory Development: normal

# Environment

1. ED setting – bed, monitor, crash cart, IV pole.   
2. Manikin Setup – child on bed without any monitors on, “patient just placed in room”.   
3. Props – ECGs needed – can be provided from sim (Laerdal) or printed – sinus bradycardia with a rate of 80. No specific lab sheets are needed as labs would all be normal. Urine drug screen is negative.   
4. Distractors – none.

# Actors

1. Roles – nurse required   
2. Nurse provides support – no specific prompts and is a “new nurse” so not very helpful. When asked for bag-mask, for example, he/she hand the subject all the bag-masks in the drawer rather than selecting the correct one.   
3. Parent (optional) – to provide history – otherwise nurse provides history and states grandmother is unavailable. Grandmother is still at home.

Say the following: “Child is comatose and has slight perioral cyanosis – no response to deep, painful stimulus.”

Physical Exam: This information is for the instructor(s) – most of the exam will be obtained from examination of the mannequin. Offer the general appearance and only offer additional clarification if asked.

Wt: estimated $2 5 \mathrm { k g }$ (only if asked)

Airway: Patent Breathing: Shallow, hypopneic respirations. Circulation: normal, slightly delayed capillary refill Pinpoint pupils noted (eyes closed)

# Instructors Notes

\*SOAP – suction, oxygen equipment, airway equipment, and pharmacy (medications).

Case evolution is limited – Child starts with vitals given above.

The goal of the assessment is to have the case go through to intubation, however, in formative use, one can choose to emphasize the initial airway management (bagging) or the intubation, depending on the level of the learner.

If naloxone is given at ${ > } 1 0 ^ { }$ -fold normal dose, patient should have a transient improvement and then return to apnea after ${ \sim } 3 { - } 4$ minutes. The likelihood that a resident will be aware of the literature about high dose naloxone is quite small. Alternately, one could simply have naloxone not work in any case, which would still be consistent with the literature on naloxone use for clonidine overdose.
