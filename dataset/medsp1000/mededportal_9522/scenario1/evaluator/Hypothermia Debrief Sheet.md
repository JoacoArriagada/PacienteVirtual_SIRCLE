**Accidental Hypothermia**

I. Definition: An involuntary drop in core body temp to <35˚C (95˚F)\*

A. Mild hypothermia – Core temperature 32-35˚C (89.6-95˚F)

B. Moderate hypothermia – Core temperature 28-32˚C (82.4-89.6˚F)

C. Severe hypothermia – Core temperature < 28˚C (82.4˚F)

\*Note – Many standard thermometers do not read below 34˚C (93.2˚F). Rectal, esophageal or bladder thermometers with the ability to measure low temperatures must be used to accurately assess the patient.

II. Epidemiology

A. Accidental hypothermia can occur in any season, climate, or weather and should remain on the differential for every medical code and trauma patient and every patient with altered mental status.

B. Death rate from hypothermia is highest in colder climates. 83% occur October to March. 3

C. The lowest temperature recorded in a child who survived hypothermia was 14.2˚C (57.6˚F), and the lowest recorded initial temperature in an adult who survived was 13.7˚ (56.7˚F). 3

III. Pathophysiology:

A. With cold exposure, the initial response of the body is to maintain a normal core temperature by means of active movement and involuntary shivering. In mild hypothermia, all of the body’s normal adaptations to counter heat loss continue to function. The mild hypothermic state is similar to a sympathetic adrenergic surge with increased oxygen (O2) consumption and minute ventilation (VO2), tachycardia, significant vasoconstriction, and shivering. As hypothermia progresses past the mild stage, adaptations to counter heat loss begin to disappear. Systemic metabolic demands diminish, functionally shutting down and decreasing O2 consumption. 1,3 (See table 1)

B. Risk factors for hypothermia

1. Decreased heat production

a. Endocrine failure (ie. hypopituitary, hypothyroid, hypoadrenalism)

b. Malnutrition or hypoglycemia

c. Age (young and elderly)

2. Increased heat loss

a. Cold exposure

b. Ethanol

c. Burns

d. Iatrogenic (ie. cold fluid infusions, exposure)

3. Impaired thermoregulation

a. Intracranial head bleeds or skull fractures

b. Cerebrovascular accidents

c. Spinal injury

d. Hodgkin’s and Parkinson’s diseases

e. Sepsis

IV. Prehospital Care and Resuscitation

A. The goal of prehospital care is to rescue the patient, provide BLS or ACLS, provide insulation to prevent further heat loss, and rapidly transport the patient to the nearest hospital.

B. Generally, BLS and ACLS protocols should be followed

1. BLS

a. Alongside ABCs, the initial focus should be on removing wet clothing and insulating the patient from further exposure. 3

b. Even if an unresponsive patient does not have a pulse, consider holding CPR if the patient has an organized rhythm on cardiac monitor. The provider should assume that any organized rhythm is sufficient to provide circulation in the setting of severely reduced metabolism. If in doubt, however, start CPR. 2 (Level C Recommendation)

b. Resuscitation should be withheld only if the patient is frozen solid, there is an ice-occluded airway, there is a clear “do not resuscitate” order, there are other obvious lethal injuries, or the patient was documented to be submerged for over an hour. 3  (Level C Recommendation)

2. ACLS

a. Patients should be intubated in the field when clinically indicated. Excessive movement should be avoided but not at the expense of securing the airway. 3 (Level C recommendation)

b. Arrhythmia can be refractive to hypothermia, so if a patient in asystole or ventricular fibrillation does not respond to one round of ACLS medication or defibrillation hold further rounds and actively rewarm the patient. 2,3  (Level C Recommendation)

V. Emergency Department Evaluation:

A. History

1. Identify patients with underlying medical conditions predisposing them to hypothermia before attributing the hypothermia to exposure alone

2. It is important to determine how long the patient has been down and if there were any other insults prior to the hypothermia such as trauma, hypoxia, infection, or alcohol or drug intoxication.

B. Things to note on physical exam

1. Temperature – clinical thermometers often read no lower than 34˚C (93.2˚F), it is important to get a core temperature reading for accurate diagnosis. 2,3

2. Blood pressure - likely to be low as the patient progresses to moderate hypothermia because of extravasation and a profound “cold diuresis”. This diuresis is caused by initial overload of the central volume receptors because of peripheral vasoconstriction and later by cold-induced failure of the renal concentrating function. 2

3. Skin – as the core temperature drops, paradoxical signs replace the obvious features of chilling. Flushing replaces cyanosis and shivering is replaced by muscle rigidity. 2

C. Laboratory evaluation

1. Blood gas – Blood gas analyzers warm the sample to 37˚C (98.6˚F) which gives a lab-corrected value with falsely elevated CO2 and low pH. Hypothermic patients can have many types of acid-base disturbances. It is best to treat the uncorrected ABG while looking for underlying contributing factors. 3

2. Coagulation studies – Severely hypothermic patients are usually coagulopathic due to the temperature dependence of various enzymes in the coagulation cascade, however they often have “normal” lab values as the blood in heated to 37˚C (98.6˚F) prior to analysis. This is an especially important consideration in the trauma patient where the patient may be exsanguinating despite normal coagulation studies. The coagulopathy is self-limiting and resolves with rewarming. 3

D. Diagnostic Imaging and EKG

1. CXR – hypothermic patients with AMS are at risk for aspiration pneumonia

2. CT – should be considered based on patient’s signs and symptoms once they are

stabilized, and may help reveal underlying infection or trauma

3. EKG

a. The Osborne wave is a positive deflection at the QRS-ST junction. It is the most common EKG finding and can be seen at any degree of hypothermia.

b. Decreased spontaneous depolarization and abnormal repolarization result in prolonged intervals, AV blocks, bradycardia, and arrhythmias

VI. ED Management

A. Passive rewarming

1. Used for mildly hypothermic patients with intact shivering, normal endocrine function, and adequate energy stores to create endogenous heat. 3  (Level C recommendation)

2. The goal is to reduce convective, conductive, and radiant heat loss.

3. Remove wet clothing and cover the patient with blankets or other insulators

4. If shivering is intact, core body temp should rise 0.5˚C per hour.

B. Active external rewarming

1. Used for patients with mild or moderately hypothermic patients. 3 (Level C recommendation)

2. Involves applying heat directly to the skin and depends on intact circulation to warm the core.

3. Forced air or warm water blankets.

4. Risk of core-afterdrop and rewarming hypotension as warming the extremities returns cold, acidic blood to the core, causing further temperature drop and worsening acidosis. 3

5. Raises core body temperature 1-2˚C per hour

C. Active internal rewarming

1. Used for severe hypothermia or those with cardiovascular instability. 3 (Level C Recommendation)

2. Warm IV fluids (40˚C or 104˚F)

a. fluids are best heated with a blood warmer. Microwaves can create dangerous temperature differentials within the fluid bag. 2

b. Monitor for fluid overload

3. Warm humidified oxygen (42˚C or 107˚F)

4. Warm fluid lavage of body cavities

a. Closed thoracic lavage with placement of two chest tubes. Evidence suggests that left pleural lavage may be the most effective irrigation method and is generally reserved for those with cardiovascular instability. 2 (Level C recommendation)

b. Gastric lavage through NG tube may be attempted however this method has fallen out of favor with some experts due to increased risk of aspiration. 2

c. Bladder lavage

5. Rate of rewarming depends on rate of infusion of fluids

D. Extracorporeal rewarming

1. Used in severely hypothermic patients with cardiac arrest. 3

2. The most rapid and effective core rewarming method but requires specialized

equipment and training

3. Hemodialysis – effectively rewarms but requires adequate blood pressure

4. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)

Recommendation Guide:

**Level A recommendations.** Generally accepted principles for patient management that reflects a high degree of clinical certainty (i.e., based on strength of evidence Class I or overwhelming evidence from strength of evidence Class II studies that directly address all of the issues).

**Level B recommendations.** Recommendations for patient management that may identify a particular strategy or range of management strategies that reflect moderate clinical certainty (i.e. based on strength of evidence Class II studies that directly address the issue, decision analysis that directly addresses the issue, or strong consensus of strength of evidence Class III studies).

**Level C recommendations.** Other strategies for patient management that are based on preliminary, inconclusive, or conflicting evidence, or in the absence of any published literature, based on panel consensus.
