**Appendix B: Faculty Guide**

**Simulating Interprofessional Geriatric Patient Care Using Telehealth**

**Faculty Roles:**

• Greeter: Arrives 20 minutes before the start of the session, gets the standard patient

settled into the simulation room, provides bottled water, prepares the room in which the group starts the process. Interacts with IT staff to be sure telehealth equipment is

operational and connected with ‘remote’ site. Welcomes student teams, helps to calm any nerves they may have, helps them to prepare to meet their patient. Introduces the team to the faculty evaluator(s). Takes the ‘remote’ student to the debriefing room and gets student settled in with the telehealth technology. Then takes the main group to the patient simulation room to meet the patient.

**• Faculty Evaluator(s):**
Familiarizes self with telehealth cart (visit tutorial at http://youtu.be/OxQSW6Qxwe0 ).

*(Educators should edit this section to reflect the type of equipment that they are using to simulate the telehealth component of this learning activity).* Faculty reviews simulated patient information and discipline specific notes. Participating faculty may assist the Greeter in getting groups and/or ‘remote student’ settled. Observes/evaluates patient encounter, taking notes for later feedback. May need to help group manage its time if the group is unable to do so. At the end of the 30 minute encounter, takes team to debriefing room, prompts them to begin team planning for 20 minutes (group completes Interdisciplinary Geriatric Assessment form). During the debriefing session the faculty facilitate an oral team self assessment and provides feedback, including “shoulda, coulda, oughta’s.”

**Occupational Therapy**:

• Should check the integrity of skin which is in contact with the volar splint to make sure

there is no pressure caused by the splint which needs to be modified, or that hand

swelling has not caused the splint to be tight in any areas. If the OT member of the team

is participating via telecommunication, it is possible the team members who are

physically with the patient could use the hand-held general camera to conduct this

inspection.

• See notes below on UE management which will apply to OT/PT.

• Occupational performance dysfunction: She is not cooking or maintaining the home like she did previously. The OTs should screen Mrs. Smith for problems with basic ADL

(dressing, grooming, toileting, bathing, and eating) and instrumental ADL (cooking,

cleaning, money management, etc). They might also make sure that Mrs. Smith is

remembering to take her medications regularly and if she is having difficulty, what

strategies has she already tried. They should not prescribe techniques or equipment at

this point but instead focus on determining Mrs. Smith’s potential need for skilled

occupational therapy.

• Environmental assessment: Are there any places in her home where she feels unsafe

during walking/moving (stairs, getting into/out of bathtub, etc)? This may indicate the

need for a home safety assessment.

• Future assessments by OT would include in-depth assessment of ADL/IADL assessment, her home environment, skin integrity inside the splint and splint

modification/management, and progression of use of the UE during activity.

**Physical Therapy**:

• The students should note that the patient’s vitals are fairly good (although the respiratory rate is a bit high).

• A DEXA scan T-score of -2.5 or lower qualifies as osteoporosis. Mrs. Smith’s score is -2.6, as such, she is osteoporotic. (Note: multiplying the T-score by 10% gives a rough estimate of how much bone density a person has lost, ie for Mrs. Smith, she’s lost about 26% bone density.)

• The five degrees of dorsal angulation is consistent with a Colle’s fracture and is to be expected. Clinical union occurs when the fracture callus is apparent on x-ray and happens at three to five weeks. It takes six to eight weeks for a fracture to heal (perhaps longer in people with osteoporosis), as such, Mrs. Smith should remain immobilized in the splint for another two to four weeks, perhaps more (a radiograph at eight weeks will determine if radiographic union/full healing has occurred). Although the student therapists should carefully remove the splint to inspect the wrist, *they should not move the wrist or allow the* *patient to move the wrist* – this is contraindicated until healing has occurred at six to eight weeks.

• Stiffness at the wrist is common with immobilization and a good sign that the body

is healing itself. When complete healing has occurred, the PT and/or OT can perform joint mobilization (Grades III and IV) to alleviate any stiffness then work toward functional activities.

• The pinkness and mild swelling of Mrs. Smith’s wrist indicates mild inflammation. The students should recommend using an ice pack and/or elevation of the hand above the level of the heart. They can also suggest gentle movement of the fingers in the splint to assist with

Ultimately, the students will want to address the functional impact of the fracture on Mrs. Smith’s life, and work toward returning her to all of her preinjury activities.
