1. **TITLE OF CASE:** Button battery ingestion
2. **TARGET AUDIENCE:** Medical students, Emergency Medicine residents and Pediatric Emergency Medicine fellows, Pediatric residents
3. **LEARNING OBJECTIVES:**
   1. **Primary Learning Objectives**
      1. Recognize potential causes of a fussy toddler
      2. Recognize symptoms of potential esophageal or tracheal foreign body
      3. Order X-ray to evaluate for foreign body
      4. Identify esophageal (vs. tracheal) foreign body on x-ray
   2. **Secondary Learning Objectives**
      1. Identify the difference between coin and button battery on x-ray
      2. Discuss mechanisms of injury of button battery ingestion
      3. Recognize need for emergent endoscopic removal of impacted esophageal button batteries
   3. **Critical Actions**
      1. Perform focused history and complete undressed physical exam based on crying toddler
      2. Order appropriate imaging
      3. Recognizes esophageal foreign body on x-ray
      4. Recognizes button battery based on x-ray findings
      5. Consult appropriate service for endoscopic removal
      6. Arrive at correct disposition
4. **ENVIRONMENT:**
   1. **Lab set up** – Emergency Department bed in simulation center or true emergency department.
   2. **Manikin set up** – The manikin will be a clothed pediatric size manikin dressed like a girl. At the beginning of the simulation, there are no monitor leads on the patient, no IV access has been obtained, and the patient is not on oxygen. Alternatively, an actor (a resident, nurse, or standardized patient) could role-play as the patient’s mother.
   3. **Props** – Available for use will be a heart monitor with leads, blood pressure cuff, and pulse oximeter. Supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula and facemask will be available. There will be a medicine cart or tray with a full complement of vasoactive agents, PALS medications, and medicines necessary for sedation, rapid sequence intubation, and analgesia. In addition, a fully stocked code cart with defibrillator will be available for use, along with a selection of direct laryngoscopes and intubation supplies.
   4. **Audiovisual** – (See Appendix A) Available for review when asked for by participants will be a chest x-ray and laboratory studies.
   5. **Distracters** – Distracters can be added at the facilitator’s discretion. Choices could include an uncooperative consultant. Additionally, the learner could be challenged to probe for possibilities of the ingestion (i.e. what objects were in reach of the child, including cleaning solutions, medications or toys/object). However, as written, there is no need for distracters as the goal is simply to recognize a potential aspiration and/or ingestion of a foreign body and recognize a button battery on x-ray.
5. **ACTORS:**
   1. Roles can vary depending on the number of participants in the simulation session and on potential actor availability. This case can be implemented using a single participant and one facilitator/operator who can provide oral feedback via overhead audio and play additional historical or consultant roles via voice only. Realism can be enhanced by using physical actors, such as nursing staff members, but these are not required to successfully implement this case.
   2. Roles may be played by resident or faculty physicians, nurses, or medical students.
   3. Actions for the roles will be as follows:
      1. Primary physician: The main scenario participant will act as the primary physician and do the primary evaluation of the patient to include obtaining a history, conducting a physical exam, and ordering any necessary radiographic studies or other interventions. The primary physician can perform any needed procedures, or can delegate these to other providers if multiple participants are present.
      2. Secondary physicians: Other participants in the scenario will serve as collaborators, assistants for any necessary procedures, and consultants.
      3. Nursing staff: The role of the nurse will be to administer medications, verify orders, and perform other tasks as directed by the physicians. The nurse can also make observations as needed to stimulate case progression.
6. **CASE NARRATIVE:**
   1. **Scenario Background**
      1. **Chief Complaint:** Drooling and crying.
      2. **Triage Nursing Note:** Mom notes that the child is drooling, crying, and refusing to eat or drink for the past 30 minutes. Prior to symptom onset, the child was acting normal.
      3. **Vital Signs:** BP 90/50 mmHg, HR 130/min, RR 24/min, O2 sat 100% on RA, T 97.9°C oral, weight 16 kg
      4. **Past Medical History:** None
      5. **Medications:** None
      6. **Allergies:** No known allergies
      7. **Family and Social History:** Non-contributory family and social history.
   2. **Initial Scenario Conditions**
      1. **History given by Mother:** Essentially, the day was normal until my child started abruptly crying. My child was acting normal, playing on the living room floor. There were lots of toys on the floor. I left the room briefly and came back and she was crying and drooling. She hasn’t wanted to eat anything since and threw up once.
      2. **Circumstances at symptom onset:** (Can be provided if the learner asks). The toys were a variety of large, soft objects, building blocks, and books. Mom does not believe anything would be small enough to swallow. There was no access to chemical or cleaning products. Her purse was open on the floor; the cellular phone was out of the purse, but nothing else seemed to be missing. There was no pills or medications in her purse. She denies possible trauma.
      3. **Associated symptoms (given by system as review of system is completed):**  She hasn’t been sick recently. She ate a normal breakfast this morning. No recent fevers or illness. No cough, choking, or wheezing. No difficulty breathing. One episode of nonbloody, nonbilious emesis with no pill fragments. No diarrhea or stool changes. No travel or sick contact. She is up to date on her vaccines.
   3. **Initial Exam**
      1. **General**: Patient is a well-nourished 3-year-old female that is crying. She is drooling and refuses to drink.
      2. **HEENT**: Atraumatic and normocephalic. Pupils are 4mm, equal, round and reactive to light. Conjunctivae are normal. Extra-ocular movements are intact. Bilateral tympanic membranes are clear. Nares are patent with no foreign body visualized. Oropharynx is clear with pooling of saliva in the posterior oropharynx. No foreign body is seen. Mucous membranes are moist without lesions.
      3. **Neck:** No stridor or soft tissue mass. Normal ROM without pain.
      4. **Skin:** Warm and dry. No diaphoresis noted. No rashes or bruises.
      5. **Cardiovascular:** Tachycardia, regular rhythm. No murmurs or rubs noted.
      6. **Respiratory**: Breath sounds are equal bilaterally. No wheezes, rhonchi or rales are auscultated.
      7. **Abdominal:** No tenderness to palpation, no rebound or guarding. Bowel sounds are present. Abdomen is non-distended.
      8. **Genitourinary:** Normal exam.
      9. **Extremities:** Range of motion is normal in all four extremities, no tenderness to palpation, and no deformities. No edema is noted. No cyanosis is noted. No hair tourniquets.
      10. **Neurological:** Patient is alert and oriented. She has no gross motor or sensory deficit.
   4. **Physiology (data points should appear when patient is placed on the appropriate monitoring devices)**
      1. Heart Rate 130/minute, sinus
      2. Blood Pressure 90/50 mmHg
      3. Respiratory Rate 24/minute
      4. Pulse Oximetry 100% on room air
      5. Temperature 97.9°C oral
      6. Weight 16 kg
7. **Scenario Branch points**
   1. Changes in patient condition:
      1. The learner has to recognize the possibility of a foreign body ingestion based on abrupt onset of drooling and refusal to eat or drink. Failure to order x-ray results should result in the patient worsening; symptoms could include inconsolable crying, repeated episodes of vomiting, coughing or choking while trying to drink.
      2. Additionally, the learner should recognize potential button battery based on the physical characteristics noted on the x-ray. Magnified views of the object further inspection are provided if requested (Appendix A). The keys of this case is recognizing the button battery on x-ray and proceeding with urgent removal by a specialist.
      3. See schematic for flow of case, if incorrect actions are followed patient can decompensate. This could include increased drooling, stidor, color change, fever, agitation.
   2. Request for old records: None available.
   3. Differential Diagnosis: The learner managing this case may consider the differential of inconsolable crying in a toddler. Esophageal vs. tracheal foreign body should be considered based on symptoms and x-ray should be ordered. Other potential ingestions could be a caustic ingestion. Learners may also consider viral or bacterial oropharyngeal infections, retropharyngeal abscess, or deep space neck infection.
   4. Intravenous fluid administration: Should be started after recognition of foreign body ingestion on x-ray (along with N.P.O. status).
   5. Other medical interventions: None required.
   6. Laboratory studies/Imaging: Screening labs are given in Appendix A. Anteroposterior and lateral x-rays are provided in Appendix A. Magnified views are also provided; these can be shown if the learner asks for them.
   7. Administration of medication: Non sedating anti-emetics may be ordered by the provider.
   8. Consultation of specialist: All specialists (ENT, gastroenterologist, and/or toxicologist) will be available for telephone consult. None should direct management, nor be obstructive.
      1. If an ENT is consulted prior to confirmed diagnosis, then they should prompt for proper imaging first. ENT can defer to gastroenterology since the foreign body is located in the esophagus. This is also an opportunity to correct the learner if the foreign body is thought to be located in the trachea.
      2. If a gastroenterologist is consulted, then they can offer to emergently perform endoscopy once concern for esophageal button battery is voiced. Alternatively, if button battery is not recognized on x-ray findings, then the gastroenterologist could recommend admission with N.P.O. status and they will see them when finished in clinic (this would represent a non-recommended action).
      3. If the poison center/toxicologist is consulted for management, then they should be available to assist/confirm treatment decisions that have been decided (emergent removal of button battery) and offer supporting information.
   9. Non-recommended action: The learner should not discharge the patient without ordering chest x-ray. Delayed removal of the button battery is contraindicated. Additionally, attempted removal of the button battery in the Emergency Department is contraindicated.
8. **Instructor Notes:**
   1. **Scenario flow** – Instructors can directly influence the flow of the scenario by providing the initial patient history.
      1. Key aspect of case – Identification of ingestion of a foreign body is essential. Once the foreign body is identified, recognition of a button battery and need for emergent removal is critical.
   2. **Information for actors** – This case can easily be presented without formal “actors” and by using other participants to serve as the nursing staff or other collaborating physicians. Any specific actors used outside of participants should be briefed about the critical actions and anticipated flow of the case ahead of time.
   3. **Scenario programming** – The settings for a high-fidelity patient simulator are fairly straightforward for this scenario and do not require specific programming. Our program typically presents this scenario with the initial vital signs as presented above, with the major branch points of x-ray and need for endoscopy.
9. **Debriefing Plan:**
   1. **Method of debriefing** – A post-case debriefing conference is recommended immediately following the end of the scenario. Consider including some of the following elements:
      1. Open-ended questions by facilitator – Begin the session with a question to the primary participant about how they felt the scenario went. This often leads to extensive participant-led discussion that will touch on many of the major issues in the case. Invite any secondary participants and/or observers to comment constructively about the case.
      2. Brief didactic review – Some possible materials for review after the scenario have been provided in Appendix B. They address several major teaching points related to the case. However, individual participants may raise additional questions.
      3. Formal participant evaluation – Our group does not use this case for formal evaluations and we have no standardized form or format for such feedback. However, this case can easily be adapted to the standardized evaluation method used at your institution using the objectives in Section III (above).
   2. **Actual debriefing materials** – See Appendix B for debriefing materials.
   3. **Rules for the debriefing** – Often an open-ended question to the participants will provoke a discussion of aspects of the case management that were good and those areas where improvement can be made. Encourage your participants to discuss the case management decisions in a non-judgmental way. When this case is used for experiential learning (without formal participant evaluation) discussions may vary as compared to situations where formal feedback or grading is anticipated. We have found that placing an emphasis on the learning that occurs from experiencing a case like this is very effective, rather than focusing on the “right answer” or whether the specific management decisions were precisely as the leader would have performed.
   4. **Questions to facilitate the debriefing –**
      1. What is the differential of an inconsolable crying toddler?
      2. What signs and symptoms suggest tracheal foreign body (vs. esophageal foreign body)?
      3. How is imaging helpful for evaluating suspected foreign body ingestion?
      4. What findings on x-ray can suggest esophageal vs. tracheal location of the foreign body?
      5. How can you differentiate a coin from a button battery on x-ray?
      6. Why is it important to note the direction of the negative pole on x-ray?
      7. Why is urgent removal of an esophageal button battery necessary?
      8. When is emergent endoscopy/surgical removal versus expectant management appropriate?
10. **Pilot Testing and Revisions**
    1. **Performance expectations, anticipated management mistakes:** This is a straightforward case of recognizing a button battery on x-ray and knowing that emergent removal is indicated. During deployment, management mistakes have included: not recognizing a possible foreign body ingestion and not ordering an x-ray; failure to recognize location (esophageal vs. tracheal); failure to recognize a button battery based on radiographic findings; and failure to understand the need for emergent removal.
