Differences in Sex Development (DSD):
Team-Based Learning Module for Pediatric Residents

**Team Application Activity**

**Case 1**: You are examining a full-term newborn born via vaginal delivery. Mom had good prenatal care and no complications before or during the delivery. She was advised that they are having a boy based on the prenatal ultrasound. Baby weighs 3 kg and is 50 cm in length. Vitals: HR 140/min, RR 40/min, T 98.4 F, O2 saturation: 98%. Baby has a normal general exam. On GU exam, you note that the baby has Prader stage 4 'phallus' and no palpable gonads. There is labial rugation, hyperpigmentation, and posterior labial fusion. Nurse mentions that the parents have been asking to speak to the doctor about the baby's genitalia, gender assignment and medical issues. You have a team of two medical students and a nurse practitioner with you on rounds.

Objective information available in the source for scene advancement:

- Electrolyte pattern most consistent with the baby's current status: Sodium 140, Potassium 5, CO2 21.
- Labs are drawn prior to discharge.
- At follow up at 5 days of life, the baby is 5% below birth weight.
- Parents report the baby is feeding well.
- On general exam the baby appears well hydrated.
- Rapid FISH test suggests there is no Y chromosome material.
- Additional pending lab test most likely result: 17 hydroxyprogesterone 25000 ng/dl.

Facilitator notes retained for runtime control:

- Likely diagnosis discussed in the source: the baby is likely a 46XX with salt wasting or simple 21 hydroxylase CAH.
- Source note on expected course: these babies often do not develop electrolyte abnormalities until 2-3 weeks of life when they may present with hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, and acidosis.
- Source note on common etiology: the most common cause of symmetrical genital ambiguity in newborns is highlighted as 21 hydroxylase deficiency.
