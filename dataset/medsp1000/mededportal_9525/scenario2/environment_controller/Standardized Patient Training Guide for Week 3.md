**Part 2 occurs without any SP involvement (see timeline of case events at end of this document)**

**Part 3 Encounters include:**

**Case Overview: Kim Lee**

**Patient Name:** Kim Lee

**Presenting Problem:** abdominal pain

**Actual Diagnosis:** the cancer which has metastasized (spread) to your liver will be discovered to be a melanoma (skin cancer), originally from the back of your scalp, under your hair

**Patient Demographics:**

**Age:** 45 years old

**Sex:** female

**Ethnicity:** open

**Height:** Average

**Weight:** Average

**Overview of Case:**

Kim Lee is a 45 year old woman with a several month history of abdominal pain that has been getting progressively worse. While her primary care doctor was away on vacation, she went to urgent care for the pain, where she saw a medical student first, followed by the urgent care doctor. They recommended and she agreed to several blood tests and imaging studies. All results were forwarded to her primary care doctor. An abdominal CT scan revealed several “spots” on the liver that were worrisome for a cancer (either from the liver originally or from somewhere else that spread to the liver). At the point of the case that you will be engaged with the students, you do not yet know that there were any abnormalities on the abdominal CT scan.

**YOUR PROFILE:**

You are Kim Lee, a 45 year old woman who grew up in northern Virginia before moving to the bay area after college. You have been married to your husband Ken for 15 years, have an 10 year old daughter (Sally) and an 8 year old son (Mark). You work as a paralegal in a downtown law firm doing international copyright work.

Life has been pretty good, nothing to complain about until you started having this abdominal pain a few months ago.

**YOUR EMOTIONAL TONE FOR THE SECOND ENCOUNTER**:

The second encounter ‘advances the clock’ in the case to the point where you are returning to your primary care doc to get the answers of the latest round of tests. You have become progressively more concerned about this pain, mostly because all the previous tests have come back mostly normal. You realize that whatever is causing this pain can’t be simple. If that were the case, you assume the doctors would have figured it out by now. You are relieved to be seeing your primary care doctor (Dr. Weinstein) again, since you’ve been with him for years, and he knows you very well.

**PROCESS OF THE ENCOUNTER FOR THE SECOND ENCOUNTER**:

Although the activity takes place in a classroom, you are to assume that it actually is taking place in your primary care doctor’s (Dr. Weinstein) office. One student will be the interviewer. S/he will likely be one of the same students you saw during part 1 of the encounter (recall that encounter took place at the urgent care clinic). While you are used to seeing students in Dr. Weinstein’s office, you are also pleasantly surprised that it is the same student. As is common with Dr. Weinstein, the student is seeing you alone first before s/he will invite Dr. Weinstein back into the room with you both.

The other students in the classroom should be invisible to you. They will stay silent unless the interviewer calls a time-out to confer with their small group members-at which point you will drop your head & become ‘invisible’ until the time-out is over. Students might ask some additional history questions, but the focus of this encounter is more on their ability *to share information with you*, rather than *take information from you*. There will be no physical exam.

The encounter will take 20 minutes. We anticipate this will be a challenging activity for the student interviewer, so s/he may not know how to end the encounter. If necessary, say to the student when you sense it should end, “I’ll go out to the Waiting Room to wait for Dr. Weinstein” and then exit the room, but leave your belongings behind.

**Content of Encounters**

The students are not being asked to take your medical history. Although they may ask you a few questions about your family history, the focus of the interview is on *sharing information* - delivering uncertain news and explaining next steps. Students should also respond to your emotional needs and concerns. If you feel that the interviewer is focusing too narrowly on the medical aspects, have an emotional meltdown or ask some of the “emotional” questions below, so the student can practice appropriately responding to your emotional needs as well. On the other hand, if the interviewer is responsive to your emotional tone, please indicate that you are grateful for his/her support.

**Questions**

As this SP activity requires the student to present some clinical uncertainty, you should feel free to ask questions of things you don’t understand. The student may or may not mention cancer. If you feel adequately supported by the student, you may elect to bring up your fear that this is cancer. However if the student does NOT bring up cancer and you do not feel adequately supported, you may elect to omit this fear during the encounter.

**Sample predominantly “medical” questions:**

* *What could the spots on the liver be?*
* *How many spots were there?*
* *What are the next steps?*

**Sample predominantly “emotional” questions:**

* *This can’t be happening to me!*
* *Are you saying this could be cancer? Am I going to die?*
* *What will happen to my kids? How will I tell them?*
* *What do I say to my husband Ken?*
* *There’s still a chance it’s not cancer, right?*

**Kim Lee IE-PBL Part #3**

* Students work in their small groups to complete the classroom based portion of the IE-PBL.
* Students begin the session by teaching each other their Learning Issues
* After teaching each other but BEFORE anything else you will join the groups
* You are returning to your primary care doc (Dr. Weinstein). Conveniently ONE of the same students you saw in urgent care is also shadowing Dr. Weinstein
* Dr. Weinstein has already seen the abnormal CT results but asks the student to share this uncertain news (e.g. that there are “spots” on the liver that shouldn’t be there, and that they might be cancer)
