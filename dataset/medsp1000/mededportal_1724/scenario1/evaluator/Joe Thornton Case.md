# 1. <u>**PROFILE**</u>

Joe Thornton is a 68 year-old retired aerospace engineer at Lockheed.  He was diagnosed five years ago with diastolic congestive heart failure, likely from poorly controlled hypertension over the years. His blood pressures, taken infrequently at home over the last 3-4 months, are typically in the 150 range.  He also has had elevated cholesterol and triglyceride levels.

Joe has difficulty complying with his medications for his blood pressure and staying on a low fat, low cholesterol diet.  Joe was doing well until approximately 6 months ago when his wife Sarah passed away suddenly from a heart attack. Sarah was the one who previously cooked healthy meals and reminded him to take his medications. Since her death, Joe has been intermittently compliant with his medications and frequently dines out alone. He has started to notice some increased difficulty with breathing while lying down and now sleeps with two pillows instead of one at night “because it makes me feel better.”

He has been depressed since his wife’s death and now lives alone in an apartment. However, he has no suicidal ideation/plan/or attempt.  At the last appointment with his previous primary care physician, Joe was advised to visit a therapist and was started on an anti-depressant (Paxil). Joe agreed and has now seen his therapist twice. The therapist stressed the importance of taking his antidepressant (the first step to getting healthier), and Joe has finally started his Paxil. Joe returns to meet his new primary care provider with an assignment from his therapist to come up with a plan to help him better manage his medical illness.

Joe Thornton is a 68 year-old retired aerospace engineer with a history of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and congestive heart failure. Joe does have some fatigue.  He has had difficulty complying with his medications for his blood pressure and staying on a low fat, low cholesterol diet.  Joe was doing well until approximately 6 months ago when his wife Sarah passed away after a heart attack. (Initially he was able to cope somewhat, but as he has become increasingly depressed he has been less interested in taking care of himself). Joe has been very depressed regarding his wife’s death and now lives alone in an apartment. He doesn’t really have anybody close with whom to discuss his problems.  He has two grown children Mark and Belle who live on the East Coast and Joe “does not want to bother them with all my problems.” Mark is a financial analyst living in Long Island with his wife and 2 children. His therapist has given him an assignment to work with his new primary care provider to come up with a plan to help him better manage his medical illness.

# 3. **BELIEFS & PREFERENCES**

It is expected (hoped) that the student will learn various things about what is going on in Mr. Thornton’s life and how his health is affecting his life, as well as how his life is affecting (or has the potential to affect) his health.

In Mr. Thornton’s case, this may include (but is not limited to):

- Various reasons why he is not compliant with recommendations for diet and exercise
- That he is beginning to realize that his sadness over the loss of his wife has impacted his physical health

ANY of these issues may be addressed by the student when discussing treatment.  What is important is that the student indicates that s/he has heard your desires by reflecting AT LEAST ONE OF THEM back to you when discussing/recommending the next step(s) in your care (his/her “action plan” -- ***framed the action plan in such a way as to incorporate my beliefs and preferences***).  For example, rather than just saying, “I want you to take all your medications, eat a low salt diet, and start exercising daily,” it is desirable for him/her to say something like:

***“It sounds like you are feeling overwhelmed by your medical conditions and the other stressors in your life.  Perhaps we should start with some small changes that won’t feel too overwhelming.”***

(This is just an example; do not be waiting for this specific phrase.)  The student does not necessarily need to solve Joe’s problem or get him to agree, only to address it -- to indicate that s/he has heard the patient’s beliefs.

This summary is provided only as a reminder for you as to “where you are coming from” as the patient.  Keep this in mind while you are writing your comments.

Your comments are the patient’s subjective personal feelings about the interpersonal skills (bedside manner) of the student. You may use the PPI you completed for reminders of what the examinee did skillfully or not so skillfully.  Write about what the student did, not about who s/he is. Never judge the person, and never judge the behavior. Simply respond honestly about how the patient <u>felt</u>.

Use the “sandwich technique” when writing your comments.  Both at the beginning and end of your comments, write what the examinee did skillfully (positive comments).  The “meat of the sandwich” (or constructive criticism), should be written in between two positive comments.

Your comments should be specific and should illustrate concrete action(s) the student made that affected you either positively or negatively.  The structure of each comment should contain this simple linkage:  a specific examinee behavior made the patient feel a certain way.  For example:  “The student repeated back things I said so I felt s/he was really listening to me.” Or, “I really liked how you took away the stigma of being ‘depressed’ as I did not want to admit that I might have a problem. You were very sensitive to this issue.”

Please do not write comments such as “you have great (or bad) bedside manner.”  A comment like that is not helpful to the student because it is not specific – there is no reference to what specific student action made you feel this way.
