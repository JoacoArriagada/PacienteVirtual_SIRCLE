<u>**20 minutes**</u> **– Chronic Disease Management**

Chronic disease – any illness that has a prolonged course and neither resolves spontaneously nor is curable.  Good examples are illnesses such as hypertension, diabetes, and congestive heart failure.  Chronic illness is extremely common – 100 million people in the U.S. (about half the population) have at least one chronic illness and no matter what field you eventually end up in, you will be seeing patients with chronic illness.

**Ask the group:  Now we’re going to talk about how to approach a patient with a chronic condition in a standardized patient exam, or in the clinic.  Did you set an agenda for your visit with Mr. Thornton?  What factors did you consider when setting the agenda for your visit with Mr. Thornton? (5 min)**

**The students or you may bring up the fact that the agenda is different, and can be more challenging, than with a patient presenting with an acute complaint.**

- Setting the agenda for the visit
    - Factors to consider
        - Time allotted for the visit (29 min)
            - Need to allot time appropriately (i.e. 15 min for history, 5 min for physical, 10 min to discuss plan)
        - Your agenda for the visit
            - What are the issues that need to be addressed today?
                - *New symptoms - Fatigue, worsening dyspnea on exertion and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea*
                - *Depression*
                - *Poorly controlled HTN (Adherence)*
        - Elicit patient’s agenda for the visit and negotiate the agenda with the patient
            - *Patient’s agenda – address fatigue and come up with a plan to better manage chronic illness*
            - *Negotiated agenda – address adherence issues (HTN) and new symptoms of fatigue, dyspnea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea*
            - What other members of the healthcare team might be able to help in the future?
                - *Nutritionist*
                - *Therapist/Social worker*
                - *Nurse educator*
                - *Case manager*
                - *Pharmacist*

**Q:  What are important issues to cover in taking the medical history of any patient with chronic illness?  (7 minutes)**

*(note to preceptors: specific answers are based upon a patient with CHF to relate back to the case of Joe Thornton)*

- In taking the medical history of a patient with chronic illness, ask about:
    - Current issues this visit (Set the agenda!)
    - Relevant symptoms/signs of disease (ex: CHF) and complications of chronic illness (end-organ damage)
        - *Dyspnea on exertion*
        - *Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea*
        - *Edema*
        - *Weight gain*
        - *Chest pain*
        - *Palpitations*
    - Patient knowledge, beliefs, and goals regarding their illness and lifestyle modifications
        - *Low salt/low fat diet*
        - *Exercise*
    - Degree of control/patient self-monitoring
        - *Monitoring weight*
        - *Monitoring BP*
    - Impact of illness on the patient’s functioning
    - Verify medications and always ask about adherence in a non-judgmental manner
        - *Ex:  “Some patients find it hard to take their medications regularly.  Has this been a challenge for you as well? How many times a week do you miss taking your medications?”*

*Some students may feel that it’s unrealistic to see a patient with a chronic illness that they don’t know (that isn’t their primary care patient). It may be helpful for you to describe how commonly cross coverage or team-based care occurs in actual clinics, to give them perspective.*

**Q:  For many of the chronic illnesses we see, behavioral change is a critical component of self-management.  How can you motivate patients to make behavioral changes and assist them in the management of their disease? (8 minutes)**

<u>6 A’s of Behavioral Counseling</u>

<u>(Adapted from Whitlock EP et al. Evaluating primary care behavioral counseling interventions: an evidence-based approach. Am J Prev Med 2002;22:267-84.)</u>

*(note to preceptors:  students may recognize this framework that was initially developed by the NCI for smoking cessation.  Sample questions in italics are suggestions of how student might ask questions to encourage behavioral change.)*

**Address** the patient’s agenda and express your desire to talk about self-management behaviors.

**Assess** knowledge, personal values and goals, previous experience with change and level of confidence regarding self-management.

*How do you feel about taking your medications regularly?*

*What has been most challenging about taking your medications regularly?*

*What changes would you like to make in terms of better managing your medical conditions?*

*What do you think would be the biggest obstacle you would face in making these changes?*

**Advise** – Provide clear, specific and personal behavioral advice, including information about personal health harms and benefits, in a non-judgmental manner (Don’t lecture the patient!).  Verify patient’s understanding (close the loop!)

*I think the problem that you are having with fatigue could be related to your heart failure and depression.  One thing I noticed today is that your blood pressure is high.  This is probably because you are having trouble taking your medications consistently.  When your blood pressure is high, this makes it even harder for your heart to pump.  This leads to fluid back-up in your lungs that causes fatigue, shortness of breath, weight gain and problems breathing at night.  I think all those problems would be better if we could get your blood pressure under control by having you take your medicines more regularly.*

**Agree** – Collaboratively select appropriate treatment goals and methods based upon the patient’s interests and their willingness to change their behavior.

*Would you be willing to work on taking your medications more regularly and possibly monitoring your blood pressure at home so we can see if we are making progress in treating your blood pressure?*

*What changes do you feel would be reasonable?* 

**Assist** – Aid the patient in achieving goals by assisting them in acquiring skills, confidence, and supports for behavioral change.  Identify personal barriers and potential resources (community resources or other members of the healthcare team).

*Making changes can be challenging, what would be the hardest things about making the changes we discussed above.  What might help you to make these changes (consider other resources)?* 

**Arrange** – Schedule follow-up contacts to provide ongoing assistance/support and to adjust the plan as needed.

**Important Physical Exam Teaching Take-Home Points:**

- Use the history to determine which PE maneuvers to perform
- Wash your hands
- Position the patient for comfort
- Examine directly on the skin
- Drape appropriately
- Tell the patient what you are going to do and share the findings

<u>**20 minutes -**</u> **Physical Exam demonstration and practice**

**now we’re going to review the physical exam, to focus on the CV exam and areas that past students have missed points on the in CPX.**

<u>**&nbsp;&nbsp;**</u>

- For this case, we would like to have <u>**two**</u> students each perform part of the cardiovascular exam, as detailed below.  (*Note to preceptors:  please communicate to students that all physical exam maneuvers on the CV exam may not be relevant to this case)*

Discuss when it would be indicated to do a screening (auscultation) vs. full CV exam, based on your differential diagnosis – how concerned are you that the patient could have a CV problem? In this case, a full CV exam is indicated, but in other cases it may not be.

**Cardiovascular exam:**

    - Blood pressure
    - Evaluate JVP – Head of bed at 45 degrees, assessing jugular venous pulsations.
    - Palpate precordium to assess for lifts/heaves and position of PMI – in 2 locations (left of sternum and below left breast)
    - Auscultate in 4 locations
    - Palpate for abdominal aortic aneurysm
    - Assess for peripheral edema
    - Check pulses (carotid, femoral, pedal)

- Faculty and group discuss technique.
- Patient gives feedback on their comfort during the students’ exam, and on the importance of explaining findings to the patient and how this might be done smoothly*.*
