**Opening discussion of additional resources for youth with special health care needs**

Learners should be led through a discussion of the different state and local resources available to youth with special health care needs, including cognitive impairments and chronic mental health issues not previously discussed in this case.   The need for youth with severe cognitive limitations to have a legal guardian in place before they turn 18 should also be discussed.  While parents are often the legal guardians for these children, sometimes it may be other relatives or a state appointed guardian.  Typical guardianship procedures include determination of disability by a physician, psychologist, and social worker before a hearing in family court before a judge.  Because the process varies widely by state, learners should be encouraged to look up the specifics for their local area.

**Distribute case text "Session 3, Part A"**

Marion Harris is an information officer at the health insurance company Blue Cross Blue Shield (BCBS).  She is reviewing the total medical expense (dollar amount of insurance payments paid to all sources for a given patient in one year) for patients enrolled in BCBS and receiving care at the Boston Children’s Hospital Adolescent and Young Adult Clinic.  She notes one of the outliers is Bryan, a now 23 year old who appears to be receiving his care both in Georgia and Massachusetts.  She notes several emergency room visits in both states for diabetes-related complications (low blood sugar, high blood sugar) and at least one admission for diabetic ketoacidosis.  She doesn’t note any primary care visits in the system in the past two years.  It appears he saw an adult endocrinologist once in Massachusetts six months ago.  Marion sends a report to the practice manager of the clinic noting that the clinic will not receive a portion of their insurance payments if this pattern continues.

**Discussion topics for Part A**

- Medical home

**Potential probe questions for Part A**

- What aspects of health and medical care are appropriate for a 23 year old to understand and manage?
- Why might Bryan be experiencing increased ER visits and admissions?
- What is the role of the medical home in caring for patients who are not physically present?  Is Bryan still “our” patient?

**Key points for discussion for Part A**

Young adults have much worse health status than adolescents. 21  Often described as “young invincibles” young adults are frequently thought to ignore their long-term health risks. 22  However, there is mounting evidence that young adults do care about their health  and rather that structural issues related to the cost of health care have kept them out of the insurance market. 25

The patient centered medical home movement dates back to the 1960s and originated within pediatrics.  The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) defined the medical home in 1992 as “accessible, continuous, comprehensive, family centered, coordinated, and compassionate” care, “delivered or directed by well-trained physicians who are able to manage or facilitate essentially all aspects” of care, and “known to the child and family.”  Additional components included in the 1992 AAP statement included provision of preventive care, assurance of ambulatory and inpatient care 24/7, continuity of care, coordination of referrals, interaction with school and community agencies, and maintenance of a central record.26

In the early 2000s, the medical home movement caught on with family physicians and internal medicine physicians, and in 2007 the major academies of these groups joined with the AAP to release the “Joint Principles of the Patient-Centered Medical Home.”  Similar to the AAP definition, these principles included the presence of a personal physician, physician directed medical practice, whole person orientation of care, and coordination of care.  Accreditation as a medical home is available via the Accreditation Association for Ambulatory Health Care ([www.aaahc.org/en/accreditation](http://www.aaahc.org/en/accreditation)) and the National Committee for Quality Assurance ([www.ncqa.org/Programs/Recognition/Practices/PatientCenteredMedicalHomePCMH.aspx](http://www.ncqa.org/Programs/Recognition/Practices/PatientCenteredMedicalHomePCMH.aspx)).  Accreditation standards vary between the two organizations but are generally aligned with the principles above.  Relevant to our case, the AAAHC requires that 50% of a patient’s visit be with the same provider or team.
**Distribute case text "Session 3, Part C"**

After receiving multiple letters and phone calls from the clinic, Bryan shows up to see you.  He is now 23 years old and has just moved back to Boston to work for the Celtics managing corporate ticket sales, although he still travels to Georgia frequently because his girlfriend lives there.  He loves his job because he gets to stay connected to sports, watch all the games, and eat at the arena.  He denies any symptoms of depression or suicidal thoughts, but does smoke marijuana when he feels “stressed” and notes he often forgets to take his insulin if he is high.   Otherwise he generally takes his insulin.  “My girlfriend really bugs me about it, but for some reason I don’t mind the way I did when my mom did.”

He saw an endocrinologist 6 months ago but never got a reminder call for a follow-up appointment and so didn’t go back.  He doesn’t remember the last time he saw a nutritionist and has never seen a counselor regularly.  Review of the chart notes the last two months refills for insulin were prescribed for him by the doctor of the day after he called the nursing line and stated he has no other way to get his insulin.  His last HbA1c was 10%.  When you approach him about finding an adult primary care provider and endocrinologist he says “Can’t I just keep coming here?  I really like you.”

**Discussion topics for Part C**

- Transfer to adult centered care
- Affordable Care Act

**Potential probe questions for Part C**

- When should patients transfer to adult-centered care?  Who decides?  What are common reasons and times to transfer?
- Do all patients need to transfer to adult-centered care?  Are there pediatric subspecialties that do not exist on the adult side?
- What types of insurance coverage are available for 24 year olds?  How did the Affordable Care Act change the insurance landscape for young adults?

**Key points for discussion in Part C**

Although some individual practices have mandatory ages for youth to transfer to adult-centered care (often 18, 21, or 25), there is no national standard for transfer in the United States.29  Unfortunately, many youth are transferred to adult care in times of crisis, such as new diagnoses of pregnancy or mental health issues.30  This should be avoided.  Some youth will not even need to physically transfer to a new provider if their provider sees patients across the lifespan.  However, patients and providers should work together to implement an adult model of care even in these environments.   Finally, primary care providers may have difficulty finding appropriate subspecialty referral sources for young adults with certain conditions of childhood, including autism31 and congenital abnormalities.

The Affordable Care Act, with its provision that all Americans secure health insurance, has many implications for the health care of young adults.  These include the elimination of insurance denial based on pre-existing conditions, complete coverage for preventive care, and the ability of young adults to stay on their parents’ health insurance until the age of 26.  The website younginvincibles.org is a particularly helpful resource regarding the impact of the Affordable Care Act on youth.
