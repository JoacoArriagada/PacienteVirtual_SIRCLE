**Distribute case text "Session 1, Part A"**

Tiffany is a medical fellow working in adolescent evening clinic.  Next up on her schedule is Bryan, a 15 year old male booked for a new patient physical.  While waiting for him to check in she opens the electronic health record and finds that he was discharged from the inpatient ward last week.  Previous to this admission it does not appear he was ever at Children’s.  Review of the notes reveals he presented to the Children’s emergency room with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and a recent history of weight loss.  He was found to be in diabetic ketoacidosis and was admitted to the inpatient endocrine service where he was started on two types of insulin – long-acting lantus and short-acting novolog per sliding scale adjusted to his carbohydrate intake and blood sugar numbers.  He did meet with the inpatient diabetes nurse educator twice.  The discharge summary includes some information on his insulin dosing, the appointment with Tiffany today, and an appointment with endocrinology in one month.   There are no other outpatient notes in the system.

**Discussion topics for Part A**

- Transition milestones by age
- Transition assessment tools
- Medical home definition

**Potential probe questions for Part A**

- What aspects of health and medical care are appropriate for a 15 year old to understand and manage?
- At what age should we start discussing the transition to adulthood with patients?
- What is the role of the primary medical home in the management of a subspecialty disease like diabetes?
- Are we Bryan’s medical home?  Why or why not?

**Key points for discussion of Part A**

The American Academy of Pediatrics, American Academy of Family Physicians, and the American College of Physicians outlined the role of the medical home in supporting the health care transition from adolescence to adulthood in their joint statement in 2011. 2   Key to their recommendations is the assessment of youth and family readiness beginning at age 12.  Just as infants and children will not reach developmental milestones at the same chronological age, adolescents and young adults will not be ready to transfer to adult-centered health care at exactly the same chronological age.  However, adolescents should be making strides toward adulthood in a myriad of arenas, including education, vocation, relationships, and health care.  A given adolescent’s trajectory will be shaped by both personal and sociocultural factors.

Multiple tools have been developed for assessing readiness to transition to adult-centered health care.  One of the most widely used and well-studied is the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ). 4 The TRAQ includes skills such as knowledge of medication names and indications and personal interactions with health care staff.  Respondents indicate their ability level in each category and their desire to learn that skill.  The TRAQ can be administered repeatedly over time to track transition readiness and individual transition plans can be created to assist with progress.

“The medical home is best described as a model or philosophy of primary care that is patient-centered, comprehensive, team-based, coordinated, accessible, and focused on quality and safety. It has become a widely accepted model for how primary care should be organized and delivered throughout the health care system, and is a philosophy of health care delivery that encourages providers and care teams to meet patients where they are, from the most simple to the most complex conditions. It is a place where patients are treated with respect, dignity, and compassion, and enable strong and trusting relationships with providers and staff. Above all, the medical home is not a final destination instead, it is a model for achieving primary care excellence so that care is received in the right place, at the right time, and in the manner that best suits a patient's needs.” \~ Patient Centered Primary Care Collaborative [https://www.pcpcc.org/about/medical-home](https://www.pcpcc.org/about/medical-home)

**Distribute case text "Session 1, Part B"**

Tiffany calls Bryan’s name in the waiting room.   A woman who appears to be his mother jumps up and a young man with his eyes cast down at the floor shuffles after her.  Once in the exam room, Bryan’s mother tells Tiffany the two of them just moved to Boston a few months ago from Georgia to be near her family after she and Bryan’s father separated.  Bryan has an older brother in college in Georgia.  She tells Tiffany that Bryan is enrolled in the Boston Public Schools and that he isn’t doing nearly as well as he did at his old school.  “And now with this diabetes on top of everything else – I don’t know what I am going to do with him.  I have to stay on top of him to take these injections, and it is so confusing trying to figure out how much to take when – there’s a lot of math involved!”  When Tiffany asks her to leave the room so she can speak to Bryan alone, she seems surprised.  “His old doctor in Georgia never did that – why do you want to do that?”  Tiffany explains the adolescent confidentiality policy and mom seems satisfied and goes to the waiting room to work on getting immunization records and growth charts from his prior pediatrician faxed to the office.

**Discussion topics for Part B**

- Parent vs. adolescent health concerns
- Adolescent confidentiality

**Potential probe questions for Part B**

- What rules and laws inform our ability to talk to Bryan without his mother?  To his prior pediatrician?
- What do we do when a parent expresses concern about an adolescent but the adolescent denies that concern?

**Key points for discussion of Part B**

Confidential care is a key component of adolescent health care, as endorsed by the Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine. 5 It helps promote the growing maturity and autonomy of adolescents transitioning to adulthood.  Furthermore, there is evidence some adolescents would forgo necessary care without it. 6 The specifics of what can be kept confidential from parents vary by state, but often include issues pertaining to sexual health, reproductive health, mental health, and substance use.  Learners should be encouraged to lookup the regulations for their state. 7  The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) protects the privacy of individuals’ health information and medical records.8

Although there are laws protecting adolescents’ right to confidential care, families are still key allies in the delivery of health care to teens.  Parental concerns should be acknowledged and explored with both the parent and the adolescent, including in private as necessary.  Families play a very important role in helping adolescents learn to be autonomous in their self-care of chronic medical conditions. 9  Both parental over-engagement and dis-engagement with the day to day management of adolescent health concerns pose threats to this developing autonomy that is so key to the transition to adult-centered care.

**Distribute case text "Session 1, Part C"**

Tiffany asks Bryan what he understands about his new diagnosis and he surprises her by giving a pretty accurate summary of Type 1 diabetes – how insulin works, why he needs to take certain amounts at certain times, the risks of taking too much or not enough.  “I don’t know why my mom thinks the medicine is so hard – I’m good at math so I guess the numbers just make sense.”  However, when she reviews Bryan’s recent blood sugars from the meter his mother brought with them she notices that most of them are still too high.  When she asks him about this, Bryan admits that he just doesn’t like taking the insulin.  “It’s not the needles – I don’t really care.  I just don’t want to deal with it, and I don’t want my mom on me all the time about it.  I don’t even want to be here – I want to go back to Georgia to live with my dad.”  When they talk further she learns he hasn’t really made any friends here and that he moved too late to try out for the basketball team, which was his major activity at his old school.  Now he mostly comes home and plays videos games in his room after school.  He often skips breakfast and when he does eat, it is usually something he picks up at a fast food restaurant or something he microwaves from the freezer.  He doesn’t really feel like talking to anyone or doing homework.  He thinks there are counseling and nursing services at his school but he has never seen anyone in those offices.

**Discussion topics for Part C**

- Medication adherence challenges in adolescence
- Role of mental health assessment in chronic disease management
- Importance of nutritional counseling in diabetes management

**Potential probe questions for Part C**

- What concerns would you have about Bryan, from the point of view of a medical provider (i.e., physician or nurse practitioner), a mental health provider (i.e., social worker or psychologist), and a nutritionist?
- Thinking developmentally, what contexts and milieus are relevant to Bryan (school, home) and what are the key self-care and self-management tasks that are relevant to transition readiness assessment?
- What is the role of the school in assisting youth with physical or cognitive issues?

**Key points for discussion of Part C**

Issues of medication adherence in adolescence are common, and are not always related to deficits in knowledge.  In adolescent Type I diabetes in particular, family support and mental health issues are most strongly related to medication adherence and glycemic control. 10  Detecting mood disorders is critical to the care of all youth, and can be particularly helpful for youth with chronic illness, 11 who are known to have a higher prevalence of emotional distress than healthy adolescents. 12  Good adolescent nutrition impacts the management of all chronic illnesses, but is especially important in diabetes when insulin dosing is tied to food intake.  In addition to counseling resources, schools are also required to provide adolescents with chronic medical conditions individual health plans. 13
