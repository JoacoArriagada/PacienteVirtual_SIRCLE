Patient Name: Bobbi Jessup

Chief Complaint: nausea, vomiting

Most likely Diagnosis and Differential with rationale from history and/or physical exam: The diagnosis for this case is acute infectious gastroenteritis caused by a virus; because it is the most common cause of gastroenteritis in adults worldwide including in the US, norovirus should be highest on the differential diagnosis for this case. This diagnosis is suggested by the patient’s acute onset of vomiting, non-bloody diarrhea, and fever; a sick contact (spouse) with the same symptoms; and recent travel history (cruises are known settings in which outbreaks of norovirus occur). Other diagnoses in the differential for this case include infection from other common viruses known to cause these symptoms, including rotavirus, enteric adenovirus, astrovirus (though this is more common in young children than in adults, and vomiting is infrequent), and sapovirus (which primarily affects young children). Other viruses are also in the differential (coronavirus, parechovirus, picobirnavirus, bocavirus, and Aichi virus), though they are much less common than those listed above. Bacteria (e.g., nontyphoidal *Salmonella* species, *Campylobacter* species, *Clostridiodides difficile*, *Clostridium perfringens*, enterotoxigenic *E. coli*, toxin-producing *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Bacillus cereus*) and parasites (e.g., *Giardia lamblia, Cryptosporidium* parvum) are also in the differential diagnosis for this case, though they are not as likely due to their lower frequency in the US population.

| MANAGEMENT OR DIAGNOSITIC PLAN | Confirmation of infection by lab testing is generally unnecessary if acute viral gastroenteritis is suspected.  If bacterial pathogens are suspected, a gastrointestinal pathogen panel or a bacterial culture of the stool may be obtained. Increasingly, PCR-based detection of GI pathogens is performed by clinical laboratories; these panels generally include common viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens. If parasites are suspected, testing for specific pathogens may be considered. If inflammatory diarrhea is suspected, testing for leukocytes in the stool may be considered.  Serum electrolytes (e.g., basic metabolic panel or comprehensive metabolic panel) may be obtained, if severe dehydration is suspected.  Supportive care, with attention to supporting the patient’s hydration, is the primary management for this infection. |
