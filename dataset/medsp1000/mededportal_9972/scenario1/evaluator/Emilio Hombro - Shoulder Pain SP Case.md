Provocative tests   * Positive Speed’s test   + With the right elbow flexed 90° and the student pulling down on the wrist, your pain is reproduced.   + You feel it on the front of the shoulder.   + If the student presses over the biceps tendon, that identifies the location of the pain. Locate this tendon on yourself so you will know when the student is pressing on the correct location.   + Make your response to this test substantially greater than any of the other provocative tests. The student needs to distinguish between the mild, non-specific discomfort commonly seen with negative tests and the more severe, localized pain of a positive provocative test. * Weakly positive empty can test   + With both arms held straight out at about 30° forward flexion and with the thumbs pointed down, the examining student will push down on the arms.   + You have no significant weakness with this test. * You feel some mild discomfort in the anterior right shoulder with this maneuver. * Weakly positive Hawkins’s test   + With the shoulder flexed forward to 90° and the elbow flexed, the examiner internally rotates the arm (meaning the forearm turns downward). * You feel some mild discomfort in the anterior right shoulder with this maneuver. |
| **Special Instructions** | Because the biceps tendon passes through the subacromion space, there is some overlap between the symptoms of biceps tendinitis, subacromion space impingement, and supraspinatus tendinitis. To portray this, you will need to portray two levels of discomfort for these tests.   * Speed’s test for biceps tendonitis (which is the correct diagnosis)   + A sharp, definite painful response to Speed’s test   + Also, a localized, sharp discomfort to pressure with the thumb over the biceps groove * Hawkins’s test for subacromion impingement   + A bothersome level of discomfort but not a sharp, severe pain   + Also, a mild discomfort with palpation of the groove just beneath the acromion process. * Empty can test for supraspinatus tendinitis or tear.   + Again, a bothersome level of discomfort but not a sharp, severe pain. |
| **Other tests** | * Negative infraspinatus test   + No discomfort with resistance to external rotation * Negative liftoff test   + No discomfort to resistance to lifting the hand off of the back * Negative (or slightly positive) cross arm test   + Very mild discomfort when the right arm is drawn across the chest so that the elbow is pushed close to the left shoulder. * Negative apprehension test   + No discomfort when the examiner raises your right arm and pushes forward from behind the shoulder. * Weakly positive “painful arc” sign   + Also, when abducting the right arm (raising the arm out from the side of the body), as your arm reaches a point just above horizontal, you feel some mild discomfort in the right shoulder. |

**SCHEME-BASED DECISION MAKING:** based on your history and exam, you should be able to answer these four key questions that are used to narrow the list of possible diagnoses.

1. The onset of the patient’s shoulder pain was

* acute (usually traumatic)
* gradual (usually non-traumatic)

1. The structure causing the patient’s shoulder pain is

* local to the shoulder area
* referred to the shoulder from another part of the body

1. If local to the shoulder, the structure causing the pain is
   * glenohumeral (meaning the shoulder joint with its capsule and ligaments, the subacromion space, and the four muscles of the rotator cuff)
   * extra-glenohumeral (meaning the AC joint, biceps tendon, and other structures adjacent to the glenohumeral joint)
2. If glenohumeral, then the pain is most likely coming from
   * the subacromion space (impingement)
   * one of the muscles or tendons of the rotator cuff (muscle tear or tendinitis)
   * the joint itself (arthritis with inflammation, ligament laxity, or scarring with adhesions)
