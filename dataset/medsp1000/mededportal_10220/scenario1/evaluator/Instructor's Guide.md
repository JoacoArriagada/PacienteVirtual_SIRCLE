**Synthetic Cannabinoid Toxicity Simulation – Instructor’s Guide**

**Simulation Overview**

Synthetic cannabinoids refers to a formidably varied array of synthetic THC-receptor agonists used to achieve euphoria typically by way of smoking or ingestion. In addition to their euphoric effects, however, synthetic cannabinoids are known to cause psychosis, agitation, elevated vital signs, myocardial ischemia and seizures. Use of products containing these chemicals, often with concomitant use of other illicit drugs, has been increasing in recent years.

Unfortunately, there is no specific toxidrome associated with this type of toxicity. What is more, synthetic cannabinoids are not yet reliably detected on routine drug screens. Clinicians, therefore, have been prompted to increase their index of suspicion for synthetic cannabinoid use in intoxicated patients.

This experience is a high-fidelity simulation of a patient presenting with severe cardiac complications related to synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.

This simulation is based in part on an actual patient presenting to the emergency department complaining of ischemic chest pain, dyspnea, nausea and vomiting. His symptoms worsened in the hours after he had ingested “spice,” a colloquial term for a brand of synthetic cannabinoid. He appeared distressed, agitated, diaphoretic and repeatedly vomited in the emergency department. He was hypertensive and an initial ECG showed waveforms with features consistent with Wellen’s sign.

His cardiac history was particularly concerning, as he had experienced similar symptoms months prior at another institution, again in the setting of synthetic cannabinoid ingestion. At that time he ultimately underwent stent placement for critical LAD artery occlusion and a low cardiac ejection fraction.

He received supportive care, including benzodiazepine and anti-emetic therapy until he left against medical advice prior to inpatient admission.

There are two primary objectives of this simulation experience. The first of these is to increase learner awareness of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity and how patients experiencing it may present in the acute setting. The second objective is twofold. Learners will (1) recognize that severe myocardial ischemia may directly result as a complication of synthetic cannabinoid overdose and (2) implement treatment(s) that mitigate the effects of synthetic cannabinoids while definitive treatment is sought.

Primary target learners include medical students on Emergency Medicine Acting Internship/Sub-Internship (EMAI) rotations and Emergency Medicine residents. Additional target learners may include internal medicine residents, as well as residents and students on toxicology rotations. Outcome measures include a pre-defined critical actions checklist and instructor assessments of effective patient care, medical knowledge, communication skills and professionalism outlined in the learner evaluation form.

**Educational Objectives**

1. Primary
   1. Learners will suspect acute coronary syndrome in an intoxicated patient and initiate appropriate management.
   2. Learners will manage a patient acutely intoxicated with synthetic cannabinoids after investigating for life-threatening complications.
2. Secondary
   1. Cognitive – the learner will:
      1. Consider and evaluate for myocardial infarction, a known complication of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.
      2. Manage acute synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.
   2. Affective – the learner will:
      1. Consult cardiologist.
      2. Consult toxicologist or Poison Control Center.
      3. Interact with an agitated patient.
      4. Interact with the family of the patient.
3. Critical actions checklist
   1. Consider and evaluate for myocardial infarction.
      1. Obtain and assess initial ECG.
      2. Obtain cardiac injury panel.
      3. Administer oxygen via nasal cannula.
      4. Administer aspirin.
      5. Administer sublingual nitroglycerin.
      6. Administer morphine.
      7. Consider administration of a beta blocker.
      8. Consult cardiologist.
   2. Identify synthetic cannabinoid toxicity after considering appropriate differential diagnosis.
      1. Consult with toxicologist or Poison Control Center.
   3. Initiate management of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.
      1. Obtain IV access.
      2. Administer IV benzodiazepine.
      3. Consider administration of IV diphenhydramine.
      4. Reassess patient, including vital signs and ECGs.

**Implementation Advice**

This case simulation highlights the pathophysiology, presentation, identification and treatment of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity. As there is no pathognomonic toxidrome associated with synthetic cannabinoid toxicity, learners participating in the simulation will actively gain experience treating a patient with a diagnosis that ultimately requires a high index of suspicion. The included ECG tracings, chest imaging and laboratory studies also give learners an opportunity to order and analyze pertinent testing and see how their results impact patient management. This simulation will also give learners a chance to obtain a focused yet thorough history and physical examination while maintaining a professional cadence in the setting of an agitated patient.

While the complications of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity can be dire, as is in this patient, this is primarily a toxicology case. Instructors playing the roles of consultants in this scenario should encourage the learners not only to treat the myocardial infarction in this patient but also identify and address synthetic toxicity as its most likely cause. Learners should leave the simulation session having treated synthetic cannabinoid toxicity in addition to addressing the acute coronary syndrome. Strategies for instructors to facilitate this have been outlined in the simulation scenario manuscript.

Programming the scenario (if using a simulator manikin) requires input of initial vital signs, although vital sign changes may also be programmed in real time to facilitate case flow. All vital sign states are listed in the simulation scenario manuscript. This simulation requires an actor to play the part of the patient, who will give the history and interact with learners. The actor may interact directly with learners or through a simulator manikin, should it be available.
