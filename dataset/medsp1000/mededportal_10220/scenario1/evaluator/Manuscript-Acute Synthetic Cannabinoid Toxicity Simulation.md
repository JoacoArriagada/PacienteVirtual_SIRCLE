1. Acute Synthetic Cannabinoid Toxicity - Simulation
2. Target Audience: EMAI and EM-PGY1

III. Learning Objectives or Assessment Objectives

1. Primary
   1. Learners will suspect acute coronary syndrome in an intoxicated patient and initiate appropriate management.
   2. Learners will manage a patient acutely intoxicated with synthetic cannabinoids after investigating for life-threatening complications.
2. Secondary
   1. Cognitive – the learner will:
      1. Consider and evaluate for myocardial infarction, a known complication of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.
      2. Manage acute synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.
   2. Affective – the learner will:
      1. Consult cardiologist.
      2. Consult toxicologist or Poison Control Center.
      3. Interact with an agitated patient.
      4. Interact with the family of the patient.
3. Critical actions checklist
   1. Consider and evaluate for myocardial infarction.
      1. Obtain and assess initial ECG.
      2. Obtain cardiac injury panel.
      3. Administer oxygen via nasal cannula.
      4. Administer aspirin.
      5. Administer sublingual nitroglycerin.
      6. Administer morphine.
      7. Consider administration of a beta blocker.
      8. Consult cardiologist.
   2. Identify synthetic cannabinoid toxicity after considering appropriate differential diagnosis.
      1. Consult with toxicologist or Poison Control Center.
   3. Initiate management of synthetic cannabinoid toxicity.
      1. Obtain IV access.
      2. Administer IV benzodiazepine.
      3. Consider administration of IV diphenhydramine.
      4. Reassess patient, including vital signs and ECGs.
      5. Disposition – Admit to CCU.

V. Actors

1. Roles
   1. Nurse
   2. Toxicologist
      1. Will return call/page after approximately 5 minutes or after an incorrect diagnosis is pursued.
      2. May suggest synthetic cannabinoid toxicity as a possible diagnosis.
      3. May suggest benzodiazepine therapy for the above diagnosis.
      4. May ask direct questions to simulation participants:
         1. "What do you think is happening with this patient?"
         2. “What did he take?”
         3. “Has he taken anything else?”
   3. Cardiologist
      1. Will return call/page after approximately 5 minutes or after an incorrect diagnosis is pursued.
         1. Encourage the learner to suspect synthetic cannabinoid toxicity as a potential cause for myocardial ischemia, rather than simply detecting MI on ECG:
            1. If the learner asks to send the patient directly to the catheterization laboratory, delay the patient’s admission.

“We just received multiple MI patients and are currently at capacity.”

“We are on our way but will take five minutes to reach your location.”

      2. Encourage the learner to manage the patient before definitive treatment is achieved.

“Why is this patient having a myocardial infarction?”

“What have you done for the patient so far? What else can you do for him until we arrive?”

      3. May note that the patient has been seen for a similar episode but left against medical advice.
      4. May ask direct questions to simulation participants:
         1. “Do we need to send him to the catheterization laboratory?”
         2. “Is he having a myocardial infarction?”
         3. “Do we need to see him?”
   4. Patient
   5. Patient’s girlfriend

1. The team leader may be a third or fourth year medical student or an emergency medicine resident. The nurse, patient and patient’s girlfriend may be played by other residents, students, instructors or actors. The toxicologist and cardiologist may be played by simulation instructors who may enter the simulation lab in person or call in from outside.

VII. Instructors Notes

1. It is recommended that the level of agitation not escalate to the extent that the learner believes administration of haloperidol is necessary.
   1. The patient is not *physically* abusive but verbally threatens staff.
   2. The patient has not and does not try to harm himself or others, despite repeated threats.
2. Tips to direct actors:
   1. The nurse can emphasize his agitation:
      1. “[Team Leader], this patient has repeatedly threatened the nurses!”
      2. “The doctor is trying to help you! Please calm down!”
      3. “Leave your IV alone! You’ll hurt yourself if you pull it out!”
   2. The patient’s girlfriend can also emphasize his agitation:
      1. “He really only acts like this when he is using.”
3. Scenario programming (please see Appendix XIII for flow sheet)
   1. Optimal management path
      1. If problem recognized and critical actions taken:
         1. Benzodiazepine administered:
            1. Vital signs improve (BP – 130/90 mmHg, HR – 75 bpm)
   2. Potential complications path(s)
      1. If problem not recognized and critical actions not taken:
         1. ACS not considered:
            1. Rhythm changes to ventricular fibrillation after 15 minutes
   3. Potential errors path(s)
      1. ACS ruled out but benzodiazepine not given:
         1. Vital signs continue to elevate:
            1. BP – 210/130 mmHg
            2. HR – 160 bpm
         2. The patient becomes increasingly disoriented.
