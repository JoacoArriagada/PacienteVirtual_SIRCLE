# Educational Objectives

# Goal:

To teach the adolescent HEEADSSS interview to medical students.

# Learning Objectives:

Overall learning objective: After reading an article on the HSEADSSS interview and attending the Adolescent Medicine Learning Session with individualized goal setting and coached role play, $3 ^ { \mathsf { r d } }$ year medical students will identify a learning goal and perform a HEEADSSS examination of an adolescent patient coming in for contraception, as measured by an OSCE station with a standardized patient checklist.

Specific learning objectives: The learner will be able to

1. Name and describe the components of the HEEADSSS interview.   
2. Identify a specific individual learning goal for interviewing an adolescent using the HEEADSSS interview format.   
3. Observe a peer doing a HEEADSSS interview and provide feedback about the interview.   
4. Interview an adolescent, asking at least one question for each of the components of the HEEADSSS interview.

# Evaluation

The following evaluation tools are available.

The learning goals can be evaluated by documenting a. The number of students with a learning goal b. The number of students who complete their learning goal c. The quality of their learning goals

\`these learning goals may be scored with a rubric that has been previously validated and has been published (Lockspeiser T, Schmitter P, Lane J, Hanson J, Rosenberg A. A Validated Rubric for Scoring Learning Goals. MedEdPORTAL; 2013. Available from: www.mededportal.org/publication/9369) Evaluating the individual learning goals is optional. When used, the Individual Learning Goals Scoring Rubric assesses the quality of learning goals in relation to their specifity and importance and whether they include a realistic learning strategy and measurable outcome. In our program, students write learning goals for their pediatric clerkship and receive some instruction about choosing and formulating goals, so this is familiar to them. For the adolescent interview teaching session, we read the goals after the teaching session but do not grade them. The purpose of the goals is to help the students focus their learning during the session. The content of the goals provides formative evaluation data about the teaching session, guiding the teacher about areas that need emphasis in subsequent sessions to make them relevant to the learning level of medical students coming through the clerkship. Scoring the goals periodically provides additional information about the quality of goals that students produce for this session, giving us insight about instruction that may be needed during the clerkship about formulating useful learning goals.

Student performance on the standardized patient (SP) encounter can be evaluated by SP Checklist (see the standardized patient case in the Evaluation Tools validated communication skills checklist. One recommended communication skills scale is The New Mexico Clinical Communication Skills Checklist. (Copyright University of New Mexico School of Medicine; contact Nancy Sinclair, nsinclair@salud.unm.edu; Hanson J, LaRochelle J, O’Malley P, Wilson B (2011) Longitudinal Evaluation of Communication Skills,

International Conference on Communication in Healthcare. Chicago, IL, Oct. 16-19, 2011). This is one of several scales is based on the Kalamazoo Consensus Statement of communication skills of a competent physician. These skills include opening the discussion, building a relationship, gathering information, understanding the patient’s perspective, sharing information, reaching agreement and providing closure. (Makoul G, Essential elements of communication in medical encounters: The Kalamazoo consensus statement. Acad Med. 2001 Apr;76(4):390-3.) Including a valid assessment of communication skills in the evaluation ensures that the components of HEEADSSS interview are assessed within the context of good communication skills. The New Mexico Clinical Communication Skills Checklist has been validated at the University of New Mexico School of Medicine and at the Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences.

The SP Checklist was developed for this project and appears in the SP case that appears in the Evaluation Tools. It should be noted that not every component of the HEEADSSS interview is included in the SP Checklist. This case is written as a summative evaluation case, not a teaching case after which the learners received feedback. As such, it includes a sampling of the components of the HEEADSSS interview, although the students are taught the entire interview and, we hope, study and perform the entire interview, not knowing which items will be on the “test.” This is much like any exam, wherein we sample from among the entire set of knowledge to which learners have been exposed and for which they are expected to study, not knowing which items will be sampled on the test. For those programs that desire a standardized patient case that assesses the HEEADSSS interview with all components and provides a framework for feedback, a standardized case is available in MedEdPortal, previously published24.

By a process of consensus among medical educators involved with your project, set minimal acceptable levels of performance for the SP checklist and the New Mexico Clinical Communication Skills Checklist, using a standard-setting approach. In our setting we utilized our medical educators involved in pediatric clerkships.

To assess knowledge and confidence students gain for the HEEADSSS interview, one may use the retrospective pre/post questionnaire that appears in the Evaluation Tools. A retrospective pre/post questionnaire is administered after an educational intervention and asks the respondent to consider their knowledge, skills and/or attitudes before the intervention and compare them to their knowledge, skills and/or attitudes after the intervention has been completed. Published work by Kelly Skeff and colleagues in the early 1990’s demonstrated that a retrospective pre/post questionnaire is valid for educational interventions. In fact, in their studies, responses to a retrospective pre/post questionnaire correlated more closely with observed behaviors after an educational intervention when compared to the more familiar pre-test before an intervention and post-test afterwards20- 22.
