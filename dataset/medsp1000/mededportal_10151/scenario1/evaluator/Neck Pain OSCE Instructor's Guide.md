**Facilitator’s Guide**

### Educational Objectives

The objectives of this OSCE are aligned with the following curriculum guidelines: the neurology clerkship core curriculum guidelines[[1]](#endnote-1), endorsed by the American Academy of Neurology (AAN); the recommended curriculum guidelines[[2]](#endnote-2) for family medicine residents, endorsed by the American Academy of Family Physicians (AAFP); and the core curriculum[[3]](#endnote-3) for a first-year neurology resident, endorsed by the AAN. As a result, we feel that the OSCE could be used to assess competencies at the resident level.

1. The examinee will systematically evaluate a patient with neck pain.
2. The examinee will obtain a complete and reliable history.
3. The examinee will formulate a differential diagnosis based on lesion localization, time course, and relevant historical and demographic features.
4. The examinee will interpret abnormal findings of the neurologic examination.
5. The examinee will order and interpret common neurologic diagnostic tests.
6. The examinee will recognize and evaluate potentially life-threatening neurologic conditions.

**Justification and Development:**

We had been using 3 different headache OSCEs[[6]](#endnote-6),[[7]](#endnote-7),[[8]](#endnote-8) for summative purposes in our neurology clerkship. The OSCE score counts as 5% of the students’ final grade. We decided to create the new OSCE, because we heard that students talked with each other about the diagnoses. We needed an assessment that required students to take a detailed neck pain history from a standardized patient, generate an initial differential diagnosis, request and interpret a diagnostic evaluation, and defend the final diagnosis. In addition, we wanted students to determine whether the patient’s neck pain presentation required emergent diagnostic imaging.

Prior to the development of this neck pain OSCE, we conducted a literature search on MededPORTAL using the term “neck pain.” There were no published OSCEs. We developed an evaluation tool, in which a 65-year old patient (the standardized patient could be male or female) presents with neck pain. The age of onset brings into question degenerative conditions of the spine. However, if questioned further, the patient will reveal the history of colon cancer that had been resected. If the examinee asks, the patient will reveal that he or she is unclear as to the staging of the cancer and was lost to follow-up. There is also a history of unintentional weight loss. The patient’s physical complaints include worsening signs and symptoms of weakness of all extremities and urinary retention. The physical findings confirm the presence of myelopathy. The MRI is from an actual patient with metastatic colon cancer to a cervical vertebral body.

Materials needed:

* Hospital gown
* Hospital bed for the standardized patient is preferred. An exam table or a comfortable chair would be an alternative, if a bed is not available.
* Comfortable rolling chair or stool for the student
* Comfortable chair and table for facilitator
* Hand sanitizer or sink with soap, paper towels, and a trash can

Files:

Neck pain OSCE facilitator guide:   Print one copy of the document for each faculty member.  Copies may be in color or black and white, single or double sided.

Neck pain OSCE SP script:  Print one copy for each of the standardized patients and faculty members.  Copies may be in color or black and white, single or double sided.

Neck pain OSCE:  Print one copy for each examinee.  Copies MUST BE SINGLE-SIDED AND IN COLOR.  Place them in a 3-ring binder with tabs separating each copy.

Neck pain OSCE Examinee Clip Board:  Print one copy for each examinee and an extra copy to tape to the door.  Copies can be in color or black and white.

Length of session

15 minutes for the patient encounter

5 additional minutes for immediate feedback would be ideal. Scoring may have to be done at the end of the day.

Faculty preparation needs

Clinical faculty should be comfortable with history, physical examination and imaging findings of neck pain due to different causes. The case is very straightforward. Nevertheless, faculty should plan to spend an hour reviewing the resource files.

In addition to having our faculty raters read the OSCE, we ran some practice sessions. The first author and another faculty member posed as students. In some of the mock sessions, the “student” intentionally tried to trick the faculty members into revealing the correct answer. In others, the “student” intentionally failed the OSCE. The raters all rated the “students” correctly, regardless of their performance.

Standardized patient preparation

In preparation for the session, the standardized patients read the script and performed a practice session with the faculty member. For this OSCE, the standardized patients did not perform any formal written feedback.
