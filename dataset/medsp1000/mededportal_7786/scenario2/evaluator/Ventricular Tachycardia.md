# Background

Ventricular tachycardia (VT) is a cardiac rhythm disturbance that can be associated with significant signs and symptoms including; weakness, dizziness, syncope and hypotension. Medical students should be familiar with this arrhythmia including: the pathophysiology of its origin and the acute reflex compensations for it.

# Learning objectives for this exercise include

1. Perform a focused cardiopulmonary examination including examination of;

a. Heart sounds b. Lung sounds and determining respiratory rate c. Palpating pulses and determining heart rate d. Auscultation of the blood pressure

2. Review normal cardiac physiology.

3. Review the cardiac conduction system.

4. Review the cardiac physiology associated with VT including: effects on various hemodynamic parameters such as cardiac output (CO), mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) and cerebral blood flow (CBF).

5. Review the electrocardiographic manifestations of VT.

6. Provide an introduction to the initial management of a patient in VT.

Provide supervision regarding proper technique when the students are listening to the heart and lungs, palpating the pulse rate and measuring the blood pressure.

Serve as a facilitator for the remainder of the session to focus on the key basic science and clinical questions listed below. The goal is to reinforce the basic science knowledge that the students have and apply these principles to clinical patient care.

When addressing “how would you treat this patient”, you can talk about and demonstrate synchronized cardioversion and have the SimMan® convert to sinus rhythm after being shocked. You can allow some of the students to perform this skill. Use a low energy level setting on the cardioverter-defibrillator (3 – 5 joules) to avoid damaging the simulator.

# Explanation of Electrocardiograms (ECG) in Appendix (VT)

The Appendix contains three ECGs. Example A is VT with a ventricular rate of 170 bpm. Example B is supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) with aberrant conduction (right bundle branch block – RBBB). Example C is sinus rhythm with a RBBB (same patient as Appendix B). At some point in the exercise, the ECGs should be distributed and reviewed with the students. Both the VT and the SVT with aberrant conduction ECGs demonstrate a wide QRS complex $( Q { \mathsf { R S } } > 1 2 0 \mathsf { m s } )$ tachyarrhythmia where as Appendix C is a classic example of a wide complex rhythm that originates from the sinoatrial (SA) node.

When introducing the ECGs into the case, we typically use Appendix A as the patient’s ECG.

# Case History:

A 73-year-old patient presents to the emergency department with a complaint of chest pain and dizziness after passing out. These complaints started about 30 minutes before arrival while the patient was waiting in line at the bank.

The patient recalls feeling warm and flushed and was noted by a bystander at the bank to have a brief fainting episode.

Upon arrival at the hospital, the patient is awake, answering questions, but complains of feeling weak, chest heaviness and shortness of breath.

Past Medical History: HTN, diabetes

Medications: Clonidine, diltiazem, metformin

Allergies: None

Pertinent Review of Symptoms: Denies headache, nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain

Vital signs – HR 184 bpm, RR 26, BP 82/40 mmHg, SaO2 92% (increase to $9 7 \%$ with supplemental oxygen)

General Appearance The patient is awake, alert and able to answer your questions.

HEENT – Normal

Cardiac – Regular, no murmur

Lungs – Rales

Abdomen – Soft, non-tender

Extremities – no edema

Neuro – Alert, oriented, moves all extremities

Skin – Cool and clammy

# Key Cardiac Physiology and Clinical Questions:

 What are the patient’s vital signs?

HR 184 bpm, RR 26, BP 82/40 mmHg, $S a O _ { 2 }$ 92% (increase to 97% with supplemental oxygen)

#  Why did this patient pass out?

The patient developed VT which resulted in a marked increase in ventricular rate which lead to a decrease in CO ( $C O = H R X$ stroke volume - SV) which in turn lead to a precipitous drop in MAP $( M A P = C O \ X$ systemic vascular resistance - SVR). The above series of events resulted in a decrease in cerebral perfusion.

What is the definition of the term mean arterial blood pressure and how can it be estimated?

The MAP is defined as the average pressure in the arteries during the cardiac cycle.

What are the determinants of the mean arterial blood pressure?

The determinants of MAP are CO and $S V R ,$ $( M A P = C O \ X S V R )$ .

#  What are the determinants of cardiac output?

The determinants of CO are HR and SV, $( C O = H R \times S V )$ ).

# What is the definition of stroke volume?

SV represents the volume of blood that is pumped from the heart with each heart beat (from the ventricles).

 How will this patient’s heart rate affect stroke volume?

Because the ventricular rate is very fast, the SV will decrease.

 What effect does this patient’s heart rate have on cardiac output?

The CO is reduced due to the very fast ventricular rate and lack of a properly timed or coordinated atrial and ventricular contraction.

What are the determinants of cerebral blood flow?

The determinants of $C B F$ are cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) and cerebral vascular resistance (CVR), $C B F = \ C P P / C V R .$ 

# What are the determinants of cerebral perfusion pressure?

The determinants of CPP are MAP and the intracranial pressure (ICP), $C P P = M A P$ – ICP.

What mechanism is responsible for attempting to restore blood pressure when this patient was at the bank?

The mechanism is the baroreceptor reflex.

# Why is the patient’s skin cool and clammy?

Cutaneous vasoconstriction decreases cutaneous blood flow, reduces heat delivery to the skin which makes the skin cool.

What is the definition of tachycardia? $H R > 1 0 0$ bpm

Can you identify P waves, QRS complexes, and T waves on the electrocardiogram? (can use Appendix A, B and C)

In Appendix A and B, $P$ waves are not visualized, however QRS complexes, and T waves are identified.

Where in the cardiac conduction does the QRS complex originate from? (Appendix A, B and C)

In Appendix A, the QRS complex originates from a focus in the ventricular myocardium (purkinje system).

Can you explain why the QRS complex is wide (>120ms in duration)?

In this case, the dominant pacemaker of the heart originates from somewhere in the ventricular myocardium / purkinje system outside of the normal conduction pathways.

#  How would you treat this patient?

Our patient is unstable and requires immediate electrical synchronized cardioversion. The patient is hypotensive, is experiencing chest pain, shortness of breath and has evidence of pulmonary edema on physical examination – rales and distended neck veins.

 What is synchronized cardioversion and how does it differ from asynchronous defibrillation?

Cardioversion refers to the passage of a direct electrical current through the myocardium during a particular point in the cardiac cycle.

#  How does cardioversion or defibrillation work?

Cardioversion or defibrillation is accomplished by passage of a direct electrical current through the myocardium.
