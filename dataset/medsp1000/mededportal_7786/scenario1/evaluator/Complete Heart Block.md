# Background:

Complete heart block (CHB) is a cardiac rhythm disturbance that can be associated with significant signs and symptoms including; weakness, dizziness, syncope and hypotension. Medical students should be familiar with this arrhythmia including: the pathophysiology of its origin and the acute reflex compensations for it.

# Learning objectives for this exercise include:

1. Perform a focused cardiopulmonary examination including examination of;

a. Heart sounds b. Lung sounds and determining respiratory rate c. Palpating pulses and determining heart rate d. Auscultation of the blood pressure

2. Review normal cardiac physiology.

3. Review the cardiac conduction system.

4. Review the cardiac physiology associated with CHB including: effects on various hemodynamic parameters such as cardiac output (CO), mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) and cerebral blood flow (CBF).

5. Review the electrocardiographic manifestations of CHB.

6. Provide an introduction to the initial management of a patient in CHB.

# Explanation of Electrocardiograms (ECG) in Appendix

The Appendix contains two examples of CHB. Example A, is CHB with an idioventricular escape rhythm, example B, is CHB with a junctional escape rhythm. At some point in the exercise, the ECGs should be distributed and reviewed with the students. The key will be for the students to identify that there is no correlation between the atrial and ventricular activity, a hallmark of CHB. Of note on each example, the atrial rate is faster than the escape rhythm, this is another hallmark seen in almost all cases of CHB. The differences between the two examples reside in the rate of the escape rhythm and the width of the QRS complex. By appreciating this distinction, the student should better understand the difference between a junctional and ventricular escape rhythm. Appendix A is an example of CHB with a ventricular escape rhythm. The ventricular response is about 30 bpm and the QRS complex is wide $( > 1 2 0 \mathsf { m s } )$ . These two findings are associated with an escape rhythm that originates below the bifurcation of the bundle of His. It is possible for a ventricular escape rhythm to occur at a faster rate, but the QRS complex will always be wide. Appendix B shows an example of CHB, with a junctional escape rhythm. The ventricular response is almost 50 bpm and the QRS complex is narrow $( < 1 0 0 \mathsf { m s } )$ . These two findings are associated with an escape rhythm that is junctional in origin.

When introducing the ECGs into the case, we typically use Appendix A as the patient’s ECG. Appendix B is used to help review the concept of wide v. narrow QRS complexes as it relates to ventricular v. junctional escape rhythm.

Appendix C is an example of an ECG with a ventricular paced rhythm. The wide QRS complexes are immediately preceded by pacing spikes. In this case, the patient has a single pacemaker lead inserted in the apex of the right ventricle (single chamber pacemaker).

# Case History:

Mr. Stevens is a 73-year-old male who presents to the emergency department after “passing out.” Earlier today, while sitting on a bus on his way into town, he felt weak, dizzy, and was noted by his wife to collapse to the floor. According to his wife, he appeared to be unconscious for less than 1 minute. She states that when he was arousable, he seemed confused for just a few moments. No seizure activity or urinary incontinence was noted during this episode.

Upon arrival at the hospital, the patient is awake, answering questions, but stills complains of feeling weak and lightheaded.

Past Medical History: HTN, hypercholesterolemia

Medications: Metoprolol, diltiazem, pravachol

Allergies: None

Pertinent Review of Symptoms: Denies chest pain, shortness of breath, nausea or vomiting

Vital signs – HR 34 bpm, RR 16, BP 84/42 mmHg, and $\mathsf { S a O } _ { 2 } 9 7 \%$ on room air

# General Appearance

He is awake, alert and able to answer your questions.

HEENT – Normal Cardiac – Regular, no murmurs

Lungs – Clear in all fields

Abdomen – Soft, non-tender Extremities – no edema

Neuro – Alert, oriented, moves all extremities

Skin – Cool and clammy

# Key Cardiac Physiology and Clinical Questions

What are the patient’s vital signs? HR 34 bpm, RR 16, BP 84/42 mmHg, and $S a O _ { 2 }$ $9 7 \%$ on room air

 Why did this patient pass out?

The patient developed CHB which resulted in a marked decrease in ventricular rate which lead to a decrease in CO $\mathrm { \mathop { C O } } = H R X$ stroke volume - SV) which in turn lead to a precipitous drop in MAP $( M A P = C O \ X$ systemic vascular resistance - SVR). The above series of events resulted in a decrease in cerebral perfusion. Syncope (fainting), can be defined as a transient loss of consciousness. It is important to emphasize that the final common pathway responsible for syncope in any patient is a transient decrease in CBF. The ongoing function of the brain also depends on the delivery of two substrates – oxygen and glucose.

 What is the definition of the term mean arterial blood pressure and how can it be estimated?

The MAP is defined as the average pressure in the arteries during the cardiac cycle. $M A P = l ( 2 \ x$ diastolic pressure) $^ +$ systolic pressure] / 3, or $M A P =$ Diastolic pressure $^ +$ 1/3 pulse pressure.

 What are the determinants of the mean arterial blood pressure?

The determinants of MAP are CO and SVR, $( M A P = C O \ X S V R )$ .

What are the determinants of cardiac output?

The determinants of CO are HR and SV, $( C O = H R \times S V _ { \prime }$ ).

# What is the definition of stroke volume?

SV represents the volume of blood that is pumped from the heart with each heart beat (from the ventricles).

How will this patient’s heart rate affect his stroke volume?

Because the ventricular rate is very slow, the SV may be increased as the diastolic filling time is prolonged.

 What effect does this patient’s heart rate have on his cardiac output?

The CO is reduced due to the very slow ventricular rate and lack of a properly timed or coordinated atrial and ventricular contraction.

What are the determinants of cerebral blood flow?

The determinants of CBF are cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) and cerebral vascular resistance (CVR), $C B F = \ C P P / C V R .$ 

What are the determinants of cerebral perfusion pressure?

The determinants of CPP are MAP and the intracranial pressure (ICP), $C P P = M A P$ – ICP.

What mechanism is responsible for attempting to restore blood pressure when this patient was on the bus?

The mechanism is the baroreceptor reflex.

#  Why is the patient’s skin cool and clammy?

Cutaneous vasoconstriction decreases cutaneous blood flow, reduces heat delivery to the skin which makes the skin cool.

 Will activation of the baroreceptor reflex increase the ventricular rate?

It is unlikely that activation of this reflex will have any measurable effect on the ventricular escape rhythm.

What is the definition of bradycardia? $H R < 6 0$ bpm

#  Why is this patient bradycardic?

Our patient is in CHB.

Can you identify P waves, QRS complexes, and T waves on the electrocardiogram? (can use either Appendix A or B)

$P$ waves, QRS complexes, and T waves are identified.

 Is each P wave associated with a QRS complex? (can use Appendix A or B)

No.

What is the atrial rate (frequency of P waves)? Appendix A – Atrial rate \~90bpm. Appendix B – Atrial rate \~90bpm.

What is the ventricular rate (frequency of QRS complexes)? Appendix A – Ventricular rate \~30bpm. Appendix B – Ventricular rate \~50bpm.

Where are the 2 independent pacemakers located that are responsible for this rhythm disturbance? (Appendix A)

The atrial pacemaker is located within the SA node; the ventricular pacemaker is located within the ventricular myocardium or purkinje system, below the Bundle of His.

When palpating the patient’s pulse, does the HR correlate with the atrial or ventricular rate on the electrocardiogram?

The patient’s pulse rate corresponds to the ventricular rate.

# What is the initial treatment of complete heart block?

Vagolytic medication such as atropine can be administered but may not provide any added benefit as the ventricular escape rhythm is “infranodal” (Appendix A). In our case, transcutaneous pacing will need to be initiated as a bridge to temporary transvenous pacing.

#  In what chamber(s) of the heart is a permanent pacemaker lead placed?

A permanent pacemaker is typically inserted in the left subclavicular fossa.

# What is a demand pacemaker?

Pacemakers are very advanced devices that have both pacing and sensing functions.
