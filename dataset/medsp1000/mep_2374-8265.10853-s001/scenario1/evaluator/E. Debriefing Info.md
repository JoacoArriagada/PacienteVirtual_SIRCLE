**Guidelines for the use of a Medical Interpreter**

Any patient with limited English proficiency has the right to discuss their medical care in their fluent language. In some cases, the need for an interpreter will have been identified by other medical professionals (i.e. nurse or registration personnel). However, if no interpreter is present and you suspect the patient may have limited English proficiency, you must inquire if they wish for an interpreter to be present for the encounter. Below are general guidelines for working with a medical interpreter.1 Please note that your institution likely has its own guidelines for patients with limited English proficiency, which may differ from these general tips.

* In-person interpreters are preferred. However, interpretation via a remote service using telephones or other electronic devices is acceptable.
* If you are fluent in the patient’s preferred language, you may speak directly using that language, provided you have satisfied any requirements your institution may impose to certify your fluency.
* In emergency circumstances, if an interpreter is not immediately available, it is reasonable to use an *ad hoc* interpreter (family, friend, employee) until a certified medical interpreter becomes available. The *ad hoc* interpreter should be instructed to interpret exactly what is said without editorializing.
* Be careful using family members as *ad hoc* interpreters. Family members often editorialize, may control the interaction, and such use may violate the patient’s ability to control confidential information.
* When using a medical interpreter:
  + Face and speak directly to the patient, not the interpreter.
  + Use “I” and “you” just as you normally would in conversation
    - *i.e. Say “Are you having pain?”, not “Ask the patient if they are having pain.”*
  + Keep information to be interpreted in shorter, “digestible” bites. This is especially important when counseling a patient. Pause frequently to allow interpretation of each piece of information prior to moving on to another subject.

**Eclampsia Educational Points**

**Pathophysiology**: The cause of eclamptic seizures remains unclear, although it is believed to involve alterations in cerebral blood flow or cerebral perfusion pressure

**Diagnosis:** Eclampsia is diagnosed by the new onset of a seizure (tonic-clonic or focal) in a pregnant, intrapartum or postpartum patient, in the absence of other clear causes of the seizure. It typically occurs in a patient who has a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy such as preeclampsia, gestational hypertension or HELLP syndrome.

Common, but not essential, associated signs or symptoms include hypertension (systolic >140 or diastolic >90), proteinuria (at least 1+ on dipstick), edema, or abnormal weight gain (>2 pounds per week in the third trimester). Peri-convulsive symptoms frequently include headaches, blurred vision, altered mental status, photophobia or epigastric/right upper-quadrant abdominal pain.

Eclamptic seizures can occur before, during or after delivery. In most case series, the majority of seizures occur antepartum (primarily in the third trimester), and nearly all occur within 48 hours postpartum. However, eclamptic seizures have occurred at less than 20 weeks gestation and at up to 4 weeks postpartum.

**Stabilization and Treatment:**

1. Begin with assessment and stabilization of the patient’s airway, breathing and circulation. The patient may require suctioning, positioning or intubation to protect the airway from aspiration. Supplemental oxygen may be required, and at least 2 large bore IV’s should be placed.
2. The mother should be protected from trauma related to the convulsive activity, including use of seizure guards on the bed railings.
3. A benzodiazepine (such as 2-4 mg of lorazepam) may be given in an attempt to abort the seizure
4. Future seizures may be prevented by giving 6g magnesium sulfate bolus over 15-20 minutes, followed by a 2g/hour maintenance infusion.
   1. The maintenance infusion should be held if the patellar reflex is absent, respirations are less than 12 per minutes, or if urine output drops to <25 mL/hour
   2. If the patient becomes hypopneic due to magnesium toxicity, calcium gluconate (1g IV) can be given
5. Hypertension should be treated with IV labetalol, IV hydralazine, PO nifedipine, or IV nicardipine can be used. Target blood pressure <160/110
6. Consult Ob/Gyn for further management and admission
