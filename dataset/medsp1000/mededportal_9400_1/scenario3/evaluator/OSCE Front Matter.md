OSCE Front Matter

<u>**Purpose**</u>

The goal of the Spanish OSCE is to evaluate and provide formative feedback regarding students’ Spanish clinical skills.  This Certification component exam aims to assess those skills that cannot be measured in the prompted phone exams.  Most importantly, this exam was designed to ensure that speaking and thinking in Spanish does not interfere with students’ abilities to remember and perform all relevant components of the history and physical exam, remember the information that they obtain in the clinical encounter, recognize the pertinent positive and negative findings, communicate those findings to their team members by documenting them in English, and maintain the analytical thought process necessary to formulate a diagnostic workup, an accurate diagnosis, and an appropriate treatment plan.  Furthermore, as this is a face-to-face encounter, it permits evaluation of interpersonal skills beyond those demonstrated in a phone exam.  Finally, the OSCE provides students with detailed, interactive feedback regarding their Spanish fluency, cultural competency, and clinical skills.

<u>**Development Process**</u>

We felt it was important that students demonstrate the same level of clinical skills while speaking Spanish as they do while speaking English.  As a result, we chose to adapt the three OSCE cases that we use to evaluate students’ clinical skills before they are permitted to begin their clinical clerkships by translating them into Spanish and adding language fluency and cultural competency feedback components.  We did not change any clinical information or evaluation criteria in order to ensure that students were held to the same standards when speaking both Spanish and English.  Although we translated the clinical cases into Spanish we continued to ask students to complete the clinical documentation portion of the exam in English.  As a result, the OSCE mirrors actual clinical situations in which students speak Spanish while caring for patients but communicate directly with their health care team members and provide medical record documentation in English.

<u>**How the Materials Have Been Used**</u>

This OSCE is used for both assessment and training.  The primary outcome of the OSCE is a pass/fail score that determines whether or not students can competently perform the duties of a medical student while caring for a Spanish-speaking patient.  However, review of the students’ objective scores on each section of the exam as well as their formative feedback allows students to identify their relative strengths and weaknesses and guides their self study and ultimate improvement.

<u>**Training Methods**</u>

Standardized patients were selected based on their ability to portray the clinical case, evaluate students, and provide students with feedback.  Additionally, the standardized patients were native Spanish-speaking females who are approximately 25-40 years old.

To prepare the standardized patients for this OSCE, we provided them with the Spanish OSCE cases to review on their own.  Then the standardized patients came to the clinical skills center and watched a video of the OSCE.  Subsequently, the standardized patients role played the case with a standardized patient trainer.  On the day of the OSCE all of the standardized patients met as a group to discuss the case and ensure that they planned to portray the case in the same manner using the same language.

<u>**Data to support the content of the materials**</u>

As the cases were originally written to evaluate students’ skills prior to beginning their third year clerkships, the content was designed to reflect the objectives and competencies of the Patient Centered Medicine 2 (second year clinical skills) course.  A team of internal medicine physicians referenced literature and practice guidelines while writing the OSCE cases.  An obstetrician/gynecologist also reviewed the cases to verify that they were clinically relevant.

<u>**Reliability of Checklists and Rating Scales**</u>

The faculty determined which clinical criteria were relevant to include in the computer portion of the exam.  They then piloted the OSCE cases by having physicians complete the exams.  Each year we review student performance on the clinical criteria items and consider eliminating questions that are answered correctly by less than or equal to three percent of students.

The Patient Perception Scale is an adaptation of a similar evaluation tool developed by the American Board of Internal Medicine.

<u>**Accuracy of Case Portrayal**</u>

To ensure the standardized patients accurately portrayed the cases we hired an extra standardized patient to watch the exam videos in real time to monitor the clinical portrayal and complete the standardized patient checklist.  We considered the case portrayal to be accurate if the observing standardized patient did not observe significant errors in clinical criteria conveyed and if the two standardized patients had 90% agreement in checklist completion.  Additionally, the bilingual faculty reviewed the exam videos to monitor the same criteria.  Finally, students provided feedback on the standardized patients’ performances.  The evaluation criteria included both “realism of encounter” and “realism of clinical signs.”

<u>**Data from Use**</u>

To date, 39 students have completed the OSCE with a 92% pass rate.  One student has retaken the OSCE and passed on the second attempt.  Overall, the Certification had an 82% pass rate.  All of the Certification exams were failed by at least one student.  Of the students who failed to earn the Certification, three failed one exam, three failed two exams, and one failed three exams.  The fact that there was not a perfect concordance between the three exams may show that they each measure different skills and that it is important to include all exams to appropriately evaluate students’ abilities to care for Spanish-speaking patients.

<u>**General Suggestions for Use**</u>

1. Students have 30 minutes to complete the encounter including the history and physical exam.  Students and standardized patients must speak Spanish during the entirety of the encounter.
2. Provide students with a ten minute warning prior to the end of the encounter.
3. Announce the end of the encounter and ask the students to step out of the exam rooms and wait in the hallway.
4. Allow standardized patients to complete the History and Physical Checklist, Patient Perception Scale, and Clinical Spanish Evaluation.
5. Once standardized patients complete the evaluation paperwork, they should invite the students back into the exam room and provide the students with verbal feedback.
6. After receiving feedback, instruct students to begin the computer portion of the exam.
7. Students have one hour to complete the computer portion of the exam.  Announce when there are fifteen minutes remaining and again when the computer portion of the exam is finished.
8. Allow students to review their History and Physical Checklist, Patient Perception Scale, Clinical Spanish Evaluation, and Computer Exam Checklist.  Review the students’ performances with them and help them identify opportunities for improvement.
9. Have the Bilingual Certification Faculty meet with students who fail the exam to identify areas of weakness and create a plan for remediation.  Areas of weakness may include: 1. the student did not have a broad enough medical Spanish vocabulary or sufficient Spanish fluency to interview and respond to the patient; 2. the student did not have sufficient medical or general Spanish vocabulary to understand the patient; 3. the student did not have strong enough clinical skills to complete the required tasks; 4. the student was unable to remember the information obtained from the patient; 5. the student did not have sufficient interpreting or translating skills; 6. the student was unable to document in English the information that he obtained in Spanish; 7. focusing on speaking Spanish prevented the student from being able to maintain a usual level of clinical skills; and 8. the student failed to appreciate the nonverbal cues the patient presented during communication.
