**Explanation of resources**

Instructors are encouraged to review the goals and objectives (see below), case narrative with teaching points, the previous emergency department records, and the resident/student performance evaluation prior to the start of the case. When ready to begin the case, the triage note is provided.

Once the case is initiated, the instructor should use the case narrative to provide the history from the patient. When a performer either asks, “What do I see when I walk in the room?” or when a physical exam is initiated, the clinical photos should be used to supplement the case to provide the accurate representation of the cyanosis present in the patient. The initial vital signs and pulse oximetry will be displayed when the performer orders monitoring and pulse oximetry, revealing tachycardia and hypoxia.

While it is not absolutely essential to review the previous day’s emergency department record, it will provide an account of the previous day’s events, whereas the patient only offers that he had some blood work and a CT scan and was told everything “looked OK.” The previous record is also supplemented by the availability of lab tests and imaging for review if requested, which may greatly influence what tests may be ordered during the repeat visit.

One of the most important resources for the case is the medication list provided from the correctional facility, as the dapsone is the culprit medication causing the methemoglobinemia. The patient cannot name them, but the list is provided. In order to be realistic, the instructor should change the date at the top of the form, and place written initials in the boxes, indicating that the medications had been given by the correctional health staff.

The tests results for the case are provided only as the learner orders the tests. An EKG, chest x-ray, head CT, and individual laboratory results are provided during the case only if ordered. The one exception is the result for an arterial blood gas, as the laboratory reports that the machine is, “unable to run due to sample error.”

At the conclusion of the case, the instructor may wish to evaluate the learner’s performance with the provided resident/student performance evaluation. The case narrative file also contains the debriefing materials for the case. In general, the learner should self-assess first. It may be helpful to have the learner describe what clinical problems were present, the working differential diagnosis, review the steps taken to diagnose the problem, and discuss the management. The major teaching points for the case are also provided in the case narrative file. References are provided at the end of the case narrative file, and may be provided to the learner for independent review.

**Tips to keep scenario flowing in the lab**

1. The scenario requires a differential diagnosis that includes hemoglobinopathy, but the patient’s history of HIV may lead the performer to address mainly infectious etiologies for the headache, dizziness, or low oxygen saturations. The work-up on this case may become very extensive, particularly if not attentive to the physical findings and reviewing the medications in the file from correctional facility.
2. The performer may request the previous day’s emergency department record, which contains the initial history and physical exam and progress notes from physicians and nurses, describing the course. The images and labs from the previous day’s evaluation are also available if the learner wants to view the actual images, though this is not a necessity, as the written record had lab results “pasted” into the notes, and notes accurately describing imaging as unremarkable. If deemed unnecessary, reports of normal results and emergency department course can be provided by the examiner.
3. The learner will frequently focus on the HIV status and pneumonia history of the patient, and will pursue infectious etiologies for the case. Several learners have requested lumbar punctures during the course of the scenario. In those instances, we have had the patient refuse the lumbar puncture, though a normal lumbar puncture result could be provided. If the scenario is thought to be infectious by the performer, two options may be taken:
   1. Have the scenario conclude with a disposition, and begin the debriefing, with a more extensive discussion of the differential diagnosis, knowledge gaps, or diagnostic mistakes that led to an incorrect diagnosis.
   2. Prompts may be given that help to shift focus on the problems of low oxygen saturations.
      1. Nurse may ask, “Why do you think his oxygen saturation did not go up after we gave him oxygen?”
      2. Nurse may ask, “Do you think his bluish color is from Raynaud’s?”
      3. If an arterial blood gas is ordered, nurse may say, “I know I hit the artery, but the sample is so brown. Do you want me to send it?”
4. If the provider gets stuck, one staff intervention could be provided to prompt the case. If facemask oxygen is not ordered by the provider, the nurse may prompt, “Hey his oxygen saturations remained low, and didn’t come up after I put him on a non-rebreather. Anything else you want me to do?”
5. When all requested results are back, the prison guard may also ask about a disposition, saying they need to call back to let the facility know if he’ll be coming back or be admitted to the hospital.
6. The file for initiating the case is provided. We recommend scrolling the changes in heart rate, respiratory rate, and pulse oximetry as provided in the above flow chart (ie, no need for a pre-designated, programmed path for the case).
