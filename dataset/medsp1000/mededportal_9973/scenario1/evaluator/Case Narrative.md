Teaching/discussion points

Methemoglobinemia occurs when iron becomes oxidized, transforming from the ferrous state $( \mathsf { F e } ^ { + + } )$ to the ferric state $( \mathsf { F e } ^ { + + + } )$ . Hemoglobin will transport an oxygen molecule only when the iron atom is in the ferrous state, so elevated levels of methemoglobinemia alter oxygen transport, resulting in cyanosis. Rarely, hereditary deficiencies of NADH methemoglobin reductase can cause methemoglobinemia, but acquired methemoglobinemia is more common. Multiple medications may cause methemoglobinemia, but is most often caused by benzocaine spray and dapsone.

There is some methemoglobin present in normal individuals, but signs and symptoms correlate to increasing levels of methemoglobin. The simulation displayed the common features of severe methemoglobinemia; cyanosis, tachycardia, fatigue, dizziness, headache, and dyspnea that is worsened with exertion. The pulse oximetry reading tends to approach $8 5 \%$ , and is not changed by supplemental oxygen.

The diagnosis is made by measurement of methemoglobin levels. Characteristically, arterial blood sampling will reveal a chocolate brown appearance, though arterial $\mathsf { P O } _ { 2 }$ will be normal, as $\mathsf { P O } _ { 2 }$ is a measure of dissolved oxygen in blood which is unaffected by methemoglobinemia.

Management depends on the severity of toxicity. Low levels only require removing the oxidative stress. Higher levels associated with signs and symptoms should be treated with intravenous methylene blue at 1-2 mg/kg. Clinical improvement occurs in about 30 minutes. Cimetidine may also be useful in dapsone-induced methemoglobinemia, as its metabolites are oxidants with a long half-life, and cimetidine is an inhibitor of dapsone metabolism. Paradoxically, high doses of methylene blue $( > 7 \mathrm { \ m g / k g ) }$ may actually worsen methemoglobinemia rather than reverse the toxicity.

As this patient reported a similar reaction in the past with Bactrim, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency was considered and evaluated before administering methylene blue, as cyanosis potentially may have been made worse if G6PD deficiency was present and methylene blue was administered. In the event the patient tested positive for G6PD deficiency, exchange transfusion would have been an alternate treatment strategy for this patient.

Once methylene blue was given, the patient’s signs and symptoms resolved, and he was taken off dapsone. Interestingly, the Infectious Disease doctor recommended placing him on atovaquone to replace dapsone for Pneumocystis jiroveci prophylaxis. The patient returned again several days later with similar symptoms, though not as severe, and again had elevated methemoglobin levels.

# Why?

Answer: Atovaquone also may cause methemoglobinemia.
