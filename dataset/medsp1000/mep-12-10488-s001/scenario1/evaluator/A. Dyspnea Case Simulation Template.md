**Ideal Scenario Flow**

After receiving the “hand-off” from the evening charge nurse, the night nurses enter the room to find a patient in respiratory distress. They recognize that the patient is in respiratory distress and immediately place the patient on bedside monitors and recognize that the patient is hypoxic. A brief history and physical exam to include heart and breath sounds with tachycardia, elevated respiratory rate and crackles both lung bases. Supplemental oxygen started via cannula, patient is laid down in bed and head of bed elevated. The patient’s respiratory distress does not improve. The nurses “huddle” and a decision is made to call the medical team. The medical and pharmacy teams arrive and introduce themselves to the patient and other team members. The nurses use SBAR to report change in patient status and to provide most recent hemoglobin. A “brief” with the team is done now or after initial assessment. The team leader assigns a specific team member to assess heart and breath sounds and the request is followed by a “cross-check” followed by a “check-back” to the team leader related to findings that include crackles in lower bases, worsening tachycardia, respiratory rate and fall blood pressure. As a member of the team, the patient is questioned to clarify history and present status. The team reviews patient chart and most recent ED labs and chest x-ray with other team members and team leader asks for team input and a plan developed. Nurse requests that new vital signs be displayed on telemetry monitor and nurse “calls-out” a request to change to oxygen mask as O2 Sat% drops at 6 liters/min., heart rate increased and blood pressure lower. Team leader gives a “check-back” to the nurse, repeating correct oxygen dosing. A medical team member “calls-out” a request for an x-ray and team leader “cross-checks” and supports order for an x-ray, team member returns later with abnormal result and “checks-back” with team leader. Pharmacist “calls-out” a request to give Lasix IV push after asking patient if he has ever taken Lasix. Team leader asks what other team members think and if order for Lasix IV push stat is an appropriate dose, pharmacist repeats order in a “cross-check” and then “checks-back” when given. IF the dosage is not appropriate, a team member with “CUS” the dosage and then a team huddle is called by the leader to discuss the correct dosage. Other team members may “call-out” other suggestions for blood work for a D-dimer, Coags, cardiac enzymes, CMP, CBC with subsequent “cross-check” and check-back” when results returned.During the scenario, team members are monitoring the patient and comforting him. Once Lasix given, SP opens valve and urine begins to flow into collection bag and a team member “calls-out” this response to treatment. Nurse requests new vital signs be displayed and “calls-out” to team leader, stable vital signs. A team member assess lung and heart sounds and “calls-out” findings to team leader, lungs have cleared and there is an aortic murmur. At this point, the morning shift cross-cover intern comes in for a “hand-off” from the team.
During the scenario, a team member may “CUS” a treatment decision and if not acknowledged by the team, that team member invokes the “Two-Challenge” rule. (See Appendix C for TeamSTEPPS® communication skills prompt card)

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

The following are common problems encountered by faculty and learners during the scenario. Clear goals, detailed orientation for faculty and learners to scenario environment, and faculty coaching can go far in preventing these from happening. Suggestions for prevention and problem-solving are included.

1. Incorrect diagnostic path: There is a delicate balance of when faculty needs to intervene and allowing the team the opportunity to work together towards a corrected course of action. Often it will depend on how much time is left in the scenario. Stepping in and suggesting a team “huddle” when the scenario is going in an un-desirable direction, for example, the team decides the patient is having an MI instead of CHF. Faculty will suggest the team review what they know and encourage learners from each profession to provide their suggestions and concerns to allow team to reorganize their plan in the appropriate direction. Any of the faculty may request a huddle at any time.

2. Uncertainty about role: Profession-specific faculty are encouraged to whisper in the ear of their respective learners to prompt team participation, such as questioning a leader’s treatment decision, provide information overlooked or unknown to entire team, or to offer an insight about patient or treatment that is critical to patient care.

3. Problems with specialized equipment: Use of a Lecat’s Ventriloscope® or other device to simulate normal and abnormal heart and breath sounds, may require separate staging and training of techs and SPs. Learners often have difficulty using these devices due to incorrect seating of earpieces in learner’s ear canals. If the heart and breath sounds are also attached to a loud speaker, everyone can hear the sounds.

4. Medical management not the goal: Avoid spending too much time on medical management of dyspnea in the brief or during debrief as the focus of the simulation is team-building. Learners often feel more comfortable discussing patient management than team skills.

5. Requests made during scenario for items not available: If scenario participants request something that had not been planned for or is not appropriate for the scenario, e.g., requesting respiratory therapy, tell them that “All hospital resources are presently treating accident victims in the ED” or something similar.

6. Failure to respond to patient worsening shortness of breath: If team does not respond to patient’s initial shortness of breath or if intervention selected does not relieve shortness of breath, standardized patient is to exaggerate breathing further and ask for help.

Instructor Notes

The following are issues that have been encountered during the five years this scenario has been used in simulation using the hospitalized patient with dyspnea case. Options and opinions based on experience are provided.

1. Use of standardized patient vs mannequin: A standardized patient is an asset in demonstrating the concept that the patient is part of the team. The scenario guide is written for a standardized patient.

2. Connecting theory to practice: A white board can be used to make responses to the medical management review visible to all learners to assist in connecting theory and practice during the scenario.

3. Dates on supplies, labs, etc.: Do not use actual dates on IV bags, lab results, etc. as they may become “outdated” when re-used in the future. Instead, use “Today, or “Yesterday”. Times can be more specific, e.g., the time blood products were hung.

4. Option for medical management review: Medical management of dyspnea may be reviewed prior to starting the scenario or once the scenario has ended as part of debrief. The advantage of reviewing before and writing key elements on a white board, is the team can reference it during the scenario. Reviewing after the scenario, has the advantage of being able to relate actual care provided and reflection on the team’s clinical reasoning.

5. Ordering and displaying diagnostic results: Accessing patient chart notes and receive diagnostic testing results during the scenario requires advance preparation. To simulate an EHR, a laptop with a mock patient chart and diagnostic testing results can be programmed for realism. Having a monitor large enough for all participants to view the results is best. This may be combined with the use of paper, e.g., ED chart note and hand-off sheets for nursing and medical learners to have their own copy to review and to make notes on during the scenario.

6. All learners participate: Learners who are not participating directly in the scenario, are designated to be observers and given a peer evaluation of team communication skills form to complete. This prevents too many learners participating in a scenario at any one time and learners rotate between learner and observer roles during the 3 scenarios.

7. Team skill feedback: Best delivered during debrief rather than by the profession-specific faculty coaching during the scenario.

8. Encroachment on debriefing time: Learning takes place during the debrief discussion, so staying on time within scenarios to be sure there is adequate time for debriefing. A faculty volunteer may be appointed this job.

9. Lack of profession-specific learner to fill scenario: If a session does not have a learner from a specific profession then the profession-specific faculty may step in to fill that role during the scenario simulation. We do not recommend learners from other professions playing the role of another profession. If there is not a faculty volunteer or a learner for a role, the simulation can proceed without that role being filled.
