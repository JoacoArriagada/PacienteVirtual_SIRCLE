**A Critically Ill Newborn : Congenital Heart Disease**

1. **TITLE OF CASE:** Critically Ill Neonate: Congenital Heart Disease
2. **TARGET AUDIENCE:** Resident physicians and medical students
3. **LEARNING OBJECTIVES:**
   1. **Primary Learning Objectives**
      1. Demonstrate an initial approach to a critical neonate including stabilization of the ABCs
      2. Recognize absence of femoral pulses and persistent hypoxia as concerning for congential heart lesion
      3. Identify cardiomegaly on chest x-ray
      4. Recognize the need for prostaglandin
      5. Identify locations for emergency access in a neonate and utilize one of these routes promptly when an IV cannot be established promptly
   2. **Secondary Learning Objectives**
      1. Perform advanced airway management when patient becomes apneic from prostaglandin initiation
      2. Disposition the patient to a location that includes pediatric cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery and intensive care resources.
   3. **Critical Actions**
      1. Perform focused history and physical exam based on degree of extremis
      2. Obtain chest x-ray
      3. Obtain access and initiate normal saline fluid resuscitation
      4. Initiate prostaglandin therapy
      5. Discuss case with cardiology or cardiothoracic surgery for acute intervention
4. **ENVIRONMENT:**

**A. Lab set up** – Pediatric Emergency Department bed in simulation center or true emergency department

**B. Manikin set up** – The manikin will be a newborn size manikin. A simulator which can accommodate umbilical access is optional and would allow the facilitator to incorporate education related to umbilical access. However, umbilical access is not required in this patient scenario and a manikin without such access is also acceptable. At the beginning of the simulation, there are no monitor leads on the patient, no IV access has been obtained, and the patient is not on oxygen.

**C. Props** – Available for use will be a heart monitor with leads, appropriately sized blood pressure cuff, and two pulse oximeter probes. Supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula, face mask and neonatal bag valve mask will be available. There will be a medicine cart or tray with a full complement of vasoactive agents, PALS medications, medicines necessary for sedation, rapid sequence intubation, and analgesia. This cart should have a Broselow tape available. In addition, a fully stocked code cart with defibrillator will be available for use, along with a selection of direct laryngoscopes and intubation supplies.

**D. Audiovisual** – (See Appendix A) Available for review as requested by participants will be a single view portable chest x-ray and laboratory studies.

**E. Historians/Distracters** – An historian, such as a parent, can be added at the facilitator’s discretion. They may be prepared in advance to provide the history discussed later in the document. If no role player is available, the faculty facilitator may simply provide history as it is requested by the learner. As written, there is no need for distractors as the goal is management of a medically complex patient.

1. **ROLES:**
   1. Roles can vary depending on the number of participants in the simulation session and on potential actor availability. This case can be implemented using as little as a single participant and one facilitator/operator who can provide oral feedback via overhead audio and play additional historical or consultant roles via voice only. Realism can be enhanced by using additional team members, such as nursing staff members, but these are not required to successfully implement this case. It is always helpful in simulation to ask participants to assign roles prior to beginning the case, in order to dedicate all case time to the simulated patient encounter.
   2. Roles may be played by resident or faculty physicians, nurses, or medical students.
   3. Actions for the roles will be as follows:
      1. Primary physician: The main scenario participant will act as the primary physician. This lead physician should perform the primary evaluation of the patient by obtaining a history, conducting a physical exam, and ordering any necessary medications or other interventions. The primary physician can perform any needed procedures, or delegate these to other providers if multiple participants are present.
      2. Secondary physicians: Other participants in the scenario will serve as collaborators and may assist with or primarily perform any necessary procedures as delegated by the primary physician. They may role play nursing or tech roles within the scenario if only physicians are participating in the simulation.
      3. Nursing staff: The role of the nurse will be to administer medications, verify orders, and perform other tasks as directed by the physicians. The nurse can also make observations as needed to stimulate case progression.
2. **CASE NARRATIVE:**
3. **Scenario Background**
   1. Chief Complaint: Respiratory Distress
   2. Triage Nursing Note: Patient complains of difficulty breathing and decreased feeding
   3. Vital Signs: Temperature: 95.3 rectal Heart Rate: 215 Respiratory Rate: 96 Oxygen saturation: unable to obtain Blood Pressure: If cuff is placed on an upper extremity should be 111/83. If placed on a lower extremity it should be initially unobtainable .
   4. Demographics: 8 day old Caucasian male
   5. Past Medical History: Born by elective repeat Cesarean section at 39 weeks to a G3P3 mother who had all routine prenatal care. The baby had a normal hospital course, including newborn pulse oximetry screening and was discharged at three days old.
   6. Medications: None
   7. Allergies: No known allergies
   8. Family and Social History: Maternal grandmother with hypertension and coronary artery disease. Paternal grandparents with diabetes.
4. **Initial Scenario Conditions**
   1. **History given by Mother:** Mother reports that child developed severe respiratory distress today. Her son has been seen at her Pediatrician’s twice since birth for weight checks. He hasn’t seemed to breastfeed as well as her two older children and had been continuing to lose weight. Despite this he had been doing well until overnight when he stopped feeding and seemed to be breathing increasingly hard. She called the pediatrician who recommended coming to the Emergency Department given his age and her description of his difficulty breathing.
   2. **Circumstances at symptom onset:** Patient has been fussy all night and breathing increasingly harder. He seemed hungry all night, but would not feed.
   3. **Associated symptoms (given by system as review of system is completed):**  Patient has respiratory distress. He has been fussy and feeding poorly. He has decreased from birth weight of 9 lbs and 4 ozs to 7 lbs and 10 ozs. He made no wet diapers overnight. He has not had fever. He has not had vomiting. Stools are normal. Remainder of review of systems is unremarkable.
   4. **Initial Exam: (A complete initial physical exam follows, however, given the degree of extremis many learners will not illicit this full exam.)**
      1. **General**: Patient is in extremis. He is poorly perfused with mottled skin. He has significant respiratory distress with grunting. He is crying persistently. He is lying on the bed with very little movement.
      2. **HEENT**: Atraumatic and normocephalic. Pupils are equal, round and reactive to light. He has bilateral red reflexes. Conjunctive are normal. Bilateral tympanic membranes are clear.
      3. **Skin:** Mottled and cool.
      4. **Cardiovascular:** Significantly tachycardic, regular rhythm. Systolic murmur can be heard at left upper and lower sternal borders. Murmur can also be heard from the back if patient is rolled. Palpation of chest wall reveals precordial heave. Capillary refill greater than 8 seconds. Absent femoral pulses.
      5. **Respiratory**: Breath sounds are equal but decreased bilaterally. Patient has tachypnea and grunting. He has significant retractions.
      6. **Abdominal:** Soft. There is hepatomegaly. Bowel sounds are decreased.
      7. **Genitourinary:** Normal circumcised male genitalia.
      8. **Extremities:** Cool to the touch. Mottled with capillary refill greater than 8 seconds. Absent femoral pulses.
      9. **Neurological:** Patient is crying but otherwise quite still. He has a palmar grasp that is symmetric.
   5. **Physiology (data points should appear when patient is placed on the appropriate monitoring devices)**
      1. Heart Rate 215, sinus
      2. Blood Pressure Upper extremity 111/83 Lower extremity: Initially unobtainable
      3. Respiratory Rate 96
      4. Pulse Oximetry Unable to be obtained
5. **Scenario Branch points**
   1. Changes in patient condition: Peripheral IV access should not be obtainable in this patient. Intra-osseous or umbilical venous access must be obtained. The patient’s tachycardia may improve slightly with fluid boluses as they are given. It would be reasonable to lower the heart rate approximately 10 beats per 20 ml/kg bolus. The rate will remain tachycardic, however, throughout the case. Oxygen administration at 100% should result in saturations in the 50-60 range, but should not resolve the patient’s overall poor perfusion. Prostaglandin therapy must be initiated for improvement in color and perfusion. Once the prostaglandins are initiated the patient’s perfusion should improve and femoral pulses should become faint but palpable. Prostaglandin therapy should cause apnea prompting intubation if there is not already a definitive airway for respiratory considerations.
   2. Request for old records: None available although mother may clarify full term, low risk repeat Cesarean section.
   3. Differential Diagnosis: The physician managing this case may consider differential of shock in a neonate, including sepsis, hypovolemia, malrotation with midgut volvulus and even non-accidental trauma. It is reasonable that broad spectrum antibiotics would be given to this patient. It is reasonable that a “babygram” or a portable abdominal x-ray would be taken in addition the chest x-ray.
   4. Intra-osseous/Umbilical fluid administration: It should not be possible to obtain peripheral intravenous access in the patients. If after 2-3 tries the primary physician does not move on to another route, prompting by an RN role player or the faculty facilitator could be considered. A fluid bolus is indicated by the physical exam showing severe tachycardia and mottling with poor capillary refill suggestive of poor perfusion. The patient may transiently improve with 20cc/kg normal saline with an improvement in the heart rate by approximately 10 beats per minute per bolus. Even though congenital heart disease is the source of this patient’s shock fluids should be administered. The patient, by history, has not fed all night, nor made any wet diapers. This coupled with the vital signs should prompt fluid resuscitation.
   5. Other medical interventions: Based on the patient’s presentation, some participants may choose to intubate the patient initially. It is not mandatory that the patient be intubated until the prostaglandins are administered. If the learner does not recognize the apnea after prostaglandin patient the patient should arrest. Antibiotics may be administered given sepsis as a consideration in this patient. Blood cultures may be obtained at the time of access as they would not delay the care. Lumbar puncture should not be performed on this patient. Foley may be placed in this patient once stabilized.
   6. Laboratory studies: Laboratory studies can be found in Appendix A.
   7. Administration of medication: This patient should receive Prostaglandin E1 therapy. It should be administered at a rate of 0.05-0.1 mcg/kg/min. Antimicrobial coverage for sepsis may be given to this patient. Appropriate choices would include ampicillin and gentamycin or ampicillin and cefotaxime. Acyclovir would also be indicated for this patient.
   8. Consultation of specialist: A cardiologist will be available by telephone consult, ideally after cardiomegaly and failed response to oxygen administration is recognized. Pharmacy or a pediatric emergency medicine physician at a local children’s hospital should be available by phone if the provider wishes to consult someone regarding prostaglandin dosing. A pediatric emergency department or pediatric intensivist should be available to accept this patient in transfer once they are on prostaglandin and intubated. No consultant in this scenario should be obstructive.
   9. Non-recommended actions: The participant should not admit to a hospital without pediatric intensive care and cardiac services. Lumbar puncture should not be performed on this patient.

**VII. Instructor Notes:**

1. **Scenario flow** – Instructors can directly influence the flow of the scenario by providing the initial patient history.
   1. Key aspect of case – Identification of critical congenital heart disease is essential. Fluid resuscitation and the initiation of prostaglandin therapy must be performed for patient survival.
   2. Specialist consultation – Part of simulation at our institutions includes the opportunity to safely flounder or even “fail” at taking care of a patient. Patients may code and die due to mismanagement or delay in order to facilitate a learning opportunity in a safe environment. For that reason, we may elect for consultants to be less helpful or less willing to lead management over the phone than said consultants would be in real life. This lack of assistance can push learners to think as solo practitioners. However, for less advanced learners or situations in which the instructor does not wish to utilize this strategy, consultants should be given the latitude to guide, lead or direct management of the patient at the discretion of the faculty leading the simulation exercise.
2. **Information for actors** – This case can easily be presented without formal “actors” and by using other participants to serve as the nursing staff or other collaborating physicians. Any specific actors used outside of participants should be briefed about the critical actions and anticipated flow of the case ahead of time.
3. **Scenario programming** – The settings for a high-fidelity patient simulator are fairly straightforward for this scenario and do not require specific programming. Our program typically presents this scenario with the initial vital signs as presented above, with the major branch points of fluid resuscitation, prostaglandin administration and recognition of need for definitive airway management. Our simulator is also prepared with a code scenario if any key stop is not performed correctly or in a timely fashion.

**VII. Debriefing Plan:**

1. **Method of debriefing** – A post-case debriefing conference is recommended immediately following the end of the scenario. Consider including some of the following elements:
   1. Open-ended questions by facilitator – Consider beginning the session with a question to the primary participant about how they felt the scenario went. The question may be as simple as “What do you feel went well and what was challenging for you in this case?” This often leads to extensive participant-led discussion that will touch on many of the major issues in the case. Invite any secondary participants and/or observers to comment constructively about the case.
   2. Brief didactic review – Some possible materials for review after the scenario have been provided in Appendix B. They address several major teaching points related to the case; however, individual participants may raise additional questions.
   3. Formal participant evaluation – Our group does not use this case for formal evaluations and we have no standardized form or format for such feedback. However, this case can easily be adapted to the standardized evaluation method used at your institution using the objectives in Section III (above).
2. **Actual debriefing materials** – See Appendix B for debriefing materials.
3. **Rules for the debriefing** – Often an open-ended question to the participants will provoke a discussion of aspects of the case management that were good, and those areas still in need of improvement. Encourage your participants to discuss the case management decisions in a constructive fashion. When this case is used for experiential learning (without formal participant evaluation) discussions may vary as compared to situations where formal feedback or grading is anticipated. We have found that placing an emphasis on the learning that occurs from experiencing a case like this is very effective, rather than focusing on the “right answer” or whether the specific management decisions were precisely as the leader would have performed. Also, we have found participants are more willing to discuss challenges or perceived “failures” in management if they are not being graded.
4. **Questions to facilitate the debriefing –**
   1. What is the differential for shock in a neonate?
   2. What is the approach to resuscitation of this patient?
   3. What are the indications for prostaglandin therapy in a neonate?
5. **Pilot Testing and Lessons Learned:**

**A:** **Number of Participants-** This scenario has been tested for small groups of medical students, Emergency Medicine residents, Pediatric residents and Pediatric Emergency Medicine Fellows. They have worked in groups of 2-6.

**B:** **Performance Expectations-** All learners are able to correctly identify shock in this patient, and most recognize this as critical congenital heart disease at some point during the case. Residents and fellows have been able to begin resuscitation and recognize the need for prostaglandins. Most struggle with prostaglandin dosing but resources to consider for this struggle are able to be discussed in the debriefing. Some students recognize this as congenital heart disease earlier in the case and become quite timid about fluid resuscitation. A discussion of the balance between judicious usage of fluid in more stable congenital heart disease versus more aggressive resuscitation in a child demonstrating this degree of shock is also a valuable debriefing discussion point.

**C: Anticipated Management Mistakes-**

* + 1. Failure to obtain IV access promptly. Some physician participants will spend an excessive amount of time attempting to obtain a peripheral IV. A formal nurse actor can be prompted to suggest another method of access after a pre-determined number of attempts. If there are no formal actors and other physicians are playing the roles of nurses or techs, this suggestion generally happens organically.
    2. Rarely students will decide to pursue a full septic work up or non-accidental trauma workup in this patient. Attempts to change the patient’s position for lumbar puncture or sending the patient to radiology away from the practitioner should prompt cardiovascular collapse.
