**Additional Materials #5**

**Interview with SP**

The interview between students and the SP takes place in a separate room from the initial classroom setting. The SP should not volunteer any information but should only respond to specific questions raised by the students.

|  |  |
| --- | --- |
| **Questions Students May Ask &**  **Context for Case** | **Information Provided by Patient** |
| In attempt to determine a dose for warfarin or in talking with the patient about the bruising, students may ask the patient questions about **warfarin**.  The patient does not know if the warfarin dose was increased or decreased because the patient does not know what dose s/he was taking before the 19th and the patient does not know which direction the bleeding time was “off” (e.g. too high or too low) when her dose was changed.  Students may discuss the issue of generic drugs and bioavailability.  Students will need information from the “pharmacy” to determine which dosage was dispensed. The “nurse” does not know the current dose because the Coumadin Clinic is not very good at communicating changes to the primary care provider’s office. | Patient may say, “I go to the Coumadin Clinic every month. Earlier this week, yes, it was Tuesday the 19th, after they saw the results of my blood work; they called me and told me that my bleeding time was off. They wanted me to go to the pharmacy and pick up a new prescription and take 2 tablets on TThSS, and one tablet on MWF. I don’t know the dosage…and the color and shape of the tablets are always different each time I get the prescription filled because of all the different generic warfarin products.” |
| Students may ask the patient questions about the **eye doctor** since the patient mentioned it previously.  Students will need to determine from calling the “pharmacy” which medication has been filled; the “nurse” has no record of it. This, again, illustrates poor communication between some physician specialists and primary care providers. | “Yes, I use some eye drops that were prescribed by Dr. Pupil for my left eye.” |
| Students may ask questions about the patient’s use of **insulin** (e.g. brand, dose).  Students will confirm from the “pharmacy” that the medication is Lantus (as indicated on the pharmacy label) and will be able to determine from the “nurse” what the patient’s dose is. | The patient does not know the name of the insulin, but relays, “I only use it once a day – at bedtime.”  “I don’t know what my dose is. My daughter-in-law usually marks the needles with a permanent marker to show me how far I am supposed to fill my syringe. You know, it is difficult to see the lines to measure the right dose.” |
| Students may ask about the drug samples (**Vytorin 10/40** and **Lovaza**) that were in the patient’s bag. | “I am certain that my daughter-in-law has been making sure that I takes each of them just like the doctor prescribed, but I don’t know how many times I take these tablets each day since they are usually already in my pill box.” |
| Students may ask the patient if she takes any medications besides **calcium** for the “weak bones” that she mentioned.  This should get the students thinking that the patient may take either alendronate or risedronate. | Patient indicates, “Yes, I do take a pill just once a week, and I think it is for my bones, but I don’t know the name of it.” |
| Students may ask the patient about the **aspirin** that was listed on the partial medication list. | The patient indicates, “My doctor did say something about a baby aspirin. I think that may be a pill that my daughter-in-law has me take in the morning with breakfast.” |
| Students may ask about: **metoprolol** or **levothyroxine** (for which the patient had old prescription vials) or about **simvastatin** or the **water pill** (listed on the partial medication list), but the patient is unable to provide any more definitive information about these medications. | If queried about these medications, the patient simply indicates, “I don’t know how I take them, but I am sure that my daughter-in-law makes sure that I am getting exactly what my doctor ordered.” |

We have set a 4 minute limit of time for students to interview the patient. At the end of 4 minutes, the patient has a timer go off, at which time the patient indicates: “My cell phone is ringing; it’s my granddaughter; I need to take this call,” and the patient abruptly dismisses the students and tells them they need to leave the room.
