**Additional Materials #4**

**Doctor’s Office**

When students initially place the call to the “doctor’s office”, an administrative assistant initially answers the phone, “Hello, Main Street Family Practice. How may I help you?” Once the students state the reason for their call, the assistant says, “Just a minute, while I transfer you back to the nurse.”

The “nurse” should not volunteer any information but should only share information that is specifically requested by the students. The left column indicates the medication questions that students may ask and describes how this information fits into the overall case. The information that the nurse is able to provide is listed in the right column.

One tablet daily. This practice first prescribed it July 6th, 2011 and there were 12 refills added.  No additional information regarding the reason for the prescription is available. |

| Students may seek clarification on **metoprolol**. The dialog with the nurse should indicate the confusion that may result when a medication list in an electronic health record has not been “cleaned up”. The topic of drug shortages may also be discussed by students.  Thus, this information helps the students recognize that the patient is getting 50 mg tablets but cuts them in half. Therefore, the current dose is metoprolol tartrate 25 mg twice daily. Since #60 tablets are dispensed each time, a single refill lasts her 2 months. Thus, while the conversation with the pharmacist suggested non-adherence, this is not the case.  The lack of diagnosis and the fact that the drug was “just continued” after being started by another healthcare facility should prompt the students to wonder if the medication is necessary, if the patient should be followed by another healthcare provider, or if other medications may have been omitted (e.g. and ACE inhibitor in heart failure). | The nurse will say, “Let’s see….we have a number of different things for metoprolol. Let’s see what seems to be the most current.  We had a Toprol XL 100 mg. 1 tab daily. First prescribed by our doctors last October with 12 refills.  It looks like the patient complained of dizziness and fatigue. Bradycardia is also listed here…  I see a Toprol XL 50 mg 1 tab daily, prescribed last November 6, 2010, also with 12 refills…Looks like we got a call from the pharmacy that they were unable to obtain this drug…..  Then, I see a metoprolol tartrate 25 mg twice daily prescribed December 6, 2010….  There is also a Metoprolol 50 mg. Take ½ a tablet twice daily that was first prescribed in January of 2011. We also called #60 tablets of this into the patient’s pharmacy again on July 6, 2011 with 3 refills.”  If asked about diagnosis, the nurse should indicate that she is “Unsure. It appears that the drug was initially started after an ER visit or a hospitalization and our office was just continuing it.” |
| Students may seek clarification about the patient’s **warfarin**, given the old prescription vial that was in the patient’s purse and the lack of dose information on the current medication list.  This dialog indicates that there is sometimes a lack of communication among healthcare providers. | The nurse indicates: “We have a number of different things listed for her. The Coumadin Clinic is always changing her dose, but we often are not made aware of the changes. I would rely on whatever the pharmacy tells you they dispensed most recently since we are not always kept in the loop.” |

The lack of specific information about which calcium product (with vitamin D or without; which calcium salt; what dose) should prompt some concern among the students, especially if they recognize that the doctor has also prescribed omeprazole for the patient (see below) (since the calcium carbonate salt that the patient is using is only absorbed in an acidic environment). | Nurse can convey, “It looks like the doctor told simply told the patient to take 1 calcium tablet with each meal. | Students may ask: Is there **anything else** on record that the patient is taking? At the end of the 4 minute time limit, the nurse indicates that s/he needs to go and hangs up the phone to disconnect the conversation.
