**Title: Medication Reconciliation Simulation Case**

**Instructor’s Guide**

**Part A: Standardized Patient Case Materials**

1. **Purpose of Materials**: This simulation case activity is used as a teaching tool to demonstrate complexities involved with Medication Reconciliation. The case involves a patient who is trying to figure out which medications she is supposed to be taking. The scenario is set in the context of a patient whose family member puts all her medications in a weekly pillbox, but the patient has dropped the pillbox on the floor and the family member is currently out of town.
2. **Description of the Development Process**: Development of this case was based upon a “Reconciliation through Simulation Patient Safety Initiative” classroom learning activity originally implemented at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine by Lindquist et al. (2008). Dr. Karpa, PhD, RPh, modified the case for use in a Penn State University College of Medicine longitudinal elective and developed specific scripts for the instructor, standardized patient (SP), “pharmacist”, and “nurse” at the physician’s office. Dr. Karpa is an associate professor in the department of pharmacology where she serves as director of medical pharmacology instruction; she is a pharmacology educator and maintains an active license to practice pharmacy.
3. **Information Pertaining to How the Materials Have Been Used**: This case has been used as a teaching tool to set the stage for additional lectures, assignments, and workshops on topics of medication reconciliation, medical safety, and appropriate medication utilization in a longitudinal curriculum for 3rd and 4th year medical students.
4. **Methods Used for Preparation**: This case requires recruitment of a skilled standardized patient (SP) who is able to convey the appropriate emotions and dialogue of a patient that is having medical problems and is concerned that she is not taking her prescribed medications appropriately (as directed by the case). Prior preparation involves (a) sending a copy of the case and script to the SP in advance of (b) a 1 hour meeting attended by the faculty instructor, the SP, the case director, and other participants (e.g. the “pharmacist”, “nurse”, and administrative assistant) to review learning objectives and clarify questions.

The most important aspect for the SP to understand during the training session is which information *must* be provided to the students so that learners have the necessary information to complete the case versus the information that the SP can *ad lib*.

Limited training is needed for other participants (“pharmacist” or “nurse”) since they are effectively “out-of-sight” and can respond to students simply by reading a written script. The most important information for the “pharmacist” or “nurse” to comprehend is that they should only provide information to students in response to students’ queries about *specific* medications (e.g., “The patient’s list of medications indicates that she takes a water pill. Could you tell me which water pill she is using?”). In contrast, these “healthcare providers” should not simply read all the medications on their scripts if asked to do so by students (e.g., “Can you tell me which medications are on the patient’s medication list?). In this latter case, the “pharmacist” and “nurse” must ask the students for clarification (e.g., “Specifically, which medication did you want to know about?”)

1. **Data to Support the Content of the Materials**

Poor communication of medical information at transitional points of care is responsible for 50% of medication errors. Indeed, Bergkvist et al. (2009) found that information is transferred erroneously at transitional points in care with every fifth medication. Not surprisingly, the number of medication errors increases with the number of drugs a person is taking (Bergkvist et al., 2009). The likelihood of an adverse event from drug errors also increases when patients have a poor understanding of preadmission medications, take one or more high-risk medications, experience a number of changes to their medication regimen, or when the patient’s medication history is taken by an intern (Pippins et al., 2008). The most common drug errors involve medication omissions and incomplete information (wrong dose, route, or frequency) (Wong et al., 2008; Pippins et al., 2008).

It is estimated that 60% of adverse drug events could be prevented or ameliorated (Forster et al., 2003; Walker et al., 2009) through (a) medication reconciliation, (b) clinical judgment to critically evaluate and verify that medications are necessary and are helping rather than causing adverse events, and (c) patient follow-up counseling and education. Indeed, when combined, these three things have been shown to reduce actual and potential adverse drug events, reduce emergency department visits, decrease hospital readmission rates, reduce overall healthcare costs following hospitalizations, and boost patient medication knowledge and adherence (Koehler et al., 2009; Jack et al., 2009; Forster et al., 2005; Halasyamani et al., 2006; Crotty et al., 2004; Schnipper et al., 2006; Dudas et al., 2001; Coleman et al. 2005; Lessard et al., 2006; Karnon et al., 2009; Nester et al., 2002; Gleason et al., 2004; Lessard et al., 2006; Karapinar-Carkit et al., 2009; Viktil et al., 2006; Kaboli et al., 2006).

To improve patient outcomes, medication reconciliation has been a priority with the Institute of Medicine, Institute for Healthcare Improvement, and Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations (Joint Commission on Accreditation of Healthcare Organizations, 2006). However, the extent to which medication reconciliation is performed in primary care settings, when a patient has responsibility for accurate coordination of his/her own medications, is unknown. It is estimated that in primary care, a patient’s medical records accurately reflect the medications that s/he is taking as little as 5% of the time (Bayoumi et al., 2009). Evidence supports the practice of medication reconciliation in the outpatient setting as a means for decreasing medication errors, improving patient outcomes, and concomitantly saving healthcare dollars (Bergkvist et al., 2009; Nester and Hale, 2002).

As important as medication reconciliation is, identifying and resolving medication discrepancies in a patient’s medical chart is only part of the answer to effective medication utilization. Patients may be taking drugs that are causing them to experience side effects, they may not be receiving appropriate therapeutic monitoring of their medications, or they may be mixing medications that result in harmful drug interactions. Using clinical judgment to verify that medications are helpful and not harmful to patients, as well as patient education/counseling about their medications are also important components for successful medication outcomes (Bergkvist et al., 2009; Nester and Hale, 2002; Gleason et al., 2004). Patients provide important information that impacts effective drug use which cannot be obtained from medical charts and prescription records. Lack of proper education and instruction about medications impedes patient adherence with their prescribed drug regimens.

At our medical school, preclinical medical students are taught basic pharmacology (mechanisms of drug action and adverse effects) in silo (learning about one therapeutic drug class at a time). When students reach their clinical years, they are faced with the challenges inherent to the clinical aspects of pharmacotherapy in which patients are often taking multiple drugs for many different diseases, are usually diagnosed with several comorbid conditions, often see multiple physicians, may use different pharmacies, and frequently experience drug side effects or drug interactions. At our institution, medical students were not previously taught the importance of medication reconciliation or provided with a formal structure for critically evaluating the use of each medication that patients take. We are under the impression that pharmacology education is similar at other institutions. Indeed, the need for medical students to receive additional training in the clinical aspects of pharmacology does not appear to be restricted to our medical school. The American Association of Medial Colleges (AAMC) devoted a white paper (Report X; 2008) to the topic of “effective prescribing practices” and encouraged medical schools to add formal clinical pharmacology training into the clinical years of medical school. We embraced the AAMC recommendation by implementing learning activities that focus on medication reconciliation, medication safety, and appropriate medication utilization (correct dosages for renal/hepatic insufficiency, matching drugs with a diagnosis, following recommended treatment guidelines, discontinuing unnecessary/harmful medications, etc.) in a longitudinal elective that is taken by 3rd and 4th year medical students.

1. **Data to Support the Reliability of Checklists or Rating Scales:** Neither inter-rater agreement nor internal consistency is applicable in this situation since we are using this case as a teaching tool rather than an assessment tool. This simulation case is applicable to all medical schools that wish to teach medical students about medical reconciliation or medication safety topics. The materials (SP, props) are readily modified for use at other medical schools.
2. **Data to Support Accuracy of Case Portrayal:** We wish to convey a situation where an elderly patient is unsure which medications she is taking and is experiencing adverse effects because of problems with the medications (weakness, muscle pain, bruising). To portray this as accurately as possible, the patient enters the classroom with a cane (due to muscle pain/weakness) and has moulage applied to her arms to simulate bruising/ecchymoses).

To identify which medications a patient is taking in real life, resources might include: the patient, a pharmacy, and a doctor’s office. To accurately simulate this, the patient gives students business cards that contain the “pharmacy” and “physician” telephone numbers. Thus, the students gather information via an interview with the patient, a call to the patient’s “pharmacy”, and by telephoning the patient’s “doctor’s office” to piece together an accurate medication list for the patient. To most accurately simulate calls placed to these types of healthcare facilities, an administrative assistant answers the phone at both the “pharmacy” and the “doctor’s office” simulating a pharmacy technician or office staff member, respectively, prior to re-directing the call to the “pharmacist” or the “nurse”.

Students were asked to complete a Likert-type evaluation of the simulation experience (1= Awful, 2 = Mediocre, 3= Adequate, 4=Very good, 5=Excellent). Overall (mean ± standard deviation), students rated the realism of the session as a 4.0 ± 0.43.

1. **General Suggestions for Using the Materials** & **Lessons Learned**
2. General Suggestions: The following describes how this session should unfold.

1. A small group of 3rd and 4th year medical students (approximately 10 to 15) gathers for a lecture.

2. As the faculty instructor begins the first slide, an SP “bursts” into the room indicating that she needs help figuring out what medications she is taking and she thinks that her medications are causing her to feel bad.

3. The faculty instructor and the SP initially have some dialog (File: Additional Materials #1) about the SP’s medications and medical conditions (e.g. where she gets her medications, what types of medications/medical conditions that she has, how we can get in touch with her doctor’s office and pharmacy, etc.). It is necessary to have the faculty instructor do this initial “interview” with the patient to provide students with the baseline information they will need to complete the objectives of the session. For example it is via this dialogue between instructor and the SP that students are provided with: phone numbers for doctor and pharmacy; a partial medication list from the patient; prescription vials and drug samples that are in the patient’s purse; information about the types of medical conditions the patient has; other physicians that the patient sees, etc.

4. The instructor turns to the students and asks, “Do you think that we can help this patient figure out which medications and what dosages she is supposed to be taking?” Once students have indicated willingness to help, the instructor divides the students into small groups (of 3 to 5 students) and gives students instructions (File: Additional Materials #2).

5. Students in each group telephone the “nurse” and the “pharmacist” and interview the patient in a separate room. We have set a time limit for these interactions. The students may speak to these individuals in any order; it is entirely up to the students’ discretion who they choose to call (“nurse” or “pharmacist”) or visit (SP) first. Therefore the scripts for the SP (File: Additional Materials #5), the “pharmacist” (File: Additional Materials #3), and the “nurse” (File: Additional Materials #4) may be used in any order. (Each of these scripts provides essential information about the drugs that the patient takes. This information enables students to piece the puzzle together and construct a complete list of the patient’s medications and dosages.)

6. Based upon the questions that the students have asked the “nurse”, “pharmacist” and SP and the information that students accumulate from these encounters, students work to construct an accurate list of medications (and dosages) that the SP is taking. In doing this, student recognize that the medications are the source of the SP’s side effects (bruising) and chief complaint (muscle aches/weakness). Students also recognize that it is possible that treatment guidelines have not been followed appropriately in the SP’s care. Furthermore, students discover how poor communication between healthcare providers can impact patient care, and they also become aware of problems that arise when patients have not been educated/engaged about the medications that they have been prescribed. These are among the topics that are discussed in the Debriefing Guide (File: Additional Materials #6).

B. Lessons learned:

1. A sufficient amount of time must be allotted for students to interact with the “nurse”, “pharmacist”, and SP. Initially, we allowed only two minutes, but this was not enough time; we have increased the time to 4 minutes. Students should be warned in advance that they will have a time limit.

1. At the first session, we had included a drug sample of Razadyne in the patient’s purse. This medication (galantamine) is used to treat Alzheimer’s dementia. When students encountered this medication, they did not treat information provided by the SP as “reliable” (they assumed the patient was suffering from dementia and was unable to supply useful information). Therefore, we removed this medication from subsequent sessions.
2. One interesting observation we have made with this case pertains to students’ willingness to ask the SP about the obvious bruises on her arms. Students have indicated during the debriefing that they saw the bruises, but few students have asked the SP about the injuries. Students indicated that they did not ask because the bruises looked so authentic that they were unsure if this was a component of the simulation or real bruises that the individual portraying the SP had on her arms. We have indicated to the SP that she can make reference to the bruises, if not asked about them, to confirm students’ suspicion that they are case-related, warfarin-induced problems.
3. It is not possible to prepare the SP for every medication question that students may ask. In our case, the SP (a paid actor) benefits from an ear bud through which a case director can supply answers to specific medication-related questions that the students may ask. We have found that a nurse educator (or other healthcare provider with knowledge of medications) is ideal to serve in the role of a case director. However, having a case director would not be necessary if the SP were a healthcare provider.

**Part B. Identifying Case Factors**

1. **Presenting complaint**: Patient enters and “interrupts” a classroom session that is just getting started (students are expecting to get a *lecture* about medication safety). The patient hobbles slightly with assistance of a cane. The patient interrupts the lecturer and asks for help: “Can you help me? I need to know what medications I am taking and if they are causing me to have all these problems. I feel achy all over and my muscles hurt.” The patient does not initially state anything about bruising, but the patient’s arms have had moulage applied to resemble ecchymoses. If questioned by the students, the patient acknowledges, “I have black and blue marks all over my body”. (During the subsequent interview between students and the patient, if it SP sensed that the students had not made a connection between the bruises on her arm and the warfarin, the patient would prompt the students by alluding to the bruises.)
2. **Length of patient encounter**: The entire session has been allotted 60-75 minutes.
3. **Learning objectives**: At the end of this session, students will be able to:
4. Gather a medication history for a patient using available resources (pharmacy, doctor’s office, patient) and synthesize an accurate medication list for a patient.
5. Identify a patient’s chief complaint in the context of medication-related adverse effects.

1. Discuss patient-specific and system factors that impact accurate medication lists.
2. Provide patient education for each drug on the medication list to empower patient’s involvement in care.
3. **Diagnosis**: Using various pieces of information supplied by the patient, the “pharmacy” and the “doctor’s office”, students identify the medications that the patient is taking and discover therapeutic duplication and drug interactions resulting in muscle aches (e.g. simvastatin) and ecchymoses due to warfarin interactions.
4. **Target Group**: medical students
5. **Student level**: 3rd and 4th year
6. **Type of Case**: Teaching
7. **Case Summary/SP Training Notes**
8. **SP Demographics**: The standardized patient is an elderly individual.
9. **Setting**: The SP knows that s/he is in an academic medical center and is seeking help identifying which medications s/he takes. The SP wishes to know whether any of the medications are contributing to his/her current problems. Students are told that the date is July 21, 2011; given that the scenario is set in the summer, the patient is wearing short sleeves (to expose bruising associated with warfarin). (By keeping the date constant, the dates on the props, e.g. the prescription vials, the pharmacy records, and the “doctor’s office charts, do not need to be changed each time we run the case.)
10. **History of present complaint**: one or two weeks
11. **Past medical history**: Patient knows that s/he takes medications for heart, blood sugar, and thyroid. Patient states that s/he has brittle bones and sees an eye doctor.
12. **Family medical history**: The patient’s daughter-in-law normally assists the patient with medications by putting them in a pillbox, but the patient dropped the pillbox (spilling the pills) and the daughter-in-law is currently out of town.
13. **Social history**: Nothing applicable
14. **Management plan**: During the debriefing session, the students are asked to identify the medications the SP is using and describe any medication changes that should be made immediately. In addition, students are asked to provide the patient with information about each medication (how/when to take, what each medication is used to treat, common side effects and how to manage, etc.) to empower the patient to be more involved in her own medical condition.
15. **Patient presentation**: The patient presents using a cane, complaining of muscle pain and weakness. Regardless of when the session is run, the students are told that the date is July 21, 2011. Since the setting is summer, the patient is wearing short sleeves and has “bruises” on his/her arms.
16. **Special case considerations**:
17. **Classroom:** Tables and chairs in the classroom have deliberately been set up to separate the students into small groups, such that each group will have approximately the same number (3 to 5) of students. Smaller groups ensure active participation of all students.
18. **Makeup**: Moulage is applied to the patient’s arms to simulate ecchymoses. Briefly, 30 minutes prior to the session, bruising is applied sporadically to both hands and the volar aspect of the forearms including the anticubital fossa. To accomplish this:

1. Use a wet stippled sponge and tap a small amount of maroon makeup onto the sponge. Indiscriminately, tap small areas of the dorsal side of the hands with the sponge.

b. With a wetted finger, blend the makeup to produce the desired bruise effect.

c. This process is repeated on the inside of the forearms.

d. For a more aged presentation of bruising, a small amount of yellow makeup is applied around the peripheral edges of the bruise.
