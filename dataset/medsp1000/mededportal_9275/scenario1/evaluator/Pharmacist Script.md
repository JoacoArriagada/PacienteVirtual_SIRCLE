**Additional Materials #3**

**Information Provided by the Pharmacist**

When students initially place the call to the “pharmacy”, an assistant answers the phone “City Drug Store, how can I help you?” Once students state the reason for the call, the assistant responds, “I need to put you on hold and get the pharmacist; just a minute.”

The “pharmacist” should not volunteer any information but should only share information that is specifically requested by the students. The left column indicates the medication questions that students may ask and describes how this information fits into the overall case. The information that the pharmacist is able to provide, if asked, is listed in the right column.

| Students may ask if the patient is taking a **water pill**, based upon the partial medication list that the patient provided.  If students ask the indication for which the water pill has been prescribed, the pharmacist will not know. This raises the issue that community pharmacists typically do not have access to patients’ diagnoses. This patient may be taking furosemide appropriately for fluid retention, heart failure, or hypertension with concomitant poor renal function. On the other hand, furosemide would not be the diuretic of choice if the patient was prescribed the drug for hypertension in the absence of renal problems. | Furosemide 20 mg, 1 tablet daily. Last filled July 6, 2011. No diagnosis provided. |
| Students may ask if the pharmacist is filling **simvastatin** for the patient based upon the partial medication list that the patient initially provided. Students should begin to recognize that this may be the source (or a contributing factor) to the patient’s muscle aches and pains. | Simvastatin 20 mg, 1 tablet daily. Last filled July 6, 2011. |
| Students may ask if the patient is taking **thyroid replacement** therapy based upon the partial medication list that the patient provided. | Students will ask the pharmacist about the **metoprolol** dose. Metoprolol was listed on the patient’s medication list as simply “2x a day”.  This dialogue with the pharmacist raises several important issues. First, students may discuss the problems associated with drug shortages. Second, it is important for students to know the inter-conversion between the XL (succinate) form and the twice daily form of metoprolol (tartrate). Third, the question should be raised in the minds of students as to whether this patient is non-adherent with this medication (note, this is not the case, as the students will determine from their conversation with the doctor’s office; the patient has been instructed to split the tablets and only takes ½ tablet twice daily). Again, the pharmacist does not know the reason for this medication being prescribed. It could be to manage tachycardia, atrial arrhythmias, heart failure (in which case the XL form would be most appropriate but if the diagnosis is heart failure, the patient should also be taking an angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor or an angiotensin receptor blocker). The students will not have all the information to make a judgment as to the appropriateness of the medication; nonetheless, these are discussion points that will likely be raised by students during the activity or the debriefing. | The pharmacist will say, “Hmmmm. We have a number of different metoprolol doses on file for her. Let me scroll through and see what we can find.” The pharmacist will read aloud as s/he scans the computer records:  “It looks like Toprol XL 100 mg, 1 tablet daily, was initially filled last October 6, 2010. Then, it was changed to Toprol XL 50 mg on November 6, 2010. Then, we have metoprolol tartrate 25 mg twice daily which was filled December 6, 2010. This raises the issue that pharmacists only have as much information as is provided by the prescribing physician. | Lantus, 10 ml vial, Use as directed. Last filled on July 6, 2011. |

The bottle that the patient was carrying indicated 1 mg tablets, but the date is old (October 6, 2010). Warfarin doses change frequently. Given the patient’s obvious bruising, it will be important to identify the strength of the tablets the patient is currently taking.  Again, the issue of the pharmacist lacking full information in terms of how the patient should be taking the medication is raised with this medication. | The pharmacist notes that Warfarin 2 mg tablets were dispensed earlier in the week, per a phone call from Dr. Platelet at the coagulation clinic.
