# 6. Care of the actively dying patient (dyspnea); 1 student; 10 minutes

B6/672: DNR/DNI. Mrs. Arlene Simpson is a 90 year old woman with end stage COPD and metastatic lung cancer admitted for influenza which has progressed to ARDS. Family and patient decided on comfort care; she will likely die in coming hours-days.

PAGE: Please call RN re: patient Simpson; much more dyspneic – would like PRN lorazepam IV as appears anxious.

# Notes for nurse:

There is another pt with last name Simpson (Alex Simpson with AML) – they should not mix them up.   
Respiratory effort appears significant and this has increased over the last hour   
You don’t have any PRN opioids or benzodiazepines available to you   
Be persistent that you think she would benefit most from a benzodiazepine, but if given reasons for opioid, accept this as plan of care   
When opioids are discussed to be given say “I don’t know if I am comfortable with that…isn’t that like…euthanasia?”   
You tried putting her on oxygen but she is pulling it off   
Family is at the bedside; believes she looks uncomfortable; she has been agitated and confused

Renal function is normal: last Cr 0.8

# Desired learner actions:

Does not mix up two patients with same last name.   
Recognize the focus should be on alleviating symptoms; not trying to figure out why she is suddenly more dyspneic – avoid interventions such as nebulizers, oxygen like face-mask or NIPPV that would likely add to her discomfort/agitation

Verbal order for morphine (or other opioid) at low dose o Morphine IV (in this situation $2 { \cdot } 4 \mathsf { m } \mathsf { g } ,$ but OK if they start lower as long as they plan to reassess soon) Q15-30 min PRN pain or dyspnea o Hydromorphone 0.2 – 0.4 mg Q15-30 min PRN pain or dyspnea

Clear, closed-loop communication when relaying orders to nursing staff

Provide education to nurse about opioids are effective for dyspnea, benzodiazepines are not – if anxiety persists after dyspnea controlled then can start low-dose benzodiazepine Benzodiazepines can cause paradoxical agitation in elderly patients, if need start LOW Develop follow-up plan; reassess discomfort 15-20 min after morphine given, preferably at the patient’s bedside, recognizing you may need to escalate dose

# Teaching points:

Opioids, preferably morphine if no contraindications exist (severe renal dysfunction) can be used to relieve refractory dyspnea   
Fans can also be used to help relieve sensation of dyspnea   
Lorazepam and other benzodiazepines should be avoided for the treatment of dyspnea; use if dyspnea adequately managed and patient has anxiety and no contraindications.   
Nurses often worry about giving “the last dose” – provide education and be supportive
