**Purpose/goal of the resource including educational objectives:**

The purpose of this resource is to provide Emergency Medicine Residents with an opportunity to practice triaging and caring for patients in a mass casualty situation. There are not many current opportunities for practicing these skills in our training program, nor are there many published resources on multiple patient simulations such as mass casualty scenarios. The primary objectives of this resource all focus on triaging and prioritizing patients and managing the overall scene. All individual patient management aspects are secondary objectives, as these were not the primary focus of this simulation exercise.

The educational objectives of this mass casualty scenario are:

1. Primary
2. Manage and prioritize multiple casualties from a bomb / blast incident concomitantly
3. Organize team and individual roles in order to care for all casualties effectively
4. Prioritize which patients need to go to the operating room expediently
5. Be aware of scene safety
6. Secondary
7. Manage patients in hemorrhagic shock; volume resuscitation with crystalloid and Packed RBCs (PRBC)
8. Manage amputations - demonstrate correct technique when applying tourniquet
9. Manage exsanguinating junctional wounds – demonstrate correct technique when packing a junctional wound with purposes of hemorrhage control
10. Manage pneumothoraces
11. Manage severe traumatic brain injury patients requiring intubation
12. Manage pregnant patients with severe blunt abdominal trauma
13. Manage children with blunt trauma
14. Manage patients with penetrating neck wounds and airway compromise
15. Effective and expedient use of ultrasound (US) in trauma

**Conceptual background (why and how it was created):**

This scenario was designed to provide residents with the experience of being in a disaster setting while still in a controlled environment. The definition of a disaster is when the demands outweigh the resources available. During an incident like this, providers are able to practice skills that they do not frequently use. This is a high acuity, low frequency event, for which simulation can provide valuable training. Effective training for these situations can not be effectively gained through reading or classroom lecture as reviews of the literature reveal that many problems in planning often recur with each new disaster1.

This has been part of a large collaborative effort between physicians, residents, nurses, paramedics, simulation experts, and volunteers. The wide variety of multidisciplinary input and support has made this a successful immersive simulation experience. This can also lead to opening a dialogue for preparation and planning within the host institution for effective incident assessment and surge capacity responses at the health care facility level2.

**Practical implementation advice (materials needed, length of session, faculty/facilitator needs, preparation needs, etc):**

This simulation is extremely dynamic and extensive; it requires a significant amount of manpower and coordination. As listed in Section V., there is a minimum of 20 people required to fill the roles listed. Having additional personnel can add to the experience but is not required. Also, learners may be used to fill some of the live actor roles.

Due to the large number of moving parts and simultaneous critical actions and decision making this simulation does require the use of 7 mannequins as well as radio communications between the Staging Director, Staging Manager, Sim Operators, and Sim Actors (RNs, EMS and Staff Surgeon) as described in Section V. Five sets of handheld radios were used, on various channels, to allow communications between a) Stage Director, Stage Manager, and Staff surgeon on one channel, and b) between the Sim Operators and Sim Actor Nurses for various cases running simultaneously on the other 4 channels. The handheld radios as well as the Sim Actor nurse badges were color coded for ease of direct communication. The on-floor faculty observers were behind mobile 1 way mirrors so that their presence was not distracting to the learners.

The length of the entire simulation is approximately 85 minutes. The timeline is available in Table 1. The scenario is 45 minutes, the debriefing is 25 minutes, and the skills demonstration for junctional hemorrhage control and tourniquet placement is 15 minutes.

The faculty and facilitators should come into the simulation having reviewed the various patients and critical actions of the case, as well as basic disaster preparedness and surge capacity concepts.

Regarding the imaging modalities, hard copies were given to the Sim Actor nurse for each patient; s/he handed these out to the learners at the appropriate time in the scenario after the particular tests were ordered. Regarding the arterial hemorrhages on live victims, we affixed small junctional hemorrhage trainers to the standardized patient’s affected extremity and moulaged.

The overall implementation requires a large commitment of time, manpower and energy. However, the return on investment is very high and is worth the effort required. Learning the skills to effectively triage patients and manage resources is a skill that requires experiential learning. Once a disaster situation arises, there is minimal time to pontificate on the best course of action, and providers are required to rely heavily on their previous training.

**How it has been successfully deployed (including common pitfalls, tips for success, etc):**

This was originally developed and successfully implemented at Hennepin County Medical Center’s Simulation Center. The residents participating in the simulation were immersed in this real life scenario and the large number of people involved added to the potentially overwhelming feeling of a real disaster. Institutions without a simulation center available could feasibly prepare this activity in other settings such as unused hospital wards, after hours clinic spaces, or areas of the emergency department that may be closed during certain parts of the day.

When preparing to perform this simulation be sure that there is adequate time to brief all Sim Actors, ensuring that all Sim Actors have a good understanding of their individual roles as well as flow of the entire scenario. Once patients start presenting to the ED, there is little time for the Stage Director to explain or clarify specific roles or portions of the scenario . A number of logistics addressing potential pitfalls and tips for success are detailed in the section above.

**What are the limitations of the resources and what are your ideas for improving/expanding it (adding this self-reflection component is encouraged):**

The limitations of this scenario are mainly that it is very labor and time sensitive. However, these are the same elements that make it so valuable to the participants. The chaos that is created in this environment is real even though the patients are not. Regardless of our profession within the healthcare or public safety realm, it is our goal to bring order to that chaos.

Ways that one may consider expanding the simulation could be incorporating other departments or specialties into a larger interprofessional and/or interdepartmental simulation experience. For example, instead of the nurses being Sim Actors, this scenario could be used as a training exercise for ED resident physicians and ED nurses together, either in the simulation center or in-situ. This could also be expanded to include other ED providers such as healthcare assistants, pharmacists, and others. This could be expanded to include other specialties, such as having surgical residents respond to this mass casualty as well. Another potential expansion would be to include prehospital providers triaging patients from a simulated disaster scene, delivering those patients to the ED and giving report. The difficulties of a multi-disciplinary exercise would be both resources and scheduling. This might be overcome by using a hospital wide resident training day or performing this exercise in-situ.

If this educational session was scheduled for a period of time greater than 90 minutes, lengthening the debriefing time to include more discussion of individual case management, as well covering the primary objectives, would be beneficial. Further, since many of our residents seemed to lack prior training on tourniquet application and junctional wound packing, in retrospect, this should have been more of a primary objective as opposed to a secondary objective.
