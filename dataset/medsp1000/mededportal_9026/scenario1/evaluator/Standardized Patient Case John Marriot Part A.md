Authors: Matthew N. Goldenberg, M.D., Derrick Hamaoka, M.D., Lisa McCurry, M.D., Graceanne Adamo, Lisa Rawn

PART A

**Purpose of the materials:**

This standardized patient case was designed as a formative educational experience to enhance medical trainees’ skills in psychiatric interviewing and case formulation. Conducting a clinical evaluation of a patient with acute emotional and/or behavioral concerns is an essential task of medical personnel in a variety of disciplines including not only psychiatry but also primary care, emergency medicine and even medical and surgical specialties. Of particular importance is the assessment of a patient’s risk of harm to self or others. Medical trainees must become proficient in the initial evaluation of patients with mental health concerns, and this standardized patient case provides an opportunity to develop those skills.

Mood disorders are common and can lead to substantial morbidity and even mortality. Bipolar disorder is a severe form of mood disorder characterized by dramatic shifts in mood states including periods of mania and depression. Bipolar disorder affects about 2 percent of the population and is the sixth leading cause of disability worldwide. Manic symptoms include elevated mood, grandiosity, decreased sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, psychomotor agitation and reckless behavior. Depressive symptoms include sadness, an inability to experience pleasure, sleep and appetite problems, low energy, poor concentration, guilt, psychomotor agitation or retardation and suicidal thoughts and/or attempts. Safety is a major concern among patients with bipolar disorder. The suicide rate among those with bipolar disorder is 10-20 times the rate in the general population. Erratic behavior during the manic phase of illness can also be dangerous.

This case provides an opportunity for trainees to interview a patient with common psychiatric symptoms. In 2007, the Association of Directors of Medical Student Education in Psychiatry (ADMSEP, www.amdsep.org) published clinical learning objectives in psychiatry for medical students which included identifying the symptoms of and discussing the phenomenology of bipolar disorder. The encounter allows students to gain familiarity with symptoms of mania including elevated mood, grandiosity and psychomotor agitation. The trainees have an opportunity to experience a “challenging” interview in that the patient speaks fast, can be difficult to redirect and has poor insight into his/her illness. Students have an opportunity to hone their skills in handling “difficult” situations, including having to interrupt a patient and specify professional boundaries.

**Description of the development process:**

The case history and related materials were developed by the submitting authors who are or were faculty members at Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences, the nation’s federal medical school. The case details were created from a composite of numerous patients seen over the course of several years of clinical work with both military and civilian populations. There is no identifiable information in the case that refers to any specific person or persons. The case was initially created by several of the authors (L.M., G.A., L.R.). The cases have since been independently reviewed for accuracy and modified by other authors (D.H, M.G.).

**Information pertaining to how the materials have been used:**

This standardized patient case has been used as a formative educational tool to enrich medical students’ clinical skills during their third-year clinical psychiatry clerkship. Over the course of their clerkship, students each interview five standardized patients, each representing a core problem in clinical psychiatry. Students interview the standardized patient for 20 minutes while being observed (on video) by a faculty member and a peer. All encounters are recorded for later review by student and/or faculty.

Following the interview, the student and the standardized patient each independently complete a checklist about the content of the interview. The standardized patient then gives the trainee verbal feedback regarding their experience as a patient in the encounter. Students then receive feedback from the faculty and/or peer who observed their interviews. The case—including the history, mental status examination, assessment and plan—is then discussed in a group format led by a faculty member.

**Methods for training:** In the initial stages of development, only one standardized patient (SP) was recruited to train on this case. The first training involved one standardized patient educator training the SP on the details of the case as presented, with limited input from the authoring faculty. The case was then piloted by having a psychiatrist unfamiliar with the details perform an intake interview with the SP. After the interview was completed, there was a discussion with both physicians and the SP educators in which all case elements were reviewed and a final standard was set for each.

All standardized patients recruited for this case (including the “pilot” SP) underwent training by SP educator. The SPs were first trained to portray the appropriate level of psychiatric symptomatology (e.g. elated mood, grandiosity, talkativeness, expansive affect), then trained on the patient history and the SP checklist they would be filling out. After the SPs demonstrated accuracy on both the symptoms and the case history/checklist, the SPs were brought together and the portrayal was adjusted by the psychiatrist and the SP educators until the SPs were unified in their portrayal.

Encounters that SPs have with students are recorded and observed by a psychiatrist and/or standardized patient educators to ensure the accuracy of the portrayal. Any needed adjustments to the portrayal are made based on these observations. As needed, additional SPs are trained by standardized patient educators and their portrayals evaluated and adjusted for accuracy by psychiatrists and standardized patient educators.

**Data to support the content of the materials:** The developers of the case have extensive clinical experience in the evaluation and treatment of psychiatric patients. Three of the authors (L.M., D.H. and M.G.) are practicing psychiatrists with diverse clinical experience, including work in both military and civilian settings. The case highlights several issues—mania, hyperreligiosity, grandiosity—whose epidemiologic and clinical importance has been extensively shown in research literature.

**Data to support the reliability of any related checklists or rating scales:** No formal study has yet been conducted on the reliability of checklists. The checklists have primarily served as self-assessment tools for the students. Future work will focus on comparing student checklists with those completed by the SP as well as an outside observer.

**Data to support the accuracy of case portrayal:** Faculty members including both physicians and standardized patient educators observe each standardized patient encounter and provide feedback to the standardized patients in order to ensure accuracy and consistency of the case portrayal.

**General suggestions for using the materials:** These materials were designed for use in a formative, non-assessed educational encounter, but can be used in a summative, graded encounter as well. Designed for use with third-year medical students, the case can be used for a variety of learning levels. The case has thus far been used in a psychiatry clerkship, but may have particular relevance for students or residents training in emergency medicine, internal medicine or family medicine. Our department has found this case to be an excellent tool for developing students’ abilities to conduct a psychiatric interview and discuss diagnostic and management decisions. Student feedback has generally been positive—in formal end-of-clerkship anonymous feedback and in less formal discussions during the clerkship, students generally have found the case realistic, and the experience fair and reasonable for their level of training.
