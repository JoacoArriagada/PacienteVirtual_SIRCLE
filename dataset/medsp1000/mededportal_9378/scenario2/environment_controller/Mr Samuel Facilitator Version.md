**PART 1**

Mr. Samuel is a 27-year-old man who presents to your internal medicine clinic today as a new patient with a chief complaint of difficulty breathing. He moved to Miami three months ago from Boston. Shortly after moving here, Mr. Samuel started having difficulty breathing while he was running. He has been running since high school and typically runs about 20-25 miles per week. The month prior to developing difficulty breathing during exercise, he did not run at all because he had twisted an ankle. When he resumed running, he assumed the difficulty breathing was due to deconditioning. However, even as he has steadily increased his exercise back to his 20-25 miles per week, he was still having trouble breathing when he ran. He describes his breathing difficulty as chest tightness and “trouble moving air” in and out. He also experiences some coughing during these episodes. Initially these symptoms would subside within 15 minutes or so after completing his run. Now he seems to have intermittent difficulty breathing unrelated to exercise. Once or twice a week, he will experience shortness of breath, chest tightness and a dry cough that lasts for several hours. The symptoms appear to gradually resolve if he sits down and rests. Activity exacerbates his symptoms. He denies chest pain, palpitations, nausea, vomiting, visual changes or dizziness associated with these episodes.

Mr. Samuel has never had any surgeries or hospitalizations. He takes no medications and has no known drug allergies. He has never smoked or used illicit substances. He drinks alcohol occasionally (1-2 drinks on average a week) and denies binge drinking. He does not have pets. He has not travelled outside of the United States in over five years. He developed a positive PPD during college after travelling in Central America. He subsequently took 9 months of INH without difficulty. No one in his family has any medical problems. He has been in a mutually monogamous sexual relationship with one female partner for the last 3 years.

On review of systems he denies recent illnesses, fever, night sweats, or a cough productive of sputum. He does admit to occasional epigastric discomfort and a burning sensation in his throat, particularly after a large meal.

You ask Mr. Samuel if he is currently experiencing any symptoms and he states that he is having some trouble breathing that started just prior to arriving at your office.

**PART 5B**

Three weeks later, you get a page from the emergency room. Mr. Samuel’s presented in extreme respiratory distress. He was tachypneic with labored breathing and minimally audible inspiratory and expiratory wheezing on auscultation. He could only speak one to two word phrases before stopping to catch his breath. His oxygen saturations were 86% on room air. He was treated with oxygen, three back-to-back albuterol nebulizers with ipratropium, and given a dose of systemic corticosteroids. His oxygen saturations continued to drop and he required increasing support with supplemental oxygen. An ABG on 10L face mask was 7.29/55/80/23/-1/96%. He was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit in anticipation of impending respiratory failure.

The inpatient team reviewed his history and wondered why he continued to deteriorate when the medical management of his asthma was excellent. They had no concerns about non-adherence or improper use of medication. They wondered if there might be an environmental trigger to his asthma. From his girlfriend, they were able to obtain the following information: Mr. Samuel is a pastry and desserts chef in a South Beach hotel. After culinary school he worked as a chef for several years. A year ago, he then took some additional courses in pastry making and has worked with desserts since then. In retrospect, she believes that when Mr. Samuel first was diagnosed with asthma, his symptoms were worse on days he worked and better when they went on vacation and during the move from Boston.

After discharge, under the guidance on an occupational medicine specialist, Mr. Samuel returns to work for a four week trial. He fills out a log with the following information:

1. Peak flow 4 times per day (before work, mid-shift, end of shift, and bedtime, with similar times on non-work days).
2. He records his symptoms and albuterol use throughout the day
3. He records what types of pastries he is making each day

He then takes a two week vacation and records the same information.

Analysis of the information obtained showed that Mr. Samuel’s symptoms appear to be worse on the days that he works with wheat flour (Triticum aestivum). His symptoms are improved on the days he is not working and even better during his two week vacation. Because the implications of occupational asthma are quite serious, Mr. Samuel and the occupation medicine physician decided to do an inhalation challenge. Under careful monitoring, Mr. Samuel did spirometry before and after inhaling the wheat flour. His pre FEV1/FVC % was 90% predicted. His post FEV1/FVC % was 70% predicted. Based on the 20% reduction in his spirometry, a diagnosis of occupational asthma was confirmed.

Mr. Samuel was counseled as to the various ways he could modify his work environment to maximize his respiratory health. Removing Mr. Samuel from the exposure is important. If the sensitizing agent (in this case wheat flour) cannot be eliminated from his work environment, then the patient may need to be removed from the environment. The ideal solution would be to ensure that Mr. Samuel does not work with wheat flour any longer. Less ideal solutions would be to reduce exposure by improving ventilation when working with the sensitizing agent and/or implementing respiratory protection. Unfortunately, respiratory protection (such as wearing a mask) is not a very practical long-term solution in many work environments since it affects clear speech and requires a carefully fit mask.

Mr. Samuel was extremely grateful to finally have a clearer understanding of the cause of his problem, and to have a roadmap to health.
