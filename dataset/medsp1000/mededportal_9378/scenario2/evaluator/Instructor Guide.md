**Mr. Samuel**

**Instructor Guide**

This progressively disclosed case of a young man with asthma refractory to initial therapy was written for a case based learning course for third year medical students designed to integrate basic science learning into the clinical year, and to create opportunities to face diagnostic error and/or reflect on the diagnostic reasoning process.

The knowledge learning objectives are as follows:

**Clinical Learning Objectives**

1. Create a differential diagnosis for a patient who presents with dyspnea
2. Describe a hypothesis driven approach to the physical exam in a patient with dyspnea
3. Describe a standard approach to reading a chest radiograph
4. Define asthma
5. Explain how the diagnosis of asthma is made
6. Propose a stepwise approach to treating asthma
7. Identify factors that may lead to an exacerbation of asthma or non-response to treatment
8. Recognize the workplace as a source of triggers for asthma

**Basic Science Learning Objectives**

1. Explain the pathophysiology of asthma
2. Identify the mechanism of action and side effects of drugs used in the treatment of asthma
3. Explain basic pulmonary spirometry and create flow-volume curves
4. Explain how lung volumes change during an acute asthma attack
5. Explain the stimulus and mechanisms involved in exercise-induced bronchoconstriction in asthmatic patients

With the final diagnosis of one of the most common occupational diseases in developed countries, occupationally-induced asthma, the case also addresses curricular mandates to include occupational health as part of the broadening focus on social and behavioral health.(3) Indeed, occupational factors aggravate or cause 10-25% of adult asthma cases. (11) Yet, occupational asthma is likely still under recognized and under reported. A recent convenience sampling survey of more than 200 Canadian pulmonologists revealed only moderate support for consideration of occupational factors in all working adults with asthma and moderate agreement that physicians’ low awareness results in a failure to ask about occupational factors.(9)

The primary learning goals of the case are arguably the two fundamental competencies for undergraduate medical students in the area of occupational health and medicine – to elicit a basic occupational history and to consider occupational exposures in the differential diagnosis of disease. In this scenario copy, the branch is the version in which the occupational trigger is not identified before the patient deteriorates and the case proceeds through the emergency/ICU pathway.

This case is a composite drawn from reviews of occupational asthma and bakery worker’s asthma specifically.(5), (14) It was reviewed by a pulmonologist for final accuracy and overall believability.

1. Practical Implementation

Group Size and Faculty:

This case is designed for the small group format; we have used it with groups ranging from 4-8 students. When possible, groups have been facilitated by one basic science and one clinical faculty member. Nonetheless, a clinician well-versed in the case and familiar with basic pulmonary physiology can run the session alone. Clinician facilitators who plan to include reflection on diagnostic reasoning who have not done this before should familiarize themselves with the common cognitive biases leading to diagnostic error.(8)

Materials and Preparation:

* Student versions for each student.
* Facilitator version of case.
* White board charting: To foster use of the white board and to save time, the facilitator draws the following on the board BEFORE students enter the room:
* The Organ System Table for q3, with only the bolded information filled in.
* Pulmonary Lung Capacities diagram – skeleton with curve, but NO labels

Conduct of Session:

The facilitator version of the case includes explicit instructions each step along the way. We have a student read each section aloud, and then ask for volunteers to go to the board at the appropriate points. The facilitator guides the students in collectively answering each question. To foster maximal collaborative learning, we do not permit students to use outside resources during the sessions unless the group cannot come to an answer and the faculty member cannot help them sufficiently through questioning or does not know the answer themselves.

For this scenario copy, the emergency/ICU branch is used.

Our sessions include a structured feedback component (see Student Participation Rubric) in which each student is encouraged to state one positive contributor in group or individual behavior to learning, and one area for improvement.

The end of the facilitator version of the case includes suggested questions to begin a discussion of the diagnostic reasoning process.
