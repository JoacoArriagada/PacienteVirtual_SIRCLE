**Q8. What is forced vital capacity? What is FEV1? Label and define the lung volumes on the diagram drawn on the board.**

FVC (forced vital capacity) is the maximum volume of air that can be exhaled during a forced maneuver.

FEV1 (forced expired volume in one second) is the volume expired in the first

second of maximal expiration after a maximal inspiration. This is a measure of how quickly the lungs can be emptied.

FEV1/FVC: FEV1 expressed as a fraction of the FVC, gives a clinically useful index of airflow limitation

*Facilitators: A skeleton of the following diagram will be drawn on the board. Have a student go to the board, and using the collective intelligence of the group, label the diagram with the various lung volumes (the actual volume in mL is not as important as knowing which portion of the diagram corresponds to VC, TV, etc.).*

**Tidal volume (TV)** is the amount of gas inhaled and exhaled with each normal breath.

**Vital capacity (VC)** is the total amount of gas that can be exhaled after a maximal inhalation.

**Residual volume (RV)** is the volume of gas remaining in the lungs after maximal expiration.

**Total lung capacity (TLC)** is the volume of gas in lungs at the end of maximal inspiration. TLC is the sum of the vital capacity plus the residual volume.

**Functional residual capacity (FRC)** is the volume of air in the lungs at the end of a normal expiration. It is the balance between the tendency of the chest wall to spring outwards and the tendency of the lung to collapse. FRC is not the same volume all the time and can be affected by many factors. FRC is the sum of ERV and RV.

**Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** is the volume that can be inspired after inspiration of the tidal volume.

**Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** is the volume that can be expired after the expiration of the tidal volume ![Pulmonary volumes](data:image/png;base64...)

**Q10. What is asthma? How do you diagnose asthma?**

Asthma is a condition characterized by a variable degree of airflow obstruction, bronchial hyper-responsiveness, and airway inflammation. *The diagnosis of asthma requires a compatible history and the presence of variable airway obstruction. In* the absence of airflow limitation, the presence of pharmacologically induced bronchial hyper-responsiveness is sufficient (such as a methacholine challeng*e). One criterion for asthma is a greater than or equal to 12% improvement in FEV1, and at least 200 ml, following bronchodilator administration.* An alternative criterion is an improvement in FEV1 of at least 20%, and at least 200 mL, after 2 weeks of treatment with an anti-inflammatory agent such as an inhaled corticosteroid.

**Q11. What is the pathophysiology of asthma?**

Inflammation of the airways is an underlying abnormality in all patients with asthma**.** The spectrum of inflammatory changes may include submucosal infiltration with activated lymphocytes and eosinophils, activation of mast cells, epithelial changes and basement membrane thickening**. In severe asthma there is also mucus plugging in airways, goblet cell hyperplasia, and smooth muscle hypertrophy and hyperplasia.** In susceptible individuals, this inflammation causes recurrent episodes of wheezing, chest tightness and cough associated with variable airflow obstruction that is reversible. **The inflammation also causes in increase in the existing bronchial hyper-responsiveness to a variety of stimuli.**

**Allergy and atopic sensitization play a major role in the initial cascade that triggers an asthma attack. Specific IgE fixes to airway mast cells and basophils, causing release of multiple inflammatory mediators and bronchospasm**. Mast cell activation releases histamine and leukotrienes, which are responsible for the immediate constriction of bronchial smooth muscle and airway edema, causing airflow obstruction. This early bronchospastic response usually responds promptly to short-acting bronchodilators and resolves within an hour. Subsequently, proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, generated by resident inflammatory cells such as mast cells, macrophages and epithelial cells, stimulate chemotaxis of eosinophils, neutrophils and activated lymphocytes, which provide ongoing inflammation, and airway hyper-responsiveness. These mediators result in delayed airflow obstruction 4 to 12 hours after the antigen exposure. This delayed airflow obstruction does not respond easily to bronchodilators and indicates the inflammatory nature of this delayed response.

Th-1 and Th-2 lymphocytes and their cytokines also play a major role in asthma. Th-1 cells generate IL-2 and interferon-gamma, which are mediators in the defense against infection. Th-2 cells produce multiple mediators of allergic inflammation, including IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, and IL-13. Early and persistent Th-2 predominance may be important in promoting ongoing airway inflammation and asthma symptoms.

**Q12. How do total lung capacity, functional reserve capacity and residual volume change during an acute asthma attack? Why?**

During an episode of acute asthma, the airway lumen are narrowed and airway resistance is elevated. Because there is increased air trapping causing hyperinflation, FRC and RV may be elevated. Total lung capacity (TLC) usually remains normal, although elevated TLC is sometimes seen in long-standing asthma.

**Q13. What two types of initial medications might you prescribe Mr. Samuel for his asthma? What is the mechanism of action and common side effects of these medications?**

Pharmacologic treatment of asthma is approached in a “stepwise” fashion. A cardinal rule of the stepwise approach is the necessity of reconsidering at each stage the possible contributions of environmental triggers, adherence and co-morbid conditions. **Inhaled corticosteroids and beta agonists** are the backbone of treating the early “steps” or stages of asthma.

**Quick relief for step 1 (mild intermittent asthma):**

**Quick-acting B2-selective adrenergic agonists (i.e. albuterol):**

Onset of action is 5 minutes or less, peak effect in 30-60 minutes, duration of action 4-6 hours.

**MOA:** Albuterol is a B2-adrenergic agonist, which binds to B2-receptors in the lungs and causes bronchial smooth muscle relaxation. B2-adrenergic agonists stimulate intracellular adenyl cyclase, which increases cAMP levels. This causes relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle and inhibition of release of mediators of immediate hypersensitivity from cells, especially from mast cells.

**Side effects:** dose-dependent, sympathomimetic-type side effects include tremor, anxiety, palpitations, and tachycardia. Can also cause dose-dependent hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia.

**Step 2 (mild persistent asthma) long-term control:**

**Inhaled corticosteroids (i.e. fluticasone, beclomethasone, etc.):**

**MOA:** suppresses airway inflammation and decreases nonspecific bronchial hyper-responsiveness by inhibiting the production of cytokines, an effect which reduces eosinophil infiltration, inhibits macrophage and eosinophil function, decreases mediator cells in the epithelium, reduces vascular permeability, and reduces the production of leukotrienes.

**Side effects:** Sore throat, coughing on inhalation, weak or hoarse voice and oral or pharyngeal candidiasis. Rinsing the mouth after each use and using an aero-chamber with the MDI can reduce the risk of candidiasis. Virtually no clinically significant adverse systemic effects observed in adults taking low-to-medium doses. At high doses, may have increased risk of skin bruising, cataracts, elevated intraocular pressure and accelerated loss of bone mass. In children, growth retardation is a concern, but ultimately most children will reach their normal predicted height.

**Subsequent Steps:**

Step 3 – moderate persistent asthma – typically requires either changing from a **low to medium dose** inhaled corticosteroid or the addition of a **long acting beta agonist** to the low dose inhaled corticosteroid. Mast cell stabilizers, leukotriene receptor antagonists and theophylline may also be considered in steps 2 and 3

Step 4 – severe persistent asthma - routinely uses both the **medium dose corticosteroid and the long acting beta** **agonist**. .

Steps 5 and 6 involve the treatment of more severe forms of asthma with the use of high dose inhaled corticosteroids, oral corticosteroids and allergy medications for patients with allergies.

**Inhaled long-acting B2 agonist bronchodilators (i.e. salmeterol and formoterol):**

Onset of action for formoterol is 5 minutes; for salmeterol is 15-20 minutes. Sustained activity for more than 12 hours. Should only be used in conjunction with inhaled corticosteroids.

B2-adrenergic agonists stimulate intracellular adenyl cyclase, which increases cAMP levels. This causes relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle and inhibition of release of mediators of immediate hypersensitivity from cells, especially from mast cells.

Side effects: generally mild and may include sympathomimetic-type stimulation such as muscle cramps and tachycardia. A black box warning exists for inhaled long-acting B2 agonists. There is an increased risk of fatal or near-fatal asthma attacks when long-acting B-agonists were added to “usual therapy” to treat asthma. The mechanism remains uncertain.

**Oral leukotriene modifiers (i.e. Leukotriene receptor antagonists: montelukast, zafirlukast; Leukotriene modifier: zileuton):**

Onset of action: several hours within a few hours but maximal effect occurs within a few days of administration.

MOA: Montelukast and zafirlukast block the action of leukotriene at the cysteinyl leukotriene receptor. Zileuton inhibits production of the cysteinyl leukotrienes by antagonizing the action of 5-lipoxygenase . Cysteinyl leukotriene production and receptor occupation have been correlated with the pathophysiology of asthma, including airway edema, smooth muscle constriction, and altered cellular activity associated with the inflammatory process.

Side effects: Very well tolerated. May cause headache, rhinitis, pharyngitis, or gastritis. Zileuton causes reversible chemical hepatitis in 2-4% of patients.
