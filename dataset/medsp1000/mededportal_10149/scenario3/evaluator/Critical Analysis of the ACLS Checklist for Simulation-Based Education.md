# Critical Analysis of the ACLS Checklists for Simulation-Based Education

# Educational Objectives:

1. To describe the purpose and basic properties of the ACLS checklists for simulation-based education including number of items and scales, and psychometric properties;   
2. To describe the application of the ACLS checklists for simulation-based education to the field of health sciences education;   
3. To evaluate the relative strengths and weaknesses of the ACLS checklists for simulationbased education; and   
4. To provide the ACLS checklists for simulation-based education and supplemental material to aid in its administration.

B. Brief Description/Purpose: The ACLS checklists for simulation-based education were developed in 2003 as a tool for assessing second year internal medicine residents in Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) at Northwestern University as part of a mastery learning program and simulation-based training program. The checklists are used to assess resident performance in medical knowledge and patient care in 6 common ACLS events: ventricular fibrillation, pulseless electrical activity, supraventricular tachycardia, symptomatic bradycardia, asystole, and ventricular tachycardia. These checklists were developed using the American Heart Association (AHA) ACLS 2005 guidelines and then updated to the AHA ACLS 2010 guidelines. Each checklist was created using a specific guideline for developing and applying evaluation checklists 1 and was to be used with a fullbody human patient simulator and covered patient care, medical knowledge, and communication skills focusing primarily on the team leader. The pre-test session was designed to evaluate the resident’s competency in medical knowledge and patient care based on the standard AHA ACLS course and clinical experience, while the post-test session was designed to test mastery of the skill being tested based on this curricular intervention.

All second year residents were assessed by the examiner on the skills checklist for each of the 6 ACLS scenarios and a minimum passing score (MPS) had to be met to be considered proficient in that ACLS skill.

Development and Psychometrics: A review of 207 academic and community hospitals showed an annual average of 54.1 events requiring ACLS per facility. Internal medicine residents are expected to recognize and manage life-threatening events that occur infrequently with little practice. The ACLS checklists for simulation-based education were designed to improve resident responses to the 6 most common ACLS scenarios through the use of a human patient simulator, checklists, and deliberate practice.2 This curriculum consists of 5 sessions in the simulation center: 2 testing (pre-test and post-test) sessions and 3 teaching sessions. The checklists used for all 6 scenarios were developed using the AHA 2010 ACLS guidelines and a guideline for creating evaluation checklists based on 12 main checkpoints. These checklists are to be used with a full-body human patient simulator allowing for realistic responses to medications, compatibility with a defibrillator and pacemaker, and practice time for chest compressions and airway management. The checklist items are weighted the same according to a three-point scale: $\mathbf { \check { A } } = \mathbf { \check { \Pi } }$ Done Correctly; $\mathsf { B } = \mathsf { I }$ Done Incorrectly; $\mathsf { C } = \mathsf { N o t }$ Done.”

# E. Application to Health Sciences Education and/or Health Sciences Education Research:

The ACGME has challenged programs to meet core competencies for their residents, one of which is patient care, through the use of milestones developed for the next accreditation system. The American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM) specifically requires that all residents be proficient in ACLS skills. Simulation as an educational tool is highly effective because it assesses specific skills and global patient care without risk to real patients. When combining simulation with reliable and valid checklists, summative assessments can determine competency in a specific skill.10 In this context, the use of this curriculum could be applied to assess patient care skills and retention of knowledge and satisfy both ACGME requirements and undergraduate medical education competency in the specific skill of Advanced Cardiac Life Support in relation to patient care and communication skills as based on EPAs.
