**Scenario 1: Airway/Breathing**

**Objectives:**

By the end of the session, each team of learners will:

* Perform CAB assessment and recognize apnea
* Call for help immediately on recognition of apnea
* Request cardiac monitor, pulse oximetry, and blood pressure
* Ensure IV access
* Perform basic positioning maneuvers to establish a patent airway, including chin lift and jaw thrust
* Demonstrate both one- and two-person bag-valve-mask ventilation techniques
* Demonstrate proper placement oropharyngeal airway device
* Assess efficacy of ventilation by examining chest movement and pulse oximetry data
* Discuss differential diagnosis of respiratory arrest and identify reversible causes

**Introduction for Learners:**

You have just arrived on the floor to check on Mrs. Anderson, a 72 year old woman who underwent hip replacement yesterday. Nursing staff called your intern because the patient was complaining of severe hip pain. The intern gave a verbal order for pain medicine, and asked you to go and check on the patient.

**The Rest of the Story:**

The patient had been receiving 2mg hydromorphone every 4-6 hours and had gotten her last dose 30 min ago. The patient reported no relief of pain, so the nurse called the intern, who ordered a one-time dose of 4mg hydromorphone. Shortly after this was administered, the patient stopped breathing.

**Case Progression:**

* Initially simulator is laying in bed, not attached to any monitors, no supplemental oxygen, unresponsive to verbal or painful stimuli.
* Ambu bag is available at the head of the bed but it remains in its packaging, and is not set up or attached to oxygen
* Initial VS: P 78, BP 159/82, R 0, SpO2 86%
* In the absence of appropriate treatment (meaning completion of ALL critical actions), pulse ox will decrease to 56% over 5 minutes
* Simulator will remain set to “can’t bag, can’t intubate” until ALL critical actions are complete, at which point airway will reset, pulse ox will come up to 96% over one minute, and other vital signs will remain stable
* If ALL critical actions are not completed within 5 minutes, the patient will undergo bradyasystolic arrest. Patient will not be recoverable at this point.

**Case Termination:**

Case will end when learners EITHER complete all critical actions successfully and stabilize patient, OR after patient has been in cardiac arrest for one minute.

**Critical Actions for Learners (and Consequences):**

* CAB assessment: check carotid pulse, look for respiratory effort
* Call for help – learners must request either code team or rapid response team
* Request cardiac monitor and pulse ox, check blood pressure
* Attach Ambu bag to oxygen, set at 10-15L
* Open airway using chin lift and initiate single-person BVM (will not work)
* Open airway using jaw thrust and initiate two-person BVM (will not work initially but will work once oral airway are inserted)
* Place oropharyngeal airway (will not work alone but will work in combination with jaw thrust and two-person BVM technique, causing pulse ox to increase and patient to stabilize)

**Teaching Points for Debriefing:**

* Assessment of responsiveness – verbal/painful stimuli
* CAB assessment
  + Check carotid pulse for <10 seconds and BEGIN CPR IF ABSENT OR IF YOU ARE UNCERTAIN
  + If pulse is present, look for breathing and begin ventilatory support if breathing is absent or agonal
  + PERFORM ABC ASSESSMENT EVERY TIME YOU ENCOUNTER AN UNRESPONSIVE OR CRITICALLY ILL PATIENT
* CALL FOR HELP IMMEDIATELY
  + IF CAB ASSESSMENT REVEALS A PROBLEM, CALL CODE TEAM
  + If patient is unresponsive but otherwise stable, it may be appropriate to call your intern/resident
  + When in doubt, call code team – better an inappropriate activation of the code team than a dead patient!
* “When you see a sick patient, say OMI” – oxygen, monitor (including pulse ox), IV access and check full vital signs…EVERY TIME
* MAIN CASE-SPECIFIC TEACHING POINT: EFFECTIVE BAG-VALVE MASK VENTILATION
  + BVM ventilation is the fastest way to provide artificial ventilation for respiratory arrest victims, but can be technically difficult
    - A tight seal over the victim’s face and a patent airway are required for ventilation to be effective
    - Anatomy and/or positioning can lead to partial or complete airway obstruction – this will cause air lead around mask or insufflation of air into victim’s stomach
    - Inadequate hand position can allow air to leak around the mask
  + It is essential to monitor the efficacy of BVM: you should see clear chest rise, and pulse ox should begin to improve within 30-60 seconds
  + Setting up the Ambu bag:
    - Remove from packaging
    - Fit mask to stem of bag
    - Attach tubing to oxygen reservoir
    - Attach other end of tubing to “Xmas tree” adapter on oxygen meter
    - Turn oxygen flow to 10-15L
  + Performing one person BVM
    - Chin lift maneuver and hand positioning
    - Assessment of efficacy – emphasize that one person BVM is UNLIKELY to be effective when performed by rescuers who don’t have a lot of experience with it
  + Performing two person BVM
    - Jaw thrust maneuver and hand positioning
    - Assessment of efficacy – more likely to work, but may need adjunct
  + Airway adjuncts
    - Oral airway
      * First line in unconscious patients
      * So helpful that BAGGING SHOULD FEEL WRONG WITHOUT IT!
      * Disadvantages include risk of airway occlusion with incorrect placement (usually obvious), not tolerated by patient who are even remotely conscious
    - Nasal airway
      * Not critical for this case but demonstrate
      * First line airway adjunct in conscious/semiconscious patients
      * Disadvantages include small caliber, lack of tongue positioning, potential for placement in esophagus
* Once ventilation is successful, meaning good chest rise is achieved and oxygenation improves, remember to reassess overall stability, including pulse and blood pressure
* If two-person BVM with oral airway is not effective, patient is classified as “can’t bag,” and definitive airway maneuvers are emergently needed – also remember that this patient is at risk for cardiac arrest, and pulse and rhythm checks must be performed frequently
* Once effective assisted ventilation is achieved and oxygenation stabilizes, think about reversible causes for patient’s respiratory arrest – in this case, opiate overdose which could be treated with naloxone
* In persons breathing room air, respiratory arrest will cause hypoxia within about three minutes (longer for persons breathing supplemental oxygen)
* Hypoxia will worsen until artificial ventilation is initiated
* Severe hypoxia will eventually lead to cardiac arrest and death
