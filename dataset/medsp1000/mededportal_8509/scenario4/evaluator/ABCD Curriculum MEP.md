**Case 4 – Hypovolemic Shock in a Child**

**Objectives:**

By the end of the session, each team of learners will:

* Perform ABC assessment of an infant and call for help immediately
* Request appropriate cardiorespiratory monitoring and ensure patent IV access and consider IO access when appropriate
* Assess and recognize physical signs (including poor perfusion/prolonged capillary refill time, sunken fontanelle, poor skin turgor, tachycardia, tachypnea, sunken eyes) and historical symptoms of hypovolemia/dehydration in an infant (including poor urine output/decreased wet diapers, poor oral intake, lethargy)
* Assess and recognize signs of progression from dehydration/hypovolemia to hypovolemic shock (particularly significance of progressive tachycardia)
* Demonstrate proper fluid resuscitation by appropriate volume of fluid bolus by weight and push/pull technique of fluid administration
* Recognize and respond appropriately to hypoglycemia in a pediatric patient as demonstrated by request for dextrose bolus

**Introduction for Learners:**

You are on your pediatric or ER rotation and are told by your resident to go see a new admission in the ER, Toby, a 6 month old brought in by his mother for 2 days of vomiting, diarrhea and fever. On your arrival, the nurse tells you that the baby has recently become more lethargic since he had been previously evaluated by the resident. Bloodwork is pending and the child has received a dose of IV antibiotic already, as there had been some concern for sepsis initially.

**The Rest of the Story:**

The patient has received IV Ceftriaxone, but has not been started yet on any IV fluids (if asked by students). The child’s oral intake for past 24 hours has been poor and he is hypoglycemic, but this is not known (D-stick not yet done, if students request it, glucose fingerstick = 40 g/dL). There is an IV in place, but when RN attempts to use it, it is nonfunctional.

**Case Progression:**

* Initially sim baby is lying in crib, not attached to any monitors, no supplemental oxygen, will cry to painful stimuli. Both nasal cannula and nonrebreather mask are available at head of bed
* If asked, child’s fontanelle is sunken and mucous membranes are dry with cap refill 4 seconds
* Initial VS: P 160, BP 80/40, R 45, SpO2 95%
* Becomes progressively tachycardic to 190s over first 2-3 minutes. Subsequently, BP decreases to 50/25 as case progresses
* Becomes progressively more lethargic so that baby is only grunting, no vocalizations.

**Case Termination:**

Case will end when learners EITHER place O2, re-establish IV access, administer 40 cc/kg of IVF and correct hypoglycemia OR at 7 minutes.

**Critical Actions for Learners (and Consequences):**

* ABC assessment: ensure open airway, check for adequate chest rise, (recognize tachypnea), assess brachial or femoral pulse and capillary refill time
* Call for help – learners must request either rapid response team or help from ER staff (who will be tied up in a pediatric arrest in other room)
* Request cardiac monitor and pulse ox, check blood pressure and cap refill
* Ensure patent IV is in place, ask for additional IV access
* Place patient on O2 as first “drug” administered in shock state
* Recognize need for IV fluids and type of fluid bolus, amount to be administered, timing of administration (goal directed therapy with continual reassessment of vitals, perfusion, liver size) and method of delivery (push/pull/stopcock, bolus via syringe push rather than fluid on pump)
* Recognize lethargy as sign of hypoglycemia and administer D25 (2-5 cc/kg)

**Teaching Points for Debriefing:**

* Assessment of responsiveness – verbal/painful stimuli- modification of GCS for non-verbal infants
* ABC assessment
  + Position airway with chin lift- large occiput in infant can cause airway obstruction and decreased responsiveness
  + Assess breathing and recognize tachypnea as physiologic compensation for acidosis from dehydration
  + Check brachial or femoral pulse in infant
  + PERFORM ABC ASSESSMENT EVERY TIME YOU ENCOUNTER AN UNRESPONSIVE OR CRITICALLY ILL PATIENT
* CALL FOR HELP IMMEDIATELY
  + If patient is unresponsive but otherwise stable, it may be appropriate to call your intern/resident
  + IF ABC ASSESSMENT REVEALS A PROBLEM, CALL RRT (can even call in pediatric ED)
* “When you see a sick patient, say OMI” – oxygen, monitor (including pulse ox), IV access…EVERY TIME (even in a child)
  + If rapid fluid administration is necessary, preferable to have 2 larger gauge IVs in place, but this may be difficult in a dehydrated infant
    - Discuss placement of IO as 2nd line option if shock is progressive and PIV access is not possible (if time allows, demonstrate technique of IO placement and can increase level of difficulty of scenario in 2nd run through to require IO placement)
* In infants in shock, oxygen delivery is compromised, so O2 should be first intervention, regardless of O2 saturation
* Tachycardia is first sign of shock. Capillary refill/perfusion assessment is much more sensitive sign of decompensation and will change long before BP changes are seen (especially in children who can increase their systemic vascular resistance -or SVR- significantly)
* Other signs of dehydration in pediatrics: fontanelle, skin turgor/warmth/perfusion, mucous membrane assessment; historical questions including urine output/wet diapers, oral intake of fluids and solids, level of activity vs lethargy
* Discussion of shock algorithm for fluid administration
  + 20 cc/kg NS over 5-10 minutes with reassessment after each bolus
  + Push/pull method with stopcock
  + Syringe push rather than pump administration of fluid
  + Reference for push/pull syringe method of fluid administration in pediatric shock: Stoner MJ, Goodman DG, Cohen DM, Fernandez SA, Hall MW. Rapid Fluid Resuscitation in Pediatrics: Testing the American College of Critical Care Medicine Guideline. *Annals of Emergency Medicine* 2007; 50(5): 601-7.
* Discussion of hypoglycemia susceptibility in shock in children, particularly with decreased oral intake in hypovolemia, or in septic shock
* If time allows, discuss differentiation of septic, hypovolemic, cardiogenic shock
