**Scenario 2: Cardiac Arrest**

**Objectives:**

By the end of the session, each team of learners will:

* Perform CAB assessment and recognize cardiac arrest
* Call for help immediately upon recognition of cardiac arrest
* Request cardiac monitoring
* Ensure IV access
* Initiate high-quality chest compressions using appropriate body position, rate and depth, and incorporating use of stool and backboard
* Assess efficacy of chest compressions by checking pulses during CPR
* List the key features that distinguish ventricular fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia from other cardiac rhythms
* Perform conventional defibrillation (as opposed to automated) in response to ventricular fibrillation
* Assess pulse and blood pressure once rhythm is stabilized

**Introduction for Learners:**

You are pre-rounding on Mr. Hoffman, a 67-year-old man who was admitted yesterday for left lower extremity cellulitis. Before you enter the room, his nurse tells you that she is glad to see you, because she had been planning to call the intern to let her know that Mr. Hoffman had been complaining of some chest pressure.

**The Rest of the Story:**

The patient is having a myocardial infarction. About one minute after the students enter the room, the patient has a ventricular fibrillation arrest.

**Case Progression:**

* Initially the patient is in bed, not attached to any monitors, and has no supplemental oxygen.
* When cardiac monitoring is applied, stable vital signs with anterior ST-elevations are displayed.
* One minute into scenario, the patient loses vital signs and goes into ventricular fibrillation.
* In the absence of appropriate treatment (meaning completion of ALL critical actions), patient will remain in cardiac arrest
* If ALL critical actions are completed within 5 minutes, the patient will return to normal sinus rhythm and recover vital signs

**Case Termination:**

Case will end when learners EITHER complete all critical actions successfully and stabilize patient, OR after patient has been in cardiac arrest for five minutes.

**Critical Actions for Learners (and Consequences):**

* CAB assessment: check carotid pulse, look for respiratory effort
* Call for help – learners must request either code team or rapid response team
* Request cardiac monitor and pulse ox
* Initiate some form of bag-valve-mask ventilation - any will suffice, as this case emphasizes CPR and defibrillation, while BVM technique is covered extensively in Scenario 1 (will have no effect)
* Place backboard for CPR
* Initiate CPR using appropriate body position (including use of stool) and compression rate and depth (will initially have no effect, two full minutes of quality CPR are required for recovery to be possible)
* Perform defibrillation (will recover on second shock once CPR criterion is met)

**Teaching Points for Debriefing:**

* Assessment of responsiveness – verbal/painful stimuli
* CAB assessment
  + Check carotid pulse for <10 seconds and BEGIN CPR IF ABSENT OR IF YOU ARE UNCERTAIN
  + If pulse is present, look for breathing and begin ventilatory support if breathing is absent or agonal
  + PERFORM ABC ASSESSMENT EVERY TIME YOU ENCOUNTER AN UNRESPONSIVE OR CRITICALLY ILL PATIENT
* CALL FOR HELP IMMEDIATELY
  + IF CAB ASSESSMENT REVEALS A PROBLEM, CALL CODE TEAM
  + If patient is unresponsive but otherwise stable, it may be appropriate to call your intern/resident
  + When in doubt, call code team – better an inappropriate activation of the code team than a dead patient!
* “When you see a sick patient, say OMI” – oxygen, monitor (including pulse ox), IV access and check full vital signs…EVERY TIME
* MAIN CASE-SPECIFIC TEACHING POINT: IMPORTANCE OF DEFIBRILLATION, AND HOW/WHEN TO DO IT
  + In adult cardiac arrest victims, defibrillation is the single most important intervention, and should be performed as soon as possible
  + Call for defibrillator at the same time as the call for help
  + While waiting for defibrillator, PERFORM CPR AND CONTINUE QUALITY CPR UNTIL THE DEFIBRILLATOR IS CHARGED AND READY
  + Once the defibrillator is available, it should be used immediately – defibrillation is more important than CPR, airway, or breathing
    - This is because ventricular dysrhythmia is the most common treatable cause of adult cardiac arrest
    - Up to 90% of adults who regain a pulse after cardiac arrest will do so in response to defibrillation
    - As soon as defibrillator arrives, USE IT!
  + AED vs conventional defibrillator
    - AED designed for use by laypeople – analyzes rhythm and will only provide shock if VF/VT detected
    - Conventional defibrillator requires operator to analyze rhythm and determine whether shock is required
    - Defibrillators used in hospital can be used in either mode
  + Operation of defibrillator – “123”
    - Step 1: Attach (use of paddles vs pads, how to hook them up, how to position hands for paddles)
    - Step 2: Charge (button on right hand paddle vs button on monitor)
    - Step 3: Shock (simultaneous depression of paddle buttons vs button on monitor – the latter for use with pads ONLY)
    - Always resume CPR immediately after shock unless patient is talking, moving, or otherwise obviously alive
  + Recognition of VF (use simulator to demonstrate rhythm)
    - Disorganized, random electrical activity
    - No QRS complexes
    - ALWAYS a lethal rhythm, patients are clinically in cardiac arrest
  + Recognition of VT (use simulator to demonstrate rhythm)
    - Organized, regular QRS complexes
    - QRS has wide, bizarre appearance, may be almost sinusoidal
    - Patients may be completely stable, pulseless, or anywhere in between - always clinically assess the patient before treating
* SECOND MAJOR TEACHING POINT: QUALITY CPR
  + “CAB” rather than “ABC” – CPR more important than respiratory support in initial phase of resuscitation (2010 ACLS guidelines) – START CHEST COMPRESSIONS IMMEDIATELY WHEN CARDIAC ARREST IS IDENTIFIED, respiratory support can be initiated when help and equipment are available
  + Patient position – supine (demonstrate operation of hospital bed including CPR lever)
  + Body position and use of stool
  + Use of backboard (demonstrate backboard on front of crash cart)
  + Compression frequency > 100 bpm (suggest Stayin’ Alive as a way to check for adequate rate)
  + Compression depth – 2 inches with full recoil (recoil is critical because it allows filling)
  + “Cycles” = 2 min by clock or 5 rounds of 30:2
  + Only pause for rhythm check after each complete cycle – otherwise avoid any/all interruptions – every interruption worsens cerebral and cardiac ischemia and makes recovery less likely
* Precordial thump
  + May be considered in witnessed, monitored, VF/VT when defibrillator is not immediately available
  + Should not be used for unwitnessed arrest or unmonitored patients
  + Should not delay defibrillation or CPR
  + Demonstrate technique for this maneuver
* Airway and BVM in cardiac arrest
  + May be beneficial for respiratory arrest, suspected hypoxemic cause of cardiac arrest (e.g., drowning), and pediatric cardiac arrest
  + Role of assisted ventilation in first 3-5 min for adults is questionable – this is because desaturation doesn’t begin until 3 min have elapsed
  + Likely more important after 3-5 min of apnea – best evidence is for quality compressions, but including BVM is standard of care in hospital
  + Coordinate BVM with CPR – this allows rescuers to assure that chest rise is present, and increases likelihood that BVM will be effective (chest compressions raise resistance and make it more likely air will leak out of mask rather than entering patient’s lungs)
* When to stop CPR
  + In the hospital, ONLY stop CPR if patient has clear signs of life (moving, talking, or perfusing rhythm with palpable pulse)
  + The code team may stop CPR if care is deemed futile, but this would NEVER be done before code team arrival
