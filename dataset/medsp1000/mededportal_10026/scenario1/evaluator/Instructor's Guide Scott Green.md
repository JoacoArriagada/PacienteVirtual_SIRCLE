**TRAINING SESSION:**

**Standardized Patient Training Session:**

* A training session for the standardized patients (SPs) includes a thorough review of the case with time for question and answer about the details of the case. Our session lasted 30 minutes and was led by a faculty internist. The session can be facilitated by any faculty member who has committed to reviewing the background materials; he/she need not be a content expert on depression or cardiovascular disease.
* During the SP training session, the Beck depression inventory should be reviewed and the implications of the score addressed. SPs should be instructed to act as if they completed the inventory after a blank one was handed to them. In actuality, the SPs will be putting their SP name and date on a pre-completed inventory.
* If necessary, use the training session to provide brief feedback training to the SPs. Our SPs receive training on this upon hire and receive a refresher every 4-6 months. They are routinely asked to provide the students feedback on their professionalism and communication skills.

**Conceptual Background**

Depression is an under-recognized medical condition after a patient experiences a myocardial infarction (MI). Practitioners often mistakenly overlook depression as a “normal response” after MI. Evidence suggests that patients who are depressed after having an MI suffer worse outcomes. Specifically, depressed patients post-MI experience more social problems over the first year, are slower to return to work, and report more stress than their non-depressed counterparts. Additionally, depressed patients after MI are at increased risk for subsequent cardiac events, including re-infarction and re-hospitalization, compared with those without depression1,2.

This workshop was developed by clinician educators as part of a small group curriculum for pre-clinical medical students. This case was developed by an internal medicine physician and vetted through a faculty psychiatrist. The session is intended to be used with pre-clinical or early clinical learners to give students practice obtaining a chronic disease history while simultaneously recognizing and treating depression after a myocardial infarction. This case and session have been used to train approximately 360 medical students in recognizing depression in patients with cardiovascular disease.

This session was evaluated by faculty facilitators and students. During the 2012-14 academic years, 100% of facilitators (n=21) agreed or strongly agreed the case and session were appropriate for the level of learner. 95% of facilitators (20/21) agreed that the amount of content and time allocation was appropriate (1 facilitator was neutral on these domains.) Students were asked a single item to “Rate the overall quality of this teaching and learning method.” Polling a random subset of students in the 2012 and 2013 academic years, 89% (74/83) of students rated this session as good or excellent.

**Educational Objectives:**

**The learner will:**

1. In this workshop, utilize a patient-centered interviewing approach in a patient with chronic heart disease
2. In this workshop, practice the collection of a relevant history in a patient with chronic heart disease.
3. In this workshop, discuss the impact of disease on a patient’s psychological and behavioral functioning.
4. In this workshop, counsel a patient on the co-morbid conditions of depressed mood.

**Facilitator Teaching Notes**

**30 min Review of Patient information and Standardized Patient Interview**

This session will focus on students’ abilities to obtain a focused history, exam, and counseling for a patient who was recently hospitalized for a myocardial infarction. Students should be assigned to read “Depression and Coronary Heart Disease: Recommendations for Screening, Referral, and Treatment” *Circulation.* 2008;118: 1768-1775 as pre-work prior to coming to the session. An additional document “Depression and Coronary Heart Disease: AReview for Cardiologists” from Clin. Cardiol. 20, 196-200 (1997) may serve as a supplemental resource.

Prior to the standardized patient (“Scott Green”) arriving at the classroom, ask students to use the “Instructions for Learners “ sheet provided to review his past history. Have the student(s) interview the SP, taking a focused history. Feel free to stop the session at “teachable moments.” The students who are not interviewing should take notes on the interview.

Students are expected to take a focused history, getting the details of the patient’s hospitalization and current symptoms. It is reasonable to obtain a screening cardiac history (to determine if the patient has any symptoms of active cardiac disease.) After reviewing the history provided on “Instructions for Learners” sheet, ask students what they would ask regarding the history (prior to the SP entering the room.)

Use whatever method you prefer to have students obtain the history. The patient clearly has symptoms of depression. Students should use their knowledge from the recommended reading to get the appropriate history on this. If students do not fully explore the patient’s symptoms of depression, they will have another chance after you have further discussion.

Screening Cardiac History or common cardiac symptoms that should be assessed include the following:

* Chest pain, especially with exertion
* Palpitations
* Shortness of breath (dyspnea [at rest or with exertion], orthopnea, or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea)
* Edema

Before the SP is excused to the hall, gauge your students as to whether your students feel they have enough information regarding the patient’s mood. If not (and they have not already brought this up), offer a tool that can be used to help diagnose depression called the Beck’s Depression Inventory. It’s just one tool that can be used to help diagnosis depression. Although the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) was discussed in the assigned pre-reading, the Beck Inventory was discussed in the supplemental reading and we have chosen to use that scale. Do they feel it would be worthwhile to give to the patient? If the consensus is yes, hand out a blank copy to the SP and ask them to complete it while in the hall. (Also give students a blank copy so they can review it.) Of note, the SPs will be expecting this and will have an already-completed depression inventory that they will turn back into you if requested. Their score will put them in the clinically depressed range.

**20 min Developing an Assessment Negotiating a Treatment Plan**

While the SP is waiting in the hall, have the students develop an assessment as well as a treatment plan. Ask the students to identify the biggest concerns for this patient. Hopefully they will focus their discussion on the importance of participating in cardiac rehab and in diagnosing and treating his depression. To help students with this, spend a few minutes discussing the assigned article on the interplay of heart disease and depression.

Once you have reviewed the article (“Depression and Coronary Heart Disease: Recommendations for Screening, Referral, and Treatment), the students should come up with a treatment plan

We would like students to come up with points to discuss with the patient about their depression and the need to return to cardiac rehab. If the student presents a reasonable argument, the SP will agree to starting an anti-depressant and going back to cardiac rehab. They will also agree to come back for a follow- up visit in the future.

Sample Assessment and Plan

1. Major Depressive Disorder: Pt with loss of pleasure in activities and Beck’s Depression Inventory consistent with clinical depression. Recommend starting SSRI such as low-dose sertraline. Will likely need to titrate up and will see patient in 2 weeks to assess symptoms. Encouraged patient to talk about feelings and concerns with wife.

2. CAD s/p MI with 2 stents. Cont aspirin, clopidogrel, beta-blocker, and statin. Follow-up with cardiology. Strongly encouraged patient to resume cardiac rehab as this will be important to resume previous hobbies and life-style. Reassured patient that he is safe to resume light gardening and fishing but avoid any heavy lifting.

3. HTN: BP well controlled. Cont metoprolol and will work with cardiology to titrate as needed.

4. Hyperlipidemia (HLD): check hospital records for most recent LDL. Cont current Lipitor dose for now. Recheck lipid panel in 6-9 mo.

**Implementation**

This workshop may be modified for use by learners of various levels in training. For example, for earlier clinical learners, the focus could be shifted to obtaining the history only and the facilitator could demonstrate counseling the patient. This case could also be used to assess communication skills for more advanced learners.

This workshop requires training of standardized patients in order to consistently demonstrate the details of the case. Faculty facilitators should be experienced in small group facilitation. We would encourage the learners be offered feedback from faculty facilitators and the standardized patient regarding their patient care and communication skills.

We recommend that the session occur in a small group classroom or an environment that simulates a clinical setting. This entire session takes about 1 hour to complete. We recommend assigning the article as pre-work for the students.

**Limitations/Ideas for Improvement/Expansion**

This curriculum provides an active learning environment using a standardized patient to teach about depression in the setting of chronic cardiovascular disease. Our workshop was well-received by standardized patients and faculty facilitators who reported the case was realistic, relevant, and for SPs, easy to portray authentically. There are some limitations with this curriculum. First, this brief training occurs only once. We recommend that the learners have some basic knowledge about depression prior to this session. Second, the impact of this workshop is limited to feedback by students and facilitators. The student response item was a standard evaluation item for all teaching and learning methods in our curriculum; a more specific evaluation, such as student confidence in assessing for depression, may yield more robust evaluation data. Furthermore, additional assessment measures, such as an observed structured clinical encounter, would be needed to ensure students can integrate these concepts into patient care. We agree that more robust evaluation data would allow for improvement and validation of this learning tool. We do not currently have this data but are investigating ways to more fully evaluate these types of sessions.
