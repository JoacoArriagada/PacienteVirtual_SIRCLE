**Instructor’s Guide for OSCE Station: New HIV Diagnosis**

**Goal of resource:**

To assess the ability of internal medicine residents to disclose a new positive HIV test result, counsel the patient in an empathic manner, and select appropriate baseline laboratory tests.

**Educational Objectives**

* To assess the ability of a medical resident to disclose a new diagnosis of HIV to a patient in an empathic manner
* To assess the ability of a medical resident to integrate the emotional state of the patient into counseling regarding a new HIV diagnosis
* To assess the ability of a medical resident to take a focused history on a patient newly diagnosed with HIV
* To assess the ability of a medical resident to counsel regarding a new HIV diagnosis to a patient with limited medical knowledge
* To assess the ability of a medical resident to select the appropriate laboratory tests for a patient with a new diagnosis of HIV

**Resource Files:**

The document titled “HIV Diagnosis Complete Case.docx” contains the following materials:

1. **Cover Sheet** – provides the objectives and checklist of materials needed
2. **Examiner instructions**

* To be given to the faculty examiner in advance of the OSCE session
* Provides explicit instructions for the faculty examiner regarding examiner tasks, structure of the OSCE station, and time breakdown of the components of the OSCE station
* Provides background material on the topic with references
* Provides a synopsis of the OSCE case and resident tasks

1. **Resident Instructions**

* To be placed on the door of the exam room for the resident to read before starting the OSCE station. Another copy can be given to the resident for use during the OSCE station
* Describes the resident’s role and tasks during the OSCE station
* Provides limited background information about the standardized patient
* Includes time breakdown of the components of the OSCE station

1. **Standardized Patient Script**

* To be given to the standardized patient and standardized patient trainer prior to the OSCE session for training
* Provides detailed background information about the standardized patient’s medical, social, sexual, and family history
* Provides detailed guidance on how to respond emotionally to news of positive HIV test and what questions to ask of resident

The document titled “HIV Diagnosis Checklists.docx” contains the following materials:

1. **Examiner checklist**

* To be given to the faculty examiner prior to the OSCE, and completed during or immediately after the OSCE
* Evaluates history-taking and counseling skills by assessment of directly observable behaviors
* Evaluates clinical decision-making by assessing ability to select appropriate baseline laboratory tests for patient with newly diagnosed HIV
* For each row, examiner should check “Yes” box if the resident performed that task correctly and “No” if they did not. There is also a space for narrative comments.
* The total score is the overall number of checked “Yes” boxes, and percentage correct is the number of checked “Yes” boxes over 24. Subscores can also be determined by repeating this process separately for the History, Counseling, and Decision-Making sections

1. **Standardized Patient checklist**

* To be given to standardized patient prior to OSCE, and completed immediately after OSCE
* Uses the “University of Illinois at Chicago Communication and Personal Skills Scale” to assess communication and interpersonal skills of residents during OSCE interaction
* For each row, the standardized patient should check one box on the 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree.” There is also a space for narrative comments.
* This checklist is scored as 5 points for each “Strongly Agree,” 4 points for “Agree,” etc.
* The total score is the summed points for the 17 checklist items, and percentage correct is that number over a maximum possible score of 90.

**Conceptual Background**

Receiving a new diagnosis of HIV is a traumatic experience for patients, who commonly exhibit shock, denial, anger, or despair as initial reactions. Delivering this diagnosis in a sensitive and compassionate way, assessing and validating the emotional response of the patient, and evaluating for adequacy of the patient's social support network are important skills for any primary care clinician. Patients also have varying pre-existing conceptions of the natural history and treatment options for HIV, as well as wide-ranging concerns regarding how this diagnosis will affect their lives, all of which need to be factored into the initial discussions about their new diagnosis. An understanding of an appropriate initial laboratory work-up is also important to clarify the degree of immunosuppression, assess for viral resistance or comorbidities which could influence decisions on antiretroviral therapy, and evaluate for sexually transmitted or blood-borne co-infections, such as syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, or viral hepatitis.

Additionally, the task of relaying an HIV diagnosis is relevant to residents beyond internal medicine or providers involved in longitudinal care, given recent released screening guidelines and an incidence (albeit stable) of 50,000 new HIV infections each year.

**Development of Materials**

This OSCE station was designed as a component of a multi-station OSCE for residents in the HIV Primary Care Track in the Internal Medicine residency program at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. The OSCE was exclusively intended for formative feedback. The station itself was written by the primary author, an instructor in infectious diseases and general medicine, as part of a medical education fellowship. The topic was chosen to align with the overall curricular objectives for the HIV Primary Care track, which are to train residents in providing primary care to HIV and LGBT patients. All elements of the case were reviewed and revised in an iterative fashion by the leadership committee of this track, which includes specialists in HIV primary care and an educator/hospitalist.

We selected the "University of Illinois at Chicago Communication and Personal Skills Scale" for the SP instrument upon review of multiple checklists to assess communication skills. This published and validated instrument by Dr. Rachel Yudkowski et al. was developed using 79 internal and family medicine residents participating in an OSCE designed to assess communication and interpersonal skills. The authors conducted reliability and validity testing, including factor analysis, and the scale was found to have a medial internal consistency (coefficient alpha) of 0.91.

**Implementation**

Training of the SP, lasting about four hours, was conducted by SP educators at the University of Massachusetts Standardized Patient Program.

The station was piloted with a faculty examiner and standardized patient, using a medical resident not involved in the HIV primary care track. This provided a good opportunity to clarify any confusion about the station, trouble-shoot any logistical problems, and fine-tune any aspects of the standardized patient performance.

Prior to implementing this OSCE station, the faculty examiner and standardized patient received their materials, each receiving opportunities to discuss the case, roles/functions of each person, general guidance on how to give effective feedback and how to complete their respective checklists. Specifically for faculty members, we provided a training session wherein we reviewed principles of effective feedback and advised faculty examiners to identify and discuss with the resident two or three aspects of the case in which they performed well, and two or three areas for improvement. We have adopted this stance based on our experiences that learners may have difficulty absorbing feedback on more than a small number of important points.

The cost of running this single case within the context of an 8-case OSCE was estimated at about $1800, which included 4 hours of SP training, SP educator time, fixed administrative costs (independent of the number of cases), travel, piloting and actual implementation.

Of note, this OSCE station also includes a faculty examiner physically present in the room as part of the interaction due to the fact that our SPs did not possess the clinical background knowledge of HIV care to give effective feedback on content issues. Furthermore, we held the OSCE in situ in actual clinic rooms to convey authenticity, without the infrastructure necessary for synchronous or asynchronous faculty observation. Variations on the case may entail the SP exclusively providing feedback on some components of the checklist, with faculty review of a videotape of the encounter or through a one-way mirror, as physical infrastructure permits.

Disclosure of positive HIV result and counseling: 14 minutes

**Total time:** 25 minutes

**Results**

Three interns in the HIV primary care track participated in this OSCE station. Results on the checklists are shown below:

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Faculty checklist** | Average | Range |
| History (4 total points) | 3 | 2-4 |
| Counseling (12 total points) | 9.3 | 8-10 |
| Decision-making (8 total points) | 7 | 6-8 |
| Total (24 total points) | 16.3 | 14-18 |

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Communications checklist** | Average | Range |
| Patient Centered Communication (40 total points) | 38 | 37-40 |
| Interpersonal Skills (45 total points) | 43.3 | 40-45 |
| Global rating (5 total points) | 4 | 3-5 |
| Total (90 total points) | 85.3 | 80-90 |

The length of time allotted for the case as a whole and for each task was appropriate. We actually found that the amount of time allotted for the case portrayal was more than sufficient, which allowed the time and space for more in-depth feedback to be provided by either or both the SP and the faculty member, if needed.

Focus groups conducted immediately after the OSCE and 2 months afterwards demonstrated that residents felt that the case was very realistic, including the dramatic emotional reaction of the SP. The participants also felt that the case was central to the skills expected of an HIV primary care provider. Overall, the residents enjoyed the OSCE as a formative experience.

Upon review of our results, the residents did relatively poorly on a) providing an initial warning statement prior to disclosure of HIV results, and b) assessing baseline understanding of HIV, which likely speaks to their inexperience in giving bad news. However, we found that the interns participating in this station were more successful with the less emotionally charged aspects of the case (i.e., history-taking, discussing HIV transmission and natural history, discussing laboratory work-up).

The OSCE represents a source of data for program evaluation on the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center HIV Primary Care Track as a whole.

**Limitations and Potential Challenges**

We summarize the potential challenges that may be encountered by faculty aspiring to use our case below.

*Insufficient support for use for summative purposes*

* This case was designed for formative feedback due to the pilot nature of our program.
* The sample size for implementation, given the small number of residents able to participate in the HIV primary care track, did not make it possible for us to determine interrater reliability in a meaningful way.
* Future directions including using standard-setting procedures could help establish competency guidelines in this field. Furthermore, if all residents in the program similarly undergo an OSCE, the checklist could potentially be mapped to internal medicine milestones and could be used in competency decisions.

*Lack of strict objectivity in assessing communication skills*

* Assessment of communication skills is subject to rater variability, however we attempted to make our checklists based as much as possible on observable behaviors.
* Furthermore, the faculty members relied upon their own extensive experiences in relaying HIV diagnoses as internal reference standards for feedback and checklist completion. We recognize that lack of standardization in constructs such as empathy and sensitivity may also contribute to variability.

*Issues related to the educational environment*

* We implemented our OSCE station with a specific cohort of residents with special expertise and interest in HIV medicine. While the task of giving bad news is generalizable to all physician trainees, the specific HIV knowledge elements may be higher than that expected of general internal medicine residents.

*Potential untoward psychological impact on participant learners and SPs*

* The case entails the SP to portray dramatic emotions that may evoke psychological stress and burnout in the SP upon repeated administration. This impact was somewhat mitigated by the low number of residents participating. We recommend periodic breaks for the SP, both between cases and more extended breaks in the middle of the day, and the avoidance of the same SPs performing this case on consecutive days.

*Resource requirements*

* The cost may be prohibitive to other institutions if an SP center is not readily available or there are insufficient funds to hire SPs. We would suggest the possibility of using either medical assistants, patient advocates or activists, or even actual patients from the population as potential substitutes for professional SPs. This less standardized approach would of course necessitate the use of this case for formative (not summative) purposes and a higher level of vigilance for the psychological health of learners as well as the patient actors.
* Time needed for training may differ among SP centers and thus our costs may not reflect other institutions’ experiences.

**Conclusions**

This station was successfully deployed as part of an end-of-year four-station OSCE for the purposes of formative assessment for medical resident participants in the HIV Primary Care Track at our institution. Also, while variable emotional reactions to a new HIV diagnosis, medical and psychiatric comorbidities, and differences in baseline medical understanding all affect the process of counseling a newly diagnosed HIV-infected patient, this station allows only one of many possible clinical interactions. While we focused on other aspects of our curriculum for our remaining OSCE stations, one could consider similar stations examining disclosing HIV results to a patient with serious psychiatric disease, an abusive partner, or complicating medical diagnoses. Furthermore, this case could be a template for a “giving bad news” station for a range of other clinical scenarios.

While we integrated this station into a larger 4-station OSCE, this case could be used as a stand-alone teaching station or simply as a role-playing exercise within an ambulatory teaching session. The fact that this does not have to be used for evaluative purposes extends the potential possibilities for its application.

This resource would be a contribution to current scholarship as there are no other published SP cases for HIV in MedEdPORTAL and a search in MedLINE revealed no publications describing the use of SP cases addressing HIV.
