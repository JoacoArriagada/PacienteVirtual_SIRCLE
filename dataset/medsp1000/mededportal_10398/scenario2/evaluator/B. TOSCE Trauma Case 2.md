# TOSCE CASE #2

**INFORMATION FOR FACILITATOR**

### Purpose of Case: Communicating Uncertain Prognosis After Acute Traumatic Injury

**Training Level**: Resident

**Simulated patient name**: Mrs. Jones

**Diagnosis:** Severe head trauma due to fall down stairs

**Setting**: Quiet room for family meetings in an ED

**Time allotted**: 7 minutes, plus 4 minutes for self-assessment and feedback

**BACKGROUND INFORMATION FOR RESIDENTS**

You are the trauma resident on call at University Hospital. You are asked to evaluate an 88 year-old female who was brought in after a fall. The patient, Mrs. Jones was found by her daughter at the bottom of the stairs, minimally responsive. The patient has atrial fibrillation and is on Coumadin (Warfarin). She required intubation in the field after becoming obtunded.

On your initial assessment, this is a frail elderly woman, ABC’s intact, GCS 6. She has a non-expanding hematoma on her scalp. Initial blood work reveals an INR of 7, with a normal blood gas. On CT scan she has a large subdural hematoma with a midline shift. This is likely an operative bleed, pending neurosurgical consultation. The functional outcome is unknown and the prognosis is likely poor. There is a chance that she might die.

The nurse informs you that that family is in the waiting room and would like to an update on the patient’s condition.

**TASK**:

1. Disclose information about the patient’s condition
2. Respond to questions and concerns that the family may have.

**TIME FOR TASK:**

* You have 7 minutes for the encounter (a warning will sound when 2 minutes remain).
* At the end of the encounter
  + Fill out your self-assessment and wait for feedback from the standardized family member.
  + You have 4 minutes to receive feedback from the standardized family member.

**Delivering Bad News: Uncertain Prognosis**

**STANDARDIZED FAMILY MEMBER SCRIPT**

**Training Level (Intended Audience):** General Surgery & Emergency Medicine residents

**Simulated patient name:** Mrs. Jones (Patient) /Ms. Jones (Daughter)

**Event:** Fall down stairs

**Setting:**  Quiet exam room in the emergency department

**Synopsis**: The purpose of this encounter is to deliver difficult news related to an uncertain prognosis after an acute traumatic event. The resident may attempt to obtain as much essential information about your mother, Andrea Jones, but will for the most part inform or communicate news of your mother’s uncertain and poor prognosis. The residents have been educated on the proper manner to deliver bad news (see appendix). This activity is intended to test the resident’s communication skills.

**Appearance and Behavior**: You are worried and teary eyed. You are dressed in jeans and a shirt with no make-up. You had discovered your mother on the bottom of the stairs moaning in pain and barely conscious. You had called 911. Your mother was still awake and moaning when the ambulance arrived. She was rushed to University Hospital. You were given NO new information about your mother’s condition when you arrived at the hospital and were directed to wait in one of the more private exam rooms. You were told that someone will be in to speak with you shortly. You’ve been waiting over an hour. You rise from your chair as soon as the resident walks into the room. Please maintain eye contact with the resident as they deliver the news.

**Opening Statement:** Prompt: You approach the resident as soon as they walk into the room and say: “How is she?” (Do not disclose your relationship to the patient *unless they inquire*).

**Information FREELY given:** You came in early this morning to check on your mom, who lives next door to you. You found her at the bottom of the steps on the floor, moaning in pain, with a bump on her head. You called 911. EMS arrived and took your mom away in an ambulance. You followed the ambulance to the hospital. No other information was disclosed. You are in a state of panic.

**Information provided ONLY when prompted**:

There are a number of ways that this encounter can begin. The ideal:

Setting up the interview:

* The residents must first greet and introduce themselves to you they enter the room.
* They must inquire about your relationship to the patient before they disclose information.

State your relationship to Mrs. Jones (“I am Mrs. Jones’s daughter, Joan.”)

State your name (“I am Joan Andrews.”) when asked. If they do not inquire about your identity repeat your opening statement… “How is she?”

* The resident should make an attempt to make you comfortable. For example, they may ask you if you wish to have anyone present in the room during the encounter (a nurse, a friend, a family member). They may ask you to sit down or may connect with you (maintain eye contact) by leaning towards you or with a soft touch to shoulder.
* The resident should ask you, “What were you told about…or what do you know about your mother’s condition?”

Note: You are anxiously looking forward to hearing what the resident has to say.

Your behavior is expectant and hopeful. You may state that your mom has a heart problem – Atrial fibrillation and that she is taking Coumadin. As far as you know she is compliant with her medication.

Conveying of information:

* Resident should offer a ‘warning shot’ before they deliver bad news – for example:
  + “I’m afraid that I have bad news to share with you.”
  + “I’m sorry to tell you that…”

Wait to hear what the resident has to say. If the resident says nothing, prompt the conversation along, show impatience and irritation… (Sample prompting statement… “This is too much…what is going on?” … “No one’s telling me anything new!” )

* Residents should provide information on the patient’s current condition, for example, what steps have been taken to determine the extent of your mom’s injury. They should inform you about the next steps in management (what to expect over the next few hours, days etc.). Resident should provide some statements that reflect a prognosis (what will happen to patient next…, will she live…, is she expected to die…will there be a disability…?) using simple language and in a manner that is unhurried.
  + - They should use little or no medical jargon.
    - If medical terminology is used by resident, it should promptly be followed by a simple non-technical explanation.
    - Resident should allow you the opportunity to express concerns and asks questions about your mom.

Note: If the resident does not define the medical terms or jargon, stop and inquire about the terminology used.

Allowing you to express emotions:

Emotions that you may want to express include grief, denial and shock.

Trainer’s notes: This case may cycle through the group a second time. In order to provide variations to this case express *grief* during the first encounter and then *grief, denial and* *anger* during the second encounter so as to provide the resident with a slightly different experience. You may also vary your appearance in the second run through the case, for example, during the first encounter appear tearful and upset with a sad look followed by silence and on the second encounter appear shocked, upset and in denial of the situation. You may also interrupt with repeating of phrases such as, “I do not believe this…”

* + To add challenge to the second encounter, you may also be aggressive or insulting to the resident using a statement like, “Oh honey, you’re so young, you don’t know what you’re talking about,” “where is the real doctor,” or use statements that show that you are NOT grasping the seriousness of her status such as, “Well, at least she’ll get to rest now. I’ve been telling her she spends too much time on her feet.”
  + Please pay close attention to whether the resident recognizes and validates your emotions during the encounter.

During the encounter the resident should be fully attentive to you – they should not respond to distractions and you should note that there are no impediments or barriers to conversation. The resident should be able to express or utilize empathetic statement to connect with you. Some relevant behaviors to observe include:

* + Facilitated behavior – body language, facial expression and eye contact
  + Establishing a rapport with you– an overall supportive, sympathetic, thoughtful or considerate and kind demeanor as well as appropriate moments of silence is important.

Addressing prognosis, Next steps, Response to Concerns:

Some questions and comments you may want to pose (or any you think are appropriate to the situation) include the following:

* Is she going to live?
* Will she need an operation?
* My dad had a head bleed too. After his stroke, he was paralyzed and ended up in a nursing home, will that happen to my mom too? She is very independent.
* When will she be coming home?
* When can I talk to her?
* Will she be able to walk again?
* How serious are her injuries?
* Will she wake up?
* When will you know more? Is there some sort of a test you can do?
* What am I supposed to tell the children?
* When can I see my mother

Note: There may be some questions that the resident may not be able to answer. The resident should be open and honest it they are unable to provide answers to some of your questions. They should inquire if you have any other concerns or questions for them before they leave the room.

Your Mother was very capable of managing her own affairs. She had no issues carrying out her activities of daily living. She is healthy except for her atrial fibrillation for which she takes a blood thinner, Coumadin. She has never really directly discussed goals of care or advanced directives with you, but she has told you that that she would want everything done to keep her alive if there was any chance of recovery.

# FAMILY MEMBER PROFILE

# MEDICAL HISTORY

Your mother, Mrs. Jones, is an 88 year old widow with a history of atrial fibrillation and is on Coumadin. This morning at 7 am you found your mother at the bottom of the stairs with a bump on her head, moaning in pain. She was brought to the State Level-1 trauma center. You have driven to the trauma center and have been waiting in the waiting room for about an hour. You have not met the doctor yet. You did see a nurse who said "your mother is having some problems. The doctors are with her and will be out to talk with you soon".

# SOCIAL HISTORY

You have three children, ages 10, 14 and 17. Your father is deceased. He died after complications of a stroke. You have no siblings, and are divorced. You work as an administrative assistant.

**SETTING**

You are alone, in one of the more private exam rooms in the ED. You are seated on a chair.

**TASK**

The surgery or emergency medicine resident, in the role of the ED physician or trauma surgeon, will break the bad news of your mother’s head injury and describe her current status to you.
