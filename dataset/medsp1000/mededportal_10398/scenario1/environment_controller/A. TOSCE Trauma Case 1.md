**TOSCE CASE #1**

**INFORMATION FOR FACILITATOR**

**Purpose of Case: Communicating News of Death After Acute Traumatic Injury**

**Training Level:**  Resident

**Simulated patient name:** Mr. Stone

**Diagnosis:** Blunt trauma, Motor Vehicle Collision

**Setting:** Quiet room for family meetings in an ED

**BACKGROUND INFORMATION FOR RESIDENTS**

You are the trauma resident on call at University Hospital. You are called to evaluate a 54 year-old male status post motor vehicle collision. EMS reports the patient, Mr. Stone, was an unrestrained driver with a prolonged extrication with severe front-end damage to the vehicle. In the field, he was obtunded with a blown right pupil.

On your initial assessment, patient had a GCS 7 and was intubated. A 32 French chest tube was placed on the left side with 475 mL of blood return. A chest x-ray confirmed proper placement of the tubes, with no other pathology noted. Then, the patient became hypertensive and bradycardic with fixed and dilated pupils, GCS 3. The patient lost pulses and ACLS protocol was initiated. After 20 minutes of CPR, the patient was pronounced dead.

The nurse informs you that the patient’s family is waiting to speak with you and are unaware of the patient’s status.

You do not have any other information about this patient nor his family.

**TASK**: Disclose information about the patient’s death.

**Delivering Bad News: Death**

**STANDARDIZED FAMILY SCRIPT**

**Simulated patient name:** Mr. Peter Stone (Patient) /Mrs. Jessica Stone (Wife)

**Event:** Motor vehicle collision

**Setting:**  Quiet exam room in the emergency department

**Synopsis**:

The purpose of this session is for the resident to deliver bad news of death. The resident is not expected to obtain a detailed history about your husband Peter or you, but to communicate news of Peter’s death.

**Appearance and Behavior**:

You are anxious, worried and jumpy. You are dressed in jeans and a shirt and have no make-up since you dressed hurriedly when you heard that Peter had been in a car accident. You have NO new information about your husband’s condition, only that he was in an accident and that everything possible is being done for him. You were directed to one of the more private exam rooms and assured that someone will be in to speak with you. You are pacing the room when the resident enters. You maintain eye contact with the resident as they deliver the news.

**Opening Statement:**

Prompt: You approach the resident as soon as they walk into the room and say: “How is he?” (Do not disclose your relationship to the patient *unless they inquire*).

**Information FREELY given:** Peter was on his way to work this morning. You had taken the day off from work so you can pack for your trip to the Poconos. You received a call 1 hour ago from the police station informing you that your husband had been in a car accident and that he was rushed to University Hospital. No other information was disclosed and you were encouraged to proceed to the hospital at once. You are in a state of panic.

At the hospital, a nurse informed you that, “Your husband is in the trauma bay and that the doctor will be out to speak with you soon.” It is 8:15 am.

**Information provided ONLY when prompted**:

There are a number of ways that this encounter can begin. The ideal encounter would involve the following:

Setting up the interview with you:

Only disclose your relationship to Peter when asked (“I am Peter’s wife, Jessica.”)

State your name (“I am Jessica Stone.”) when asked. If identity is not asked, repeat your opening statement… “How is he?”

If asked what you were told or what you know about your husband’s condition, answer using the information above.

Note: In this phase, you are anxiously looking forward to hearing what the resident has to say. Your behavior is expectant and hopeful.

Conveying of information and difficult news to you:

Wait to hear what the resident has to say. If the resident says nothing, you may prompt the conversation along or you may show impatience and/or irritation… (Sample prompting statement… “This is too much…what is going on?” … “No one’s telling me anything new!”)

If medical terminology is used without a simple explanation, interrupt and inquire about the terminology.

Allowing you to express your emotions:

Emotions to express include grief, denial, shock and anger.

Trainer’s notes: This case may cycle through the group a second time. In order to provide variations to this case express *grief* during the first encounter and then *grief, denial and* *anger* during the second encounter so as to provide the resident with a slightly different experience. You may also vary your appearance in the second run through the case, for example, during the first encounter appear tearful and upset with a sad look followed by silence and on the second encounter appear initially shocked and in denial and then angry with the situation. You may also interrupt the resident with repeating of phrases such as, “I do not believe this…, I do not believe this…”)

Some questions and comments you may want to pose (or any others you think are appropriate to the situation) include the following:

* + “How can this be? This was his last day at work before our big vacation with the kids. We were leaving for the Poconos tomorrow morning you know… (in disbelief)”
  + “What am I supposed to tell the children?”

If next steps for viewing the deceased are not discussed, you may prompt as, “When can I see my husband?” or “What happens next?”

There may be some questions that the resident cannot answer. It is ok for the resident to say “I don’t know.”

**STANDARDIZED FAMILY MEMBER PROFILE**

**MEDICAL HISTORY**

You are Jessica Stone, your spouse, Mr. Peter Stone, is 54 years old. He is a high school teacher with no history of any medical problems. You have been married for 27 years.

**SOCIAL HISTORY**

You have three children: ages 25, 22, and 17 years. Your parents are deceased; you have no siblings. You work for the county library.
