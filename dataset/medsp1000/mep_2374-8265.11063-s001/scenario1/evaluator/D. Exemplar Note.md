**Appendix D: Exemplar Note**

Frieda Baker is a 65-year-old woman with a history of well controlled (intermittent) asthma who presents to the ED with a complaint of worsening difficulty breathing and chest tightness. She has had asthma since childhood and her symptoms are typically well controlled with occasional use of her albuterol inhaler (2 puffs, once or twice a month). Triggers have included respiratory infections, and air pollution. She now reports 2 weeks of worsening episodes of difficulty breathing, noting that she “can’t get air into her lungs”. These episodes are associated with wheezing and diffuse chest tightness as well as with a dry cough. In the past 2 weeks, she has required increased use of her albuterol inhaler, 2 puffs up to 6 times daily including at night. Her inhaler provides relief but for shorter periods. She has started waking from sleep 2-3 times per week in the past two weeks. The dry cough occurs frequently during the day and at night. She denies fever or chills. Her symptoms are worse when she goes outside, noting that the air is very smoky from the wildfires that have been burning for several weeks, about 200 miles from her home. She is an avid gardener. She has noted these symptoms, but not as severe, in the past when the air quality has not been as bad. However, this year, she cannot tolerate being outdoors, even while wearing a “dust mask” that she purchased from the pharmacy. She remains indoors most of the day with the air-conditioner on, and this helps her symptoms. She is a former smoker but quit 28 years ago. Her son brought her to the ED due to concerns about her wheezing and cough and her report that her inhaler was almost empty.

Ms Baker expresses concern that she will be unable to function as she has in the past. She is newly retired and is upset at being housebound and reliant on others. She is worried that she will need more costly medications to control her breathing, that the air quality will damage her lungs permanently and notes that she cannot afford to evacuate or relocate. She is a widow and has had to ask neighbors for help getting groceries.

**Summary Statement**

65-year-old woman with long standing well controlled (intermittent) asthma and remote history of tobacco use, reports new onset of worsening dry cough, wheezing and chest tightness with symptoms occurring at night over the past 2 weeks. She reports increased use of her inhaler. Her symptoms are associated with outdoor activity and exposure to significant air pollution related to nearby wildfires.

**Diagnosis:** Asthma exacerbation due to air pollution related to wildfire- known history of intermittent asthma, and sensitivity to environmental smoke. No chest pain, no recent tobacco use, no fever or chills, and nonproductive cough. Symptoms are worse when outdoors and exposed to smoke from wildfires which have been more intense and longer lasting than in the past. Higher amounts of particulate in the atmosphere and sustained smoke in air are provoking her symptoms. She gets some relief with avoiding outside air and the use of an albuterol inhaler. Physical exam reveals wheezing and diminished peak flow from baseline.

**DDX**

* Bronchitis: remote history of tobacco but no fever or significant sputum production.
* COPD: remote history of tobacco but no significant sputum production. Baseline spirometry not consistent with COPD (FEV1 >80%, FEV1/FVC ratio > 70%).
* Pneumonia: no fever, chills, improves indoors and with inhaler, no significant sputum production

**PLAN**

**Immediate Care**

* Immediate treatment with short acting beta agonist (SABA)
* Administer first dose of corticosteroids
* Monitor peak flow and clinical status to determine disposition
* Consider CXR to evaluate for signs of pneumonia or infectious process
* Assess patient’s mental health in the context of anxiety over progression of her disease, isolation, dependence on others and financial limitations

**Upon discharge**

* Add short course of oral steroids
* Add inhaled steroid
* Review inhaler technique to confirm correct use

**Patient Education**

* Educate on use of peak flow meter and determine an Asthma Action Plan
* Discuss avoidance of exertion and reduction of outdoor activity such as gardening on poor air quality days
* Recognize that these environmental events which have been in existence are now more intense and will be a recurrent problem
* Monitor daily Air Quality Index, outdoor avoidance if >100
* Direct patient to an inexpensive mask that will filter pollen and particulates. Need N-95 non-medical grade masks which are available at hardware and home improvement stores or high-quality filters for home HVAC which can filter damaging fine particles Need to assure fit.
* Advise patient that although environmental conditions are more severe, they are seasonal, so outdoor activity restrictions are time bound. Ongoing monitoring of air quality will be required.
* Recognize emotional distress provoked by wildfire and climate conditions and provide a referral for social work and community services to address medication needs, grocery delivery and emotional support through behavioral health providers or support groups
* Provide contact for the national Disaster Distress Helpline, which provides counseling for people experiencing emotional distress related to human caused or natural disasters.
