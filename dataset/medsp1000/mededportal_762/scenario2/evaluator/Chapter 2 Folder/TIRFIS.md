Tobacco Intervention Risk Factor Interview Scale

A.	RELATIONSHIP

<table><tr><td><p>Item 1 -- Confidence</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor explicitly conveys with confidence and certainty that risk factor change is (a) vital to health maintenance and (b) can be successfully accomplished leading to improved health and well-being.  That is, the doctor is optimistic in delivering the message that change is needed and can be effectively achieved: an it’s important . . . you can do it attitude.</p><p>Item 2 -- Joining</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor phrases recommendations and guidance for risk factor change in a non-prescriptive and collaborative manner.  There is both an explicit and implicit understanding that the task of risk factor change is shared, i.e., We’ll work together, with the doctor supporting and guiding the patient.  The doctor responds to patient resistance by reiterating the collaborative alliance.</p><p>Item 3 -- Empathy</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor explicitly states an understanding that risk factor/lifestyle change poses a strenuous challenge to the patient, e.g., I understand it is very difficult to quit smoking (using smokeless tobacco). Statements such as this may be spontaneously offered by the doctor at any point during the interview, or mentioned to confirm the patient’s report of past/current effort.</p></td><td><p>	(4)</p><p>	(4)</p><p>	(4)</p></td><td><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor acknowledges the need for risk factors change, but does not explicitly state any optimism for successfully achieving risk factor change.  The doctor sounds equivocal when discussing the importance or likely success of behavior change.</p><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor implies or weakly acknowledges the collaborative effort of risk factor change.  The doctor indicates some willingness to work with the patient.</p><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor implies but does not explicitly state an awareness of the difficulty of risk factor modification, e.g., the doctor says, “uh huh” or “good” to acknowledge the reported effort, but offers no further elaboration.  The patient can infer some weak understanding in the doctor.</p></td><td><p>	(2)</p><p>	(2)</p><p>	(2)</p></td><td><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor fails to challenge, respond to, or correct the patient’s ambivalence re: risk factor change; the doctor does not convey the need for change nor indicate its possibility of success.  The patient is left without any clear indication of what opinion the doctor holds.</p><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor conveys no indication of the shared responsibility in risk factor change.  Rather, the patient is made to feel totally responsible and unsupported in the change process.  The patient has limited opportunity to respond to the doctors’ prescriptions or directives.</p><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor fails to acknowledge the challenge and effort of risk factor modification.  Recommendations are phrased without any indication of the inherent difficulty with behavior change.  The doctor conveys a feeling of criticism to the patient.</p></td></tr></table>

Risk Factor Interview Scale

B.	POSITIVE FOCUS

<table><tr><td><p>Item 4 -- Reinforce Effort</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor strongly and verbally praises the patient for reported efforts to initiate or achieve behavior change.  Additionally, the doctor reinforces the patient for reaching any positive decision to initiate change following the interview.  Thus, past, present, and future efforts to change receive verbal reinforcement.  Examples include:  “It’s really great that you’ve stopped smoking (using smokeless tobacco).”  “You’ve made a difficult but important decision today to try and stop smoking (using smokeless tobacco).”</p><p>Item 5 -- Highlight Positive Consequences</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor provides comments, questions, or advice which explicitly highlight both long-term (i.e., C.D.) and immediate positive consequences of change in lifestyle.  An example of the long-term consequences is: “If you quit smoking you really can improve your health.” Examples of the immediate positive consequences are:  “People notice the good taste of food when they quit smoking.”  Or, more generally, “People notice a lot of positive things when they quit using tobacco . . .”</p><p>Item 6 -- Reframing Failure</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor responds to the patient’s report of failure of fear of failure by offering a positive interpretation.  For example, when the patient says, “I really blew it,” the doctor may respond by elucidating some degree of success, e.g., “I’m not so sure.  How did you quit smoking (using smokeless tobacco) for a whole week?”  Or, “What can you learn from that experience?” Additionally, examples of previously successful change are transposed to the present in order to identify strengths that apply to the current task of change.  For example: “You stopped smoking (using smokeless tobacco) before, how about you try doing what you did in the past to stop now.”</p></td><td><p>	(4)</p><p>	(4)</p><p>	(4)</p></td><td><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor weakly praises the patient, only for successful change in the past.  No significant reinforcement is offered for past efforts that weren’t durable.</p><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor implicitly or weakly acknowledges the positive consequences of lifestyle change.  But, only long-term positive consequences (i.e., altered disease outcome) are mentioned.  No mention is made of the immediate benefits of change.</p><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor acknowledges the feelings of failure expressed or implied by the patient, but without explicitly reframing failure as a positive or learning experience that is relevant to the present task.</p></td><td><p>	(2)</p><p>	(2)</p><p>	(2)</p></td><td><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor fails to provide any reinforcement or positive feedback to the patient for past or present behavior change.  Instead, the doctor’s response to reported efforts and past successes appears unresponsive or noncommittal.</p><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor fails to note or acknowledge any positive consequences for lifestyle change.  Instead of discussing positive consequences, the doctor highlights avoiding a negative, e.g., “You won’t have a heart attack” or uses other scare tactics.</p><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor ignores or confirms the patient’s feeling of failure.  No effort is made to reinterpret this feeling or past experience.  Similarly, no connection is drawn between past successes and present tasks of change.</p></td></tr></table>

C.	INSTIGATING BEHAVIOR CHANGE

<table><tr><td><p>Item 7 -- Stages of Change*</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor inquires about a patient’s willingness to quit and uses this information to tailor counseling and negotiate a plan.  Through open-ended questioning, the doctor elicits from the patient a strategy for pursuing specific behavior change.  Thus, the doctor may ask, “What do you feel ready to do about your tobacco use . .” .  Vague intentions such as “I’m going to cut down . . . I’m going to think about quitting” are followed up with probing questions.  Thus, the patient may eventually say, “I’ll try not to use tobacco while driving . . . I’ll start to carry chewing gum . . .”</p><p>Item 8 -- Nicotine Dependence*</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor specifically asks nicotine dependence questions, and offers nicotine replacement.</p><p>Item 9 -- Behavior Change Technique</p><p>	(5)</p><p>The doctor introduces and explicitly employs at least 2 of the following behavior change techniques: (a) self-monitoring, (b) contingency contracting/self-reinforcement, or (c) stimulus/cue-control.  For example, the doctor may assign a self-monitoring task, help the patient establish a self-reinforcement plan, or assist the patient to identify and alter cue-eliciting stimuli.  The doctor helps patient set a timely quit date (for the active quitter) and/or follow-up date.</p></td><td><p>	(4)</p><p>	(4)</p><p>	(4)</p></td><td><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor inquires about a patient’s willingness to quit, but does not use this information correctly to tailor counseling or negotiating a plan.  In a closed-ended manner, the doctor initially prescribes, chooses, or otherwise explicitly selects for the patient a strategy for pursuing change.  The patient, in turn, may react with resistance to which the doctor responds by becoming more open-ended.  Thus, some degree of negotiation occurs, but it appears mildly adversarial.</p><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor asks incompletely about nicotine dependence, or prescribes nicotine replacement therapy when not necessary or wanted.</p><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor utilizes written materials, brochures, or prepared guides as a method of instigating behavior change.  Alternatively, an explicit referral is made, accomplished by minimal elaboration.  Quit date (for active quitter) and/or follow-up are not set in timely fashion, or are set at inappropriate times (such as times of stress).</p></td><td><p>	(2)</p><p>	(2)</p><p>	(2)</p></td><td><p>	(1)</p><p>No determination is made about a patient’s willingness to quit.  Behavior change is not discussed, leaving the patient unsure where to begin change.  Alternatively, the doctor prescribes the strategy for change and remains essentially unresponsive to the patient’s choice.  The patient and doctor may engage in an adversarial and oppositional discussion of what change Ashould@ occur.  The doctor displays a limited willingness to discuss alternative choices.</p><p>	(1)</p><p>The doctor does not ask nicotine addiction questions, or does not offer nicotine replacement when useful or indicated; or, the doctor offers nicotine replacement when contraindicated.</p><p>	(1)</p><p>Neither written nor systematic behavior change techniques are presented to the patient.  Instead, the doctor’s advice seems only informational.  No mention is made by the doctor of quit date (for active quitter) and/or follow-up date.</p></td></tr></table>

This structure is followed throughout the interview.</p></td><td><p>	(4)</p></td><td><p>	(3)</p><p>The doctor seems to follow systematically a series of topics or agenda items.
