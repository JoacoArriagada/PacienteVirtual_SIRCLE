# Disclosure of a Complication: Pulmonary Emboli

RESIDENT CASE NOTES

# THE PATIENT

Henry Bass is a 75-year male with colon cancer. Ten days ago he was admitted to the SICU with respiratory failure, intra-abdominal sepsis, and failure to thrive after Hartman’s procedure for a perforated color cancer. He has been in the ICU for 10 days, and you have been taking care of him for the last three days. You were able to extubate Mr. Bass three days ago, but he still requires BIPAP and NT suctioning for thick secretions and a poor cough. He has also had a low grade fever for which you haven’t found a cause. Last night, in addition to the fever, Mr. Bass became acutely hypoxic and hypotensive and required intubation and ventilator support. A CT angio of the chest shows clear, bilateral pulmonary emboli. In retrospect you discover that although he has had compression devices on his legs, you forgot to prescribe an anticoagulant. His hypotension is resolved but he still has a higher FIO2 requirement than he did previously and will likely spend several days on the ventilator.

# MS. ARLENE COHEN (SISTER

Ms. Arlene Cohen and Joanne Bass (Henry’s daughter) are the patient’s only surviving family members. Henry has been a widower for many years and Joanne lives on the west coast; she only recently came into town to see her Dad. Ms. Cohen is the patient’s main caretaker. She has been visiting Henry every day in the hospital and obviously feels quite responsible for him. You have been talking to her for the last three days. It was Ms. Cohen whom you called last night to tell about the PE and reintubation.

# MS. JOANNE BASS (DAUGHTER)

You’ve seen the daughter once or twice, but have never spoken with her.

# YOUR OBJECTIVES FOR THIS ENCOUNTER ARE TO:

Explain the complication and its impact on the patient in understandable, nonmedical terms   
 Apologize, and to be forthright and honest in taking responsibility for the medication omission (despite the recognition that it may not have prevented the PE) Actively listen, assess the family’s understanding   
 Respond appropriately (non-defensively) to family emotions Explain how complications of this nature are avoided, and what you might do differently next time   
 Engage family support for the revised treatment plan Ensure continuity of communication and care

# THE PATIENT

Henry Bass is a 75 –year old man who has colon cancer. Ten days ago he had his colon removed. Following the surgery, Henry developed an infection in his abdomen as well as significant breathing problems that required a breathing tube to be inserted through his mouth into his windpipe and being hooked up to a ventilator. Because of this he is in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) of the hospital. He has been there since surgery. After five days on the ventilator, the breathing tube was removed. That was three days ago. Yesterday was the first “good day” he had had since becoming ill, and the first day you felt relaxed and hopeful since before surgery. You saw him yesterday at noon, and expected him to be transferred to the floor today. The only outstanding issue was a little fever the doctors were following. The doctors called late last night and you were told that Henry had had a set back this morning. The breathing tube had to be put back in because he was having trouble breathing again. You return and find him still in the SICU with the breathing tube and on the ventilator once more. Something obviously went wrong.

# MS. ARLENE COHEN (ELDERLY SISTER)

You and your niece are your brother’s only surviving family members. Henry has been a widower for many years and had only one daughter, who lives in on the west coast and is in town to see her Dad. You have been visiting Henry every day in the hospital and feel very responsible for him. You have been talking to this resident for the last three days. Overall, you have been very satisfied with the care in the ICU and appreciate that the resident was able to get Henry off the ventilator three days ago, when others thought it might take longer. Still, you are very concerned that he is back on the ventilator once again.

# MS. JOANNE BASS (UNMARRIED ADULT DAUGHTER)

You live on the west coast and have been somewhat estranged from your Dad since your mother died many years ago. You’ve come back home to try to “patch things up” before his cancer advances. You know the surgical resident less well than your Aunt Arlene. She is very worried about your Dad, which makes you worried, and you’re wondering what happened. You believe your Dad will ultimately die from the cancer, but you think he has some good months if not years left and don’t want to see him suffer un-necessarily. You are concerned about who is responsible for the set-back and whether this means your Dad has less quality time left because of it.

# SCENARIO OVERVIEW

Overview: You, the family members, should begin the encounter by appearing worried, fearful, and stressed. “Worry” should change to “frustration” after you begin to understand what happened. Through much of the first half of the 20-minute encounter, you should be on the “offense,” asking questions and expressing emotion. It is the doctor’s responsibility to regain your trust and to resolve any issues before the time is up.

# SCENARIO SEQUENCE

The doctor should come in and reintroduce himself. The doctor should explain what happened with clear, understandable, non-medical terms. For example:

(DOCTOR): In addition to his fever, Mr. Bass started to have a hard time breathing overnight. The oxygen levels in his blood dropped and his blood pressure became low as well. Because of the breathing trouble, the breathing tube and ventilator had to be replaced. One of the things that can cause this is a blood clot in the lung. We sent Mr. Bass to have a CAT scan of the lungs and it confirmed that blood clots were in the lung. There doesn’t seem to be any other issues and Mr. Bass is “stable” except that he needs a little extra oxygen than he did previously. This may be from the clot or because he may have a pneumonia, which might explain the fever in the first place.

Feel free to interrupt the doctor. Appear confused, like you are having trouble processing what the doctor says after hearing something about a “set-back” and the “blood clot.” Act confused as well if the doctor says, “pulmonary embolus,” or “PE,” which is the technical term and medical slang respectively for a blood clot that has formed somewhere in the blood stream and then traveled and lodged in the vessels that send blood through the lungs. For example:

+ “What do you mean a blood clot?” “When did this happen?”   
+ “A pulmonary embo... what is that?”   
. “How did this happen.”   
. “I didn’t realize that something like this could happen.”   
. “Does this happen often?”   
“Is this related to his cancer?”

If that doesn’t prompt the doctor to re-explain, say, “I don’t understand.” Ideally, the doctor should proactively assess your understanding of the explanation with follow-up questions like, “Tell me what you understand about this set-back.” “I want to make sure you understand, so tell me what you’ve heard.”

You should also challenge the doctor for details about the impact of having a pulmonary embolus, especially if he/she “glosses” over it, like “no big deal, it is small and everything is fine.” For example:

. “Did he lose consciousness? How long was he without normal oxygen?”   
. “Was he in pain?”   
 “How long will he have to be on the ventilator now?” “Will this delay his coming home?”

“How will you know when the clot is gone?”

# “What else has to be done?”

The doctor should indicate that the complication resulted from a blood clot that usually forms in the legs and then breaks loose, traveling with the rest of the blood as it returns to the heart and then is pumped to the lung where it lodges and blocks blood flow. Because the blood is blocked from the lung, it doesn’t get oxygen and the patient’s oxygen levels drop. Immobile patients and patients with cancer (Mr. Bass has both) are at higher risk for this. There are some things that can be done to help prevent this from happening (medication to thin the blood and pumps on the legs that move the blood), but only one of them was done for Mr. Bass (the leg pumps). The complication should be honestly and truthfully explained, including the fact that it may have occurred because his blood wasn’t thinned with medications. The doctor should not appear evasive, or overly defensive, and he/she should take appropriate responsibility. For example:

(DOCTOR): His breathing problem is because of the pulmonary embolus or blood clot in the lung. The clot may have formed in the first place because of being in bed for the last week or so and because of his cancer. We tried to prevent it from happening with the leg squeezers, but we didn’t give him blood thinning medicine as an added protection. This was an oversight and I was responsible for it. We chose not to start the medication initially for fear of unwanted bleeding following surgery, but I forgot to start it once the bleeding risk was lower.

If the doctor blames someone else or appears evasive, you should ask,

“What caused the blood clot?” “Are you saying it’s normal to get blood clots? Can’t you do anything to prevent them?”   
 “Are you saying he was supposed to get some medication, and didn’t?”   
. “Who was ultimately responsible?”

Even if the doctor explains it all very well, you need to challenge the doctor on why it wasn’t done. You also need to be frustrated and upset—even a little unreasonably so—that your brother / Dad suffered as a result of the set-back.

“Isn’t there a way to make sure this isn’t forgotten?” “It makes me angry that after all he has been through, my brother had to suffer this kind of set-back.”   
“In a university hospital, I certainly don’t expect mistakes like this to happen on a so-called ‘routine’ procedure.” “He was just starting to do so well – and he was so happy to be off the ventilator – I can’t bear how this must feel to him.” “I wish I had known about his risk for this, I could have followed up to make sure he was properly protected?”

During the explanation the doctor should express empathy and sincerely apologize. The doctor should also allow you to vent frustration, and he/she should validate your feelings. If you don’t get this kind of empathy from the doctor, you should say,

 “Don’t you think I have the right to feel this way?” or  “How would you feel if this happened to someone you love?”

The doctor must explain how physicians try to avoid this kind of complication. For example:

(DOCTOR): We know that patients are at risk for pulmonary embolism and we try to balance the risk of bleeding with the risk of clot formation when we decide to give a blood thinner or not. In this case, I thought the risk of bleeding was high enough to avoid the medication in the beginning. I should have revisited this after the procedure was done. To address this oversight,, we have implemented a review system where the physician is called if this medication is not prescribed by 2 days after surgery.

If the doctor says, “This kind of complication is just unavoidable,” you should respond:

“I don’t accept that,” or “I would feel better if I knew what you did or will do to avoid it in the future.”

Finally, the doctor should explain the new treatment plan for Henry, now that he’s on the ventilator and has the pulmonary embolus, with clear, understandable, non-medical terms. You should again interrupt, or have a confused look on your face to indicate poor understanding. If the doctor doesn’t respond to a subtle cue, interject with: “What you just said made no sense to me,” or “Tell me again what is happening next, and why?”

Assuming the doctor has done a good job defusing your anger and answering your questions, you offer some sign of acceptance, such as:

“Now that I understand it, I appreciate your concern and continued care.” “Thank you for taking the time to explain this.” “I think what you’ve outlined is a good plan for going forward.”

The doctor should provide closure to the discussion prior to leaving. This is best done by again apologizing and reassuring you that the medical staff will continue to provide the best care possible, and explain how that will happen, and when and how you will get further information. Regardless of what the doctor says, you should ask the doctor to clarify,

“Who is on call next?” and “What should I do if I have other questions?”
