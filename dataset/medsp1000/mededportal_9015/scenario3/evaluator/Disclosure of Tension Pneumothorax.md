# Disclosure of Complication: Tension Pneumothorax

RESIDENT INSTRUCTIONS

# THE PATIENT

George Miller is a 75-year male with colon cancer. Ten days ago he was admitted to the SICU with respiratory failure, intra-abdominal sepsis, and failure to thrive after Hartman’s procedure for a perforated color cancer. He has been in the ICU for 10 days, and you have been taking care of him for the last three days. You were able to extubate Mr. Miller three days ago, but he still requires BIPAP and NT suctioning for thick secretions and a poor cough. You decided to place a new central line last night because Mr. Miller had persistent fevers and positive blood culture, possibly caused by the first central line. (The source of ongoing fever is unclear.) Prior to the placement you notified the family by phone, briefly discussing the risks but mainly focusing on the reasons for the line. The line placement was complicated by a tension pneumothorax, which you and the ICU team identified and treated with an emergency chest tube. However, during the sequence of events Mr. Miller became hypoxic and required re-intubation. The lung is now reexpanded and the chest tube is in good position on F/U CXR but he still has a higher FIO2 requirement than he did previously and will likely spend several days on the ventilator. You notified the family early this morning about the reintubation.

# MS. HELEN COOPER (ELDERLY SISTER)

Ms. Helen Cooper and Jenny, George’s daughter, are the patient’s only surviving family members. George has been a widower for many years and Jenny lives on the west coast; she only recently came into town to see her Dad. Ms. Cooper is the patient’s main caretaker. She has been visiting George every day in the hospital and obviously feels quite responsible for him. You have been talking to her for the last three days. It was Ms. Cooper whom you called last night to explain the need to replace the central line.

# MS. JENNY MILLER (DAUGHTER)

You’ve seen the daughter once or twice, but have never spoken with her.

# OYOUR OBJECTIVES FOR THIS ENCOUNTER ARE TO:

Explain the complication and its impact on the patient in understandable, nonmedical terms  Apologize, and to be forthright and honest in taking responsibility for the complication  Actively listen, assess the family’s understanding  Respond appropriately (non-defensively) to family emotions  Explain how complications of this nature are avoided, and what you might do differently next time Engage family support for the revised treatment plan 1 Ensure continuity of communication and care

# THE PATIENT

George Miller is a 75 –year old man who has colon cancer. Ten days ago he had his colon removed. Following the surgery, George developed an infection in his abdomen as well as significant breathing problems that required a breathing tube to be inserted through his mouth into his windpipe and being hooked up to a ventilator. Because of this he is in the Surgical Intensive Care Unit (SICU) of the hospital. He has been there since surgery. After five days on the ventilator, the breathing tube was removed. That was three days ago. Yesterday was the first “good day” he had had since becoming ill, and the first day you felt relaxed and hopeful since before surgery. You saw him yesterday at noon, and expected him to be transferred to the floor today. The doctors called late last night and asked permission to put in a new intravenous catheter (also called a “central line”) because he had a fever and the old catheter might be infected. They said it was routine and that complications were rare. You were called again today and were told that George had had a set-back this morning. You return and find him still in the SICU with the breathing tube and on the ventilator again. Something obviously went wrong.

# MS. HELEN COOPER (ELDERLY SISTER)

You and your niece are your brother’s only surviving family members. George has been a widower for many years and had only one daughter, who lives in on the west coast and is in town to see her Dad. You have been visiting George every day in the hospital and feel very responsible for him. You have been talking to this resident for the last three days. You dimly remember (s)he called last night, but it was late and you weren’t sure what it was about. Overall, you have been very satisfied with the care in the ICU and appreciate that the resident was able to get George off the ventilator three days ago, when others thought it might take longer. Still, you are very concerned by what you see.

# MS. JENNY MILLER (ADULT DAUGHTER)

You live on the west coast and have been somewhat estranged from your Dad since your mother died many years ago. You’ve come back to the Twin Cities to try to “patch things up” before his cancer advances. You know the resident less well than your Aunt Helen. She is very worried about your Dad, which makes you worried, and you’re wondering what happened. You believe your Dad will ultimately die from the cancer, but you think he has some good months if not years left and don’t want to see him suffer un-necessarily. You are concerned about who is responsible for the set-back and whether this means your Dad has less quality time left because of it.

# SCENARIO OVERVIEW

You, the family members, should begin the encounter by appearing worried, fearful, and stressed. “Worry” should change to “frustration” after you begin to understand what happened. Through much of the first half of the 20-minute encounter, you should be on the “offense,” asking questions and expressing emotion. It is the doctor’s responsibility to regain your trust and to resolve any issues before the time is up.

# SCENARIO SEQUENCE

The doctor should come in and reintroduce himself. The doctor should explain what happened with clear, understandable, non-medical terms. For example:

(DOCTOR): When placing the large IV or central line near the neck, the needle penetrated the lung and it collapsed. This made it so Mr. Miller couldn’t breathe effectively. To reexpand the lung, a tube had to be placed into the chest, outside of the lung. This is called a chest tube. Because of the breathing trouble, the breathing tube and ventilator had to be replaced. There doesn’t seem to be any other issues and Mr. Miller is “stable” except that he needs a little extra oxygen than he did previously. This may be because when the lung collapses it takes a while for it to re-expand and work efficiently or because he may have a pneumonia, which might explain the fever in the first place.

Feel free to interrupt the doctor. Appear confused, like you are having trouble processing what the doctor says after hearing something about a “set-back” and the “lung collapsing.” For example:

“You mean he lost a lung?” “When did this happen?”   
“I don’t think I would have let you put in the line if I knew this could happen.”   
“Now what is going to happen to his lung? Is this permanent damage?”   
. “Why was it so important that he get another central line?”   
“Is this related to his cancer?”

If that doesn’t prompt the doctor to re-explain, say, “I don’t understand.” Ideally, the doctor should proactively assess your understanding of the explanation with follow-up questions like, “Tell me what you understand about this set-back.” “I want to make sure you understand, so tell me what you’ve heard.”

You should also challenge the doctor for details about the impact of having a collapsed lung, especially if he/she “glosses” over it, like “it’s no big deal, a lung collapsed, we’ve re-inflated it, now everything is fine.” For example:

“Was there any damage to his brain? How long was he without normal oxygen?”   
. “Was he in pain?”   
 “How long will he have to be on the ventilator now?”   
 “Will this delay his coming home?” “How will you know when the lung has re-expanded?” “Is Dad going to have more trouble with breathing in the future because of this?”

The doctor should indicate that the complication resulted from a technical procedure that did not go exactly as planned. It was not an error of negligence or judgment, but an unfortunate risk associated with executing this procedure. The complication should be honestly and truthfully explained, including the fact that it occurred at the hands of the doctors. The doctor should not appear evasive, or overly defensive, and he/she should take appropriate responsibility. For example:

(DOCTOR): The collapsed lung occurred as a complication to our placement of the central line. We had to put in a new line because of the greater threat of infection from the old catheter. We had to weigh the risk of possibly hitting the lung during the procedure— something that happens about $1 \%$ of the time to well-trained doctors—against the possibility that the infection would spread and cause an even greater set-back. I was responsible for making that decision, and although I deeply regret the complication, I would make the same decision again because it had to be done.

If the doctor blames someone else or appears evasive, you should ask, “Who did it?” or “Who was ultimately responsible?”

Even if the doctor explains it all very well, you need to challenge the doctor on why the procedure “wasn’t done correctly.” You also need to be frustrated and upset—even a little unreasonably so—that your brother / Dad suffered as a result of the set-back.

“Have you had training on this procedure? Why didn’t it work this time?” “It makes me angry that after all he has been through, my brother had to suffer this kind of set-back.” “In a university hospital, I certainly don’t expect mistakes like this to happen on a so-called ‘routine’ procedure.” “He was just starting to do so well – and he was so happy to be off the ventilator – I can’t bear how this must feel to him.”   
“I wish you had told me this might have happened.”

During the explanation the doctor should express empathy and sincerely apologize. The doctor should also allow you to vent frustration, and he/she should validate your feelings. If you don’t get this kind of empathy from the doctor, you should say,

“Don’t you think I have the right to feel this way?” or “How would you feel if this happened to someone you love?”

The doctor must explain how physicians try to avoid this kind of complication. For example:

(DOCTOR): When putting in a line, we monitor the patient closely, we position them carefully to have the best angle, two people work together to insert the line, we use imaging or other signs to guide us, we call for back-up if the patient has some unusual feature that makes the procedure more difficult. Next time I do this, I will remember how you weren’t really clear about the procedure’s risk ahead of time and try to explain it better.

If the doctor says, “This kind of complication is just unavoidable,” you should respond:

“I don’t accept that,” or “I would feel better if I knew what you did or will do to avoid it in the future.”

Finally, the doctor should explain the new treatment plan for George, now that he’s on the ventilator with a collapsed lung, with clear, understandable, non-medical terms. You should again interrupt, or have a confused look on your face to indicate poor understanding. If the doctor doesn’t respond to a subtle cue, interject with: “What you just said made no sense to me,” or “Tell me again what is happening next, and why?”

Assuming the doctor has done a good job defusing your anger and answering your questions, you offer some sign of acceptance, such as:

. “Now that I understand it, I’m glad you did what you did.” “Thank you for taking the time to explain this.” “I think what you’ve outlined is a good plan for going forward.”

The doctor should provide closure to the discussion prior to leaving. This is best done by again apologizing and reassuring you that the medical staff will continue to provide the best care possible, and explain how that will happen, and when and how you will get further information. Regardless of what the doctor says, you should ask the doctor to clarify,

“Who is on call next?” and “What should I do if I have other questions?”
