# End of Life: Ovarian Cancer

RESIDENT CASE NOTES

# THE PATIENT

Mrs. Andrea Smith is a 65-year-old woman from St. Paul with diabetes who underwent pelvic exenteration for ovarian cancer 3 years ago. She was doing well at home until 6 months ago when she was diagnosed with metastatic, uncurable disease. She has been hospitalized several times with obstructive uropathy and renal failure, but has been successfully treated and has never required ICU admission. She was admitted to the hospital 5 days ago and has been in the ICU for the past 3 days. She was transferred from the floor with a deteriorating mental status. She was treated for kidney failure and presumed infection, possibly pneumonia. She developed worsening hypoxemia and has required intubation and her mental status hasn’t improved. A head CT was obtained that showed findings consistent with a large hemispheric stroke. The husband knows about the kidney failure, the possible infection, that she is on life support, and that she’s had a stroke, but he does not how bad it is. Currently her blood pressure is dropping slightly (SBP 90s) and she still has a respiratory drive. She is unresponsive without any sedation. The team feels like the patient’s chance of meaningful survival is minimal and any additional care would be futile. It’s time to have an end-of-life conference with the family to determine code status and agree on a revised care plan.

# MR. MATT SMITH (HUSBAND)

You’ve seen the patient’s husband, Mr. Matt Smith, but have not had any significant personal interaction with him. You’ve heard from the nurses that he has been asking a lot of questions recently and is upset about the amount of equipment used to support his wife’s life and worried that she is in pain.

# MS. MARY WARNER (SISTER)

The patient’s sister, Mary, has just arrived from her home (which is three hours away), having been recently notified by Matt that Andrea was “going downhill.”

# THE PATIENT

Mrs. Andrea Smith is a 65-year-old female with diabetes who underwent pelvic exenteration for ovarian cancer 3 years ago. Andrea was diagnosed with an incurable cancer recurrence 6 months ago and has been hospitalized several times for kidney failure because the tumor has wrapped around the ureters (tubes that connect the kidneys and bladder and carry urine) and blocked them, but she has never required admission to the SICU before this. She was doing well at home, but had not returned to her volunteer work after the recurrence. She lives with her husband, Matt, in St. Paul.

Mrs. Smith has been in the hospital again with kidney failure and became increasingly confused over the last several days. Yesterday she was transferred to the SICU. Soon after arriving in the SICU she began to have breathing problems and needed to have a breathing tube placed in her wind pipe and needed to be connected to the ventilator to breathe for her. She then had a CAT scan of her brain that showed a “large stroke.” The amount of equipment being used to support her life has increased, and she is largely non-responsive now. She looks terrible – almost dead.

# MR. MATT SMITH (HUSBAND)

Even before you brought her to the hospital, Andrea had not been quite “herself.” She seemed weaker than usual, and was not eating or sleeping very well. You’ve seen first-hand the deterioration in her condition, particularly over the last 3 days. The machines, the tubes, and the complicated procedures all prevent you from getting very close to her. You are often at her bedside, holding her hand. She does not respond to you. Her entire body is swollen. All the vital sign numbers on the monitor seem low, but you’re not sure what they mean. Many doctors and nurses are in and out all day; you are not sure who is who, but they all appear to be concerned and working hard. You’ve never talked with this resident before.

You feel that your wife is slipping away from you. You don’t believe she would want to be kept alive at all costs and recall conversations with her wherein she talked about the need for quality of life and that she didn’t want to prolong her life if she couldn’t enjoy the things that made her happy. You’re ready to assume the responsibility, as proxy, for letting Andrea die, as long as her sister also agrees, and if the doctors can assure you that she couldn’t survive for very long, even if unusual measures were initially successful.

# MS. MARY WARNER (SISTER)

You and Andrea have been best friends besides being sisters, even though you live three hours away. They each had children at about the same time in life and have remained very involved with each other’s families. Extended gatherings during the summer are more than a tradition – they have provided the social network that’s kept the family together. When your brother-in-law called saying Andrea was worse, you rushed right down. You can’t believe how poorly your sister looks today compared to just two weeks ago, when she talked about having a family reunion with all the grandchildren. You’re concerned that the doctors haven’t done all they can and wonder if the young resident (who you have never met until today) is “up to it. You feel guilty that you haven’t spent as much time with Andrea, because your own husband has moderate dementia and requires significant daily care.

# SCENARIO OVERVIEW

You, the family members, should begin the encounter in a state of concern that Andrea’s state has deteriorated so drastically in the last three days. You know that the CAT scan showed a stroke, but not how devastating it is or what it means. It should take you a while for this news to sink in. You both should ask a lot of questions to clarify Andrea’s chances of survival. Mary should stay in a state of denial longer than Matt. When he finally understands, Mary should be the more emotional of the two of you (Matt is more prepared for this). After about 10 minutes, you should accept that he is terminal, but you should proceed to disagree about what should happen next. Mary should also have a harder time focusing on what Andrea would want, rather than what Mary wants (i.e., for the sisters to go to the lake with their grandchildren one last time). Matt feels guilty for being the one to say, “It’s time to let her go.” By the end of 20 minutes, you both are ready to agree to DNR code and accept “comfort care.”

# SCENARIO SEQUENCE

The doctor should enter and introduce himself and try to establish some rapport; you should respond appropriately, and ask for clarifying introductions about the doctor’s role and position.

(Mary): “So, you’re just the resident (or fellow) in charge of my sister? Where is the senior doctor? Aren’t there other people taking care of her?” (Matt): “There have been so many doctors and nurses these last couple of days; it’s kind of hard to know who is who or what they are all doing.”

The doctor should explain the purpose of the meeting. The purpose is delicate, and may be explained in several ways, for example:

(DOCTOR): Talking with family members about their loved one’s wishes and preparing for the end-of-life is something we do regularly with all of the families in the SICU. I’ve called this meeting today because Andrea’s situation has changed and we need to discuss a new care plan going forward. I’d like to get to know both or you better as we talk, and to be as helpful as I can in making some decisions.

If the doctor begins the conversation awkwardly by saying, “Mrs. Smith does not have a code status determined, and we need to get that into the charts,” you should say, “What do you mean, code status?” “Why? I don’t understand.”

Preferably, the doctor will first try to gain some insight as how much and what you know and understand. For example, the doctor might ask, “Tell me what you understand about your sister’s condition,” or “What do you understand has happened?” You might answer:

(Matt): “Well I just don’t know what’s happened to her. She has not really been herself for several weeks, but until three days ago she seemed to be doing okay. But obviously something has really changed since then. She looks all swollen up and grey. She doesn’t seem to hear me. I feel like we’re losing her.”

(Mary): “That’s what I was going to ask you—what’s going on? I saw her two weeks ago and we talked about going to the lake next spring, and she seemed fine; but now she doesn’t even respond to me.”

The doctor should explain the patient’s status clearly and without medical jargon. The basic information should include several facts, for example:

(DOCTOR): Several things have changed. As you know, Mrs. Smith has the recurrent cancer that has caused her kidneys to deteriorate, but we’ve been able to successfully combat this so far. This time, we’re not so lucky. When Andrea came to the SICU, she was showing signs of full kidney failure. We’ve been treating her for this, but it is a difficult process to stop once it takes hold. Then she became more and more confused. We thought this might be due to infection or pneumonia because of her breathing problems (thus the breathing tube) which have since become really severe. But as we evaluated things further with the CAT scan, we found the stroke. The stroke is extensive and will probably extensively limit her abilities.

Throughout the explanation, you should interrupt and ask questions. Try to get a clear picture of how the situation deteriorated. Ask a lot of “why” questions. (“Why did the kidneys fail? Why would someone get an infection in a hospital? Aren’t things kept sterile here?”) Once you hear how bad the stroke is, Matt should register fear and sadness, Mary should reflect a state of denial that the stroke is serious and irreversible. For example:

(Matt): I feared something like this must have happened, but it’s just so hard to accept.” (Mary): “What are you saying? How can you be sure of what you’re telling me?”

Assuming the doctor explains how extensive the stroke is, you should then go on to ask a lot of questions about what kind of stroke it was; what does it mean when someone has this kind of stroke, etc. At some point, the doctor should indicate his opinion that given the magnitude of the stroke, further medical interventions would be “futile;” or that Mrs. Smith has a “minimal chance of survival,” or something similar. You should ask for clarification about what that means.

(Matt): “If you keep treating the kidney failure or infection, will the stroke reverse   
itself? Is this really permanent?”   
(Mary): “What do you mean, ‘minimal chances of survival’? What do you mean,   
‘futile’? That just says to me that you’re ready to give up. Well, I’m not. I’m not   
ready to accept that she is going to die. Nobody really knows how much longer she   
may have. We’re not God. I’ve heard of other people who have recovered from   
strokes.”

The doctor should address these perceptions, but gently persist with explaining that based on Mrs. Smith’s other problems (the recurrent, uncurable cancer and kidney failure), the pattern is pretty clear: Mrs. Smith really has no significant chance of surviving, and that even if she were to survive physically, she would be severely handicapped from the stroke and require long-term institutional care. Her quality of life would be very minimal. She would never “be herself again” because of the irreversible damage to the brain.

If the doctor was unclear about the impact of the stroke, or the patient’s chance of survival as described above, you should show bewilderment (“What are you trying to tell us?”), guilt (“If only I had brought her to the hospital sooner, this wouldn’t have happened”), or anger (“I don’t know what all this talk is about. Andrea is going to get better. I just know it”).

By the 10-minute point, reality should hit you and you should show emotion, tears, sadness etc. Do this in any way that would be genuine for you, if you were in this situation. The doctor should allow you to express these feelings, should show empathy, offer words of comfort, and ask if you would like additional support (i.e., a member of the clergy, other family, social worker).

After you’ve expressed some emotion, the next step is to create a plan. The doctor should explain that since the patient can’t express her wishes, the doctors assume the patient would want everything done and work to that end - in other words do everything possible. However, when there is family present, then the doctors use the family as a surrogate to know what the patient would want done. The doctor should ask about the patient’s values and beliefs, as well as your own. The doctor should reinforce that the focus should be on what the patient would want done, not what you would personally want for yourself (if you were in that position) or for her.

(DOCTOR): Did Andrea ever talk about what she would want if she was on life support without a chance to get off? Do you know whether Andrea has a living will? Had she formalized her wishes? Tell me, how has your family dealt with dying family members in the past? What has been your experience? What are your customs or beliefs about death?

The doctor should ask if you know what a “DNR/DNI” code status means. He should explain it, and also suggest that you allow Mrs. Smith to have that written in her chart. The doctor should clarify that in Andrea’s condition, reviving her heart with CPR or electric shocks would be extremely traumatic to her and also futile. Keeping her on artificial life support (i.e., the breathing tube) also delays the inevitable and prolongs suffering.

The doctor should ask you whether you feel comfortable determining that status. Here is where the second difference between Matt and Mary should surface.

(Matt): “Andrea never got around to drawing up a ‘living will,’ but we did talk about it when she was diagnosed with cancer. I don’t think she would want any heroic measures. I don’t think she would want to be on a ventilator for the rest of her life, not able to communicate, or enjoy life.”   
(Mary): “Even though you say ‘there’s no hope,’ I think we should give her a chance. I say we support her for a while longer and see what happens. I’d like to take her to the lake just once more, to have the grandchildren around.”   
(Matt): “You know, it’s hard being the one to say, ‘It’s time to let her go.’ But I’m the one who has been with her all these years, and I think I know what Andrea would say if she could. It’s easy for you to say, ‘Give her another chance.’ You’ll being going back to Duluth by tomorrow night. Meanwhile, I would be the one to take care of her and watch her suffer.”   
(Mary): “Well, I’m not ready to just pull the plug and walk away. That seems too cruel.”

At some point, the doctor should try to explain what “comfort care” means. You should ask “what would happen if you agree to a DNR/DNI,” if the doctor doesn’t bring it up first. The doctor should explain that the goal of care shifts from healing and making the patient better, to providing and assuring relief of pain and suffering until the patient dies. You should ask questions like:

“How will you make sure she is not in any pain?”

“Will you still give her oxygen? Will the breathing tube be removed?”

“Will you feed her, give her any water?”   
“So, you’ll stop treating the infection?”   
“How long before she dies? What will she actually die of?”   
“Will she stay here, or be moved somewhere else?”   
“Can I stay with her until she passes?”   
“Will you still be her doctor?”

Basically, once you understand what comfort care means, and that the doctor is not going to abandon you or Andrea, it becomes easier to accept. You should end the encounter by giving your approval to have “DNR/DNI” entered into Andrea’s chart and prepare for comfort care.

The resident should end the encounter by assuring you there will be follow up and how you can get in contact with him.
