# End-of-Life Kidney and Pancreas Transplant

# RESIDENT CASE NOTES

# THE PATIENT

Mr. Andrew Smith is a 65-year-old male from St. Paul with diabetes who had a successful kidney and pancreas transplant at the UMMC two years ago. He was doing well at home, but had not returned to work after his transplants. Mr. Smith had been hospitalized in the past for rejection, but had never before required ICU admission. He has been in the ICU for 5 days now. He was transferred from the floor with a deteriorating mental status. He was treated for presumed sepsis but soon thereafter he required intubation with worsening hypoxemia. A head CT was obtained that showed findings consistent with a large hemispheric stroke. The family knows he had a stroke. Currently his blood pressure is dropping slightly (SBP 90s) and he has respiratory drive. He is unresponsive without any sedation. The team feels like the patient’s chance of meaningful survival is minimal and any additional care would be futile. It’s time to have an end-of-life conference with the family to discuss DNI/DNR and comfort Care.

# MRS. MARY SMITH (WIFE)

You’ve noticed that the patient’s wife, Mary, has been at the hospital nearly every day but haven’t had much personal interaction with her. She seems fairly familiar with the medical environment, having been through the double transplant two years ago. You’ve heard from the nurses that she has been asking a lot of questions recently and is upset about the amount of equipment used to support her husband’s life and worried that he is in pain.

# MR. BILL SMITH (BROTHER)

The patient’s brother, Bill, donated a kidney two years ago to help his “little” brother survive. He has just arrived from Duluth, having been notified by Mary that Andrew was “going downhill.”

# THE PATIENT

Andrew Smith is a 65-year-old male with diabetes and kidney failure who had a successful kidney and pancreas transplant at the UMMC two years ago (the pancreas improves the diabetes). Andrew had been hospitalized in the past for organ rejection, but had never required admission into the SICU before this. He was doing well at home, but had not returned to work after his transplants. He lives with his wife, Mary, in St. Paul.

Mr. Smith became increasingly confused over the last several days and yesterday was transferred to the SICU. Soon after arriving in the SICU he began to have breathing problems and needed to have a breathing tube placed in his wind pipe as well as the ventilator to breathe for him. He then had a CAT scan of his brain that showed a “large stroke.” The amount of equipment being used to support his life has increased, and he is largely non-responsive now. He looks terrible – almost dead.

# MRS. MARY SMITH (WIFE)

Even before you brought him into the hospital, Andrew had not been quite “himself.” He seemed weaker than usual, and not eating or sleeping very well. You’ve seen first-hand the deterioration in his condition over the last 3 days. The machines, the tubes, and the complicated procedures all prevent you from getting very close to him. You are often at his bedside, holding his hand. He does not respond to you. His entire body is swollen. All the vital sign numbers on the monitor seem low, but you’re not sure what they mean. Many doctors and nurses are in and out all day; you are not sure who is who, but they all appear to be concerned and working hard. You’ve never talked with this resident before.

You feel that your husband is slipping away from you. You don’t believe he would want to be kept alive at all costs and recall conversations with him wherein he talked about the need for quality of life and that he didn’t want to prolong his life if he couldn’t enjoy the things that made him happy. You’re ready to assume the responsibility, as proxy, for letting Andrew die, as long as his brother also agrees, and if the doctors can assure you that he couldn’t survive for very long, even if unusual measures were initially successful.

# MR. BILL SMITH (BROTHER)

You donated a kidney two years ago to help your “little” brother survive. When your sister-in-law called saying Andrew was worse, you rushed right down from Duluth. You can’t believe how poorly your brother looks today compared to just two weeks ago, when he stopped by and talked about going fishing in the spring. You’re concerned that the doctors haven’t done all they can and wonder if the young resident (who you have never met until today) is “up to it.

# SCENARIO OVERVIEW

You, the family members, should begin the encounter in a state of concern that Andrew’s state has deteriorated so drastically in the last three days. You know that he’s had a stroke, just not how big. You know people who have recovered from strokes, even some without any weakness or paralysis. It should take you a while for the news about the extent of the stroke to sink in. You both should ask a lot of questions to clarify Andrew’s chances of survival. Bill should stay in a state of denial longer than Mary. When he finally understands, Bill should be the more emotional of the two of you (Mary is more prepared for this). After about 10 minutes, you should accept that he is terminal, but you should proceed to disagree about what should happen next. Bill should also have a harder time focusing on what Andrew would want, rather than what Bill wants (i.e., for the brothers to go fishing one last time). Mary feels guilty for being the one to say, “It’s time to let him go.” By the end of 20 minutes, you both are ready to agree to DNR code and accept “comfort care.”

# SCENARIO SEQUENCE

The doctor should enter and introduce himself and try to establish some rapport; you should respond appropriately, and ask for clarifying introductions about the doctor’s role and position.

(Bill): “So, you’re just the resident (or fellow) in charge of my brother? Where is the senior doctor? Aren’t there other people taking care of him?” (Mary): “There have been so many doctors and nurses these last couple of days; it’s kind of hard to know who is who or what they are all doing.”

The doctor should explain the purpose of the meeting. The purpose is delicate, and may be explained in several ways, for example:

(DOCTOR): Talking with family members about their loved one’s wishes and preparing for the end-of-life is something we do regularly with all of the families in the SICU. I’ve called this meeting today because Andrew’s situation has changed and we need to discuss a new care plan going forward. I’d like to get to know both or you better as we talk, and to be as helpful as I can in making some decisions.

If the doctor begins the conversation awkwardly by saying, “Mr. Smith does not have a code status determined, and we need to get that into the charts,” you should say, “What do you mean, code status?” “Why? I don’t understand.”

Preferably, the doctor will first try to gain some insight as how much and what you know and understand. For example, the doctor might ask, “Tell me what you understand about your brother’s condition,” or “What do you understand has happened?” You might answer:

(Mary): “Well I just don’t know what’s happened to him. He has not really been himself for several weeks, but he was certainly doing okay three days ago. I feel like something has really changed since then. He looks all swollen up and grey. He doesn’t seem to hear me. I feel like we’re losing him.”   
(Bill): “That’s what I was going to ask you—what’s going on? I saw him two weeks ago and we talked about going fishing next spring, and he seemed fine; but now he doesn’t even respond to me.”

The doctor should explain the patient’s status clearly and without medical jargon. The basic information should include several facts, for example:

(DOCTOR): Several things have changed. As you know, Mr. Smith has had problems with rejecting the donor kidney and pancreas before, but we’ve been able to successfully combat the body’s natural mechanism for rejecting these “foreign” organs. This time, we’re not so lucky. When Andrew came to the SICU, he was showing signs of sepsis, which means infection throughout the body. We’ve been treating him for this, but it is a difficult process to stop once it takes hold. Once a person has sepsis, his or her ability to think and respond normally changes, as you’ve noticed. The sepsis led to breathing problems (thus the breathing tube) which have since become really severe. The pattern with transplant patients like this is after a period of fighting it all, the body just starts to give out. Something “gives.” In Mr. Smith’s case, what has happened is that he suffered a large stroke in his brain.

Throughout the explanation, you should interrupt and ask questions. Try to get a clear picture of how the situation deteriorated. Ask a lot of “why” questions. (“Why did he get sepsis this time and not the other time?”) When you hear about the extent of the stroke, Mary should register fear and sadness, Bill should demonstrate disbelief and minimize the severity.

(Mary): I knew something bad was going to happen.   
(Bill): People get better from strokes. I know a guy from work who had a stroke and nothing is wrong with him.

Assuming the doctor explains that it really is a stroke, you should then go on to ask a lot of questions about what kind of stroke it was; what does it mean when someone has this kind of stroke, etc. At some point, the doctor should indicate his opinion that given the magnitude of the stoke, further medical interventions would be “futile;” or that Mr. Smith has a “minimal chance of survival,” or something similar. You should ask for clarification about what that means.

(Mary): “If you keep treating the sepsis, will the stroke reverse itself? Is this really permanent?”   
(Bill): “What do you mean, ‘minimal chances of survival’? What do you mean, ‘futile’? That just says to me that you’re ready to give up. Well, I’m not. I’m not ready to accept that he is going to die. Nobody really knows how much longer he may have. We’re not God. I’ve heard of other people who have recovered from strokes.”

The doctor should address these perceptions, but gently persist with explaining that based on all of the patients who have been treated with these conditions, the pattern is pretty clear: Mr. Smith really has no significant chance of surviving, and that even if he were to survive physically, he would be severely handicapped from the stroke and require long-term institutional care. His quality of life would be very minimal. He would never “be himself again” because of the irreversible damage to the brain.

If the doctor was unclear about the impact of the stroke, or the patient’s chance of survival as described above, you should show bewilderment (“What are you trying to tell us?”), guilt (“If only I had brought him to the hospital sooner, this wouldn’t have happened”), or anger (“I don’t know what all this talk is about. Andrew is going to get better. I just know it”).

By the 10-minute point, reality should hit you and you should show emotion, tears, sadness etc. Do this in any way that would be genuine for you, if you were in this situation. The doctor should allow you to express these feelings, should show empathy, offer words of comfort, and ask if you would like additional support (i.e., a member of the clergy, other family, social worker).

After you’ve expressed some emotion, the next step is to create a plan. The doctor should explain that since the patient can’t express his wishes, the doctors assume the patient would want everything done and work to that end - in other words do everything possible. However, when there is family present, then the doctors use the family as a surrogate to know what the patient would want done. The doctor should ask about the patient’s values and beliefs, as well as your own. The doctor should reinforce that the focus should be on what the patient would want done, not what you would personally want for yourself (if you were in that position) or for him.

(DOCTOR): Do you know whether Andrew has a living will? Had he formalized his wishes? Tell me, how has your family dealt with dying family members in the past? What has been your experience? What are your customs or beliefs about death?

The doctor should ask if you know what a “DNR/DNI” code status means. He should explain it, and also suggest that you allow Mr. Smith to have that written in his chart. The doctor should clarify that in Andrew’s condition, reviving his heart with CPR or electric shocks would be extremely traumatic to him and also futile. Keeping him on artificial life support (i.e., the breathing tube) also delays the inevitable and prolongs suffering.

The doctor should ask you whether you feel comfortable determining that status. Here is where the second difference between Mary and Bill should surface.

(Mary): “Bill never got around to drawing up a ‘living will,’ but we did talk about it when he had his transplant two years ago. I don’t think he would want any heroic measures. I don’t think he would want to be on a ventilator for the rest of his life, not able to communicate, or enjoy life.”   
(Bill): “Even though you say ‘there’s no hope,’ I think we should give him a chance. I say we support him for a while longer and see what happens. I’d like to take him fishing just once more, for old time’s sake.”   
(Mary): “You know, it’s hard being the one to say, ‘It’s time to let him go.’ But I’m the one who has been with him all these years, and I think I know what Andrew would say if he could. It’s easy for you to say, ‘Give him another chance.’ You’ll being going back to Duluth by tomorrow night, and fishing season is over, Bill! (Get real.) Meanwhile, I would be the one to take care of him and watch him suffer.”   
(Bill): “Well, I’m not ready to just pull the plug and walk away. That seems too cruel.”

At some point, the doctor should try to explain what “comfort care” means. You should ask “what would happen if you agree to a DNR/DNI,” if the doctor doesn’t bring it up first. The doctor should explain that the goal of care shifts from healing and making the patient better, to providing and assuring relief of pain and suffering until the patient dies. You should ask questions like:

. “How will you make sure he is not in any pain?”   
“Will you still give him oxygen? Will the breathing tube be removed?”   
“Will you feed him, give him any water?”   
. “So, you’ll stop treating the sepsis?”   
“How long before he dies? What will he actually die of?”   
. “Will he stay here, or be moved somewhere else?”

# “Can I stay with him until he passes?”

“Will you still be his doctor?”

Basically, once you understand what comfort care means, and that the doctor is not going to abandon you or Andrew, it becomes easier to accept. You should end the encounter by giving your approval to have “DNR/DNI” entered into Andrew’s chart and prepare for comfort care.

The resident should end the encounter by assuring you there will be follow up and how you can get in contact with him.
