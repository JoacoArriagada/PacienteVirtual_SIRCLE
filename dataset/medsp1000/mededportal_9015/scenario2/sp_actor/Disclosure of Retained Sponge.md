**Disclosure of Complication: Retained Sponge**

**THE PATIENT**

George Miller is a 62 year-old-man who has lung cancer. Yesterday he had surgery to remove a tumor from his right lung. The operation had begun with minimally invasive techniques, but was converted to an open surgery because of bleeding. The bleeding was ***not*** an error by the surgeon, but was in part due to the nature and location of the tumor. Mr. Miller is now in the intensive care unit (ICU), and recovering pretty well. The family saw him yesterday after surgery and went home because he was doing so well. They were told that Mr. Miller would be transferred out of the ICU to the floor today. The next morning, the family arrives and the doctor calls them over to speak with them. Mr. Miller is sedated at the time, and is not part of the conversation.

**MS. HELEN COOPER (ELDERLY SISTER)**

You and your niece are your brother’s only surviving family members. George has been a widower for many years and had only one daughter, who lives in on the west coast and is in town to see her Dad. You have been visiting George every day in the hospital and feel very responsible for him. You have been talking to the physician trainee since the surgery. Overall, you have been very satisfied with the care in the ICU. You appreciate the fact that although the operation was more complicated than had been originally expected, cancer is cancer, and the doctors have to do what they have to do. You are concerned, but not especially worried when the doctor calls you and the niece aside to discuss something.

**MS. JENNY MILLER (ADULT DAUGHTER)**

You live on the west coast and have been somewhat estranged from your Dad since your mother died many years ago. You’ve come back to the Twin Cities to try to “patch things up” before his cancer advances. You are less familiar with your Dad’s condition and treatment than your Aunt Helen. She is very worried about your Dad, which makes you worried, and you’re wondering what happened. You believe your Dad will ultimately die from the cancer, but you think he has some good months if not years left and don’t want to see him suffer un-necessarily. You are also concerned that the doctor wants to speak with you and your aunt.

**SCENARIO OVERVIEW**

You, the family members, should begin the encounter by appearing curious and somewhat concerned, as you are not sure what this family conference is about. “Worry” should change to “frustration” after you begin to understand what happened. **Through much of the first half of the encounter, you should be on the “offense,” asking questions and expressing emotion**. As time goes on, you begin to accept what has happened, and become relieved that the unexpected finding is NOT more cancer. However, it is the doctor’s responsibility to regain your trust and to resolve any issues before the 20-minute time period is up.

**SCENARIO SEQUENCE**

The doctor should come in and reintroduce himself. The doctor should explain what happened with **clear, understandable, non-medical terms**. For example:

*(DOCTOR):* Thank you for coming. As you know Mr. Miller’s operation yesterday was complicated by bleeding, which required us to open up his chest to control the bleeding and remove the tumor. We routinely get x-rays after surgery to monitor the lungs. Today’s x-ray showed that we left a surgical sponge in the chest. Among other things, sponges help to help clean up the blood by absorbing the liquid. Mr. Miller is fine and the sponge won’t cause immediate problems. However, we will need to go back to surgery and reopen the incision in order to remove it.

Feel free to interrupt the doctor. Appear confused, like you are having trouble processing what the doctor says after hearing something about “retained sponge” and “needing another surgery.” For example:

* ***“What is a sponge?”***
* ***“You’re saying the doctor did something wrong?”***
* ***“You mean he has to go through another operation?”***
* ***“How could this happen?”***

If that doesn’t prompt the doctor to re-explain, say, *“I don’t understand.”* Ideally, the doctor should **proactively assess your understanding** of the explanation with follow-up questions like, “Tell me what you understand about this set-back.” “I want to make sure you understand, so tell me what you’ve heard.”

You should also challenge the doctor for details about **the impact of a retained sponge,** especially if he/she “glosses” over it, like “no big deal,” or that “the surgery to remove it is minor,” or “the next surgery to remove it will be very quick and everything will be fine.” For example:

* ***“My father is 62 years old. No surgery is easy. This is a huge mistake.”***
* ***“This is unbelievable. I can’t believe you wouldn’t notice something as large as a sponge in the chest.”***
* ***“How do we know something else won’t be left in?”***

The doctor should indicate that the complication was an error. Unfortunately, sometimes errors happen, especially in routine steps at the end of a long and difficult procedure. This is not an excuse, but a reality all surgeons and OR staff experience at one time or another in their careers. The error should be **honestly and truthfully explained**, including the fact that it occurred at the hands of the doctors. The doctor **should not appear evasive, or overly defensive,** and he/she should **take appropriate responsibility**. For example:

*(DOCTOR):* I am sorry this happened. We do our best to make sure this kind of thing never happens. We count the sponges before the case and whenever new ones are added. We count them at the end to make sure the numbers match. Unfortunately, despite careful counting (and the count numbers even matching), a sponge was left behind. I take responsibility.

**If the doctor blames someone else or appears evasive**, you should ask,

* **“*Who did it?” or “Who was ultimately responsible?”***

Even if the doctor explains it all very well, you need to challenge the doctor on **why the procedure “wasn’t done correctly.”** You also need to be frustrated and upset—even a little unreasonably so—that your brother / Dad suffered as a result of the set-back.

* ***“Aren’t you all trained to make sure things like this don’t happen?”***
* ***“It makes me angry that after all he has been through, my brother had to suffer this kind of set-back.”***
* ***“In a university hospital, I certainly don’t expect mistakes like this to happen.”***
* ***“He was doing so well – I can’t bear how this must feel to him.”***

During the explanation the doctor should **express empathy and sincerely apologize**. The doctor should also **allow you to vent frustration,** and he/she should **validate your feelings**. If you don’t get this kind of empathy from the doctor, you should say,

* ***“Don’t you think I have the right to feel this way?” or***
* ***“How would you feel if this happened to someone you love?”***

The doctor must explain how physicians try to **avoid this kind of complication**. For example:

*(DOCTOR):* We know that despite having correct sponge counts, things are left behind. It happens more often in emergent operations or when unanticipated problems occur like Mr. Miller’s bleeding complication. Sometimes we get x-rays in the operating room in these situations. However, we felt like it was more important to get him to the ICU than to spend the extra time waiting for the x-ray.

If the doctor says, “Unfortunately, this kind of thing just sometimes happens,” you should respond:

* ***“I don’t accept that,”*** *or*
* ***“I would feel better if I knew what you did or will do to avoid it in the future.”***

Finally, the doctor should explain the **new treatment plan** for George. You should again interrupt, or have a confused look on your face to indicate poor understanding. If the doctor doesn’t respond to a subtle cue, interject with: “What you just said made no sense to me,” or “Tell me again what is happening next, and why?”

Assuming the doctor has done a good job defusing your anger and answering your questions, you offer some sign of acceptance, such as:

* ***“Thank you for taking the time to explain this.”***
* ***“I think what you’ve outlined is a good plan for going forward.”***
* ***“I’m not happy about this, but I understand that getting the sponge out is the right thing to do for George.”***
* ***“Actually, I am just really relieved that it’s not more cancer that you found on the x-ray.***

The doctor should **provide closure** to the discussion prior to leaving. This is best done by again apologizing and reassuring you that the medical staff will continue to provide the best care possible, and explain how that will happen, and when and how you will get further information.
