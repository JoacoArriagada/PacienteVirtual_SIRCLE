| **Brief narrative description of case** | Patient is a 13 year old male with no prior medical history who presents with altered mental status / agitation. Symptoms are secondary to an intentional ingestion of bupropion. Patient brought by EMS to the ED agitated and hallucinating. He presents with widening of his QRS interval on cardiac monitoring, then develops a generalized tonic-clonic seizure that resolves with appropriate treatment. Patient subsequently develops pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT). Anticipated team actions include primary and secondary surveys, treatment of widened QRS with bicarbonate (although this does not treat QRS widening in bupropion toxicity), magnesium, and/or potassium, treatment of seizure with benzodiazepines, treatment of pulseless VT per PALS (Pediatric Advance Life Support), and rapid sequence intubation. |

**Ideal Scenario Flow**

Participants enter the room to find a patient agitated and muttering. They immediately examine the patient and place him on bedside monitors. Lab work is obtained with insertion of an IV. The participants recognize the widened QRS on monitors and request an ECG, and treat with sodium bicarbonate given the possibility of ingestion. They recognize a change in patient status when he begins having a seizure. Oxygen is placed on the patient and the team begins managing upper airway obstruction with positioning and NP airway. The team gives benzodiazepines for seizure rescue. The seizure resolv**es,** and the team continues to provide airway and respiratory support. The team then recognizes onset of VT with loss of pulses and initiates high-quality CPR, places defibrillation pads, and follows the PALS algorithm for pulseless cardiac arrest by rapidly defibrillating, administering epinephrine and considering additional anti-arrhythmics. The team demonstrates effective teamwork and communication throughout, recognizing changes in the patient’s condition including pulseless VT, ROSC and return of sinus rhythm. The team obtains a definitive airway, and discusses additional treatment of ingestion including anti-arrhythmics and/or fat emulsion. The team continues care after return of spontaneous circulation while handing the patient off to the ICU for ongoing management.

**Anticipated Management Mistakes**

1. Failure to recognize widened QRS and intervene prior to cardiac arrest- We noticed that sometimes there would be a delay in requesting the ECG. If the QRS widening was not clearly visible or noticed on the simulation monitor, participants did not always identify the possibility of an ingestion and take steps to manage the ECG changes. If needed we would prompt participants by stating that a respiratory therapist had arrived to obtain the study, as is routine in resuscitations at our institution.
2. Failure to effectively manage upper airway obstruction with seizure- At times, a provider had not yet assumed the airway role prior to the onset of seizures or the person in that role would not recognize and manage upper airway obstruction. If actions to support oxygenation and ventilation were not made, a facilitator would verbalize that the patient was making snoring noises and taking shallow breaths.
3. Failure to rapidly recognize and manage pulseless ventricular tachycardia- Occasionally there would be a delay in noticing a rhythm change on the monitor as the team was focusing on evaluating and managing seizure activity. During the debrief we would review PALS guidelines and observed metrics including time to first shock, time between pulse checks and time to epinephrine.
