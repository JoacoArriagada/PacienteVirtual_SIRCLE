Training Materials

**Stage			Pre-contemplation**

**Student Goals** 	♦  Qualify Megan’s tobacco use.  Move Megan to contemplation stage by clearly advising her to quit

**♦**  Identify negative consequences of smoking (effect of second hand smoke on Amy, cost)

<u>**Case Summary**</u>

Megan is a 33 year old single mother with a six-month-old daughter. Megan has smoked since she was twelve. She understood the need to quit while pregnant, but never really tried. Now she thinks it’s too late.

Her baby, Amy, was born six months ago and Megan has continued to smoke. She lives with her mother who is also a smoker. The father of the child lives nearby but is minimally involved. Megan works as a waitress in a restaurant/bar where most of her coworkers smoke.

Megan was told to quit smoking during her pregnancy by the obstetrician. The obstetrician said to “quit”, but did not offer information on medications or behavioral counseling. Megan told the obstetrician she had quit, even though she hadn’t, just to get the Dr. off of her back. She even admits to smoking while nursing Amy. Megan knows that her mother smoked during her pregnancies and the entire time Megan was growing up. She does not seem to have suffered any negative consequences as a result of her mother’s behavior, so Megan assumes nothing will happen to Amy if she smokes around her.

In addition, Megan feels that she needs to smoke to deal with the stress of being a single mother. Her income is minimal and the baby’s father only gives her monetary support from time to time. She has few friends and spends most of her free time at home with the baby. She has no hobbies or outside interests and has no intent on furthering her education.

Megan is in the office for the baby’s sixth-month check up. Amy has been diagnosed with an inner ear infection but is otherwise fine. The pediatrician has informed Megan that a medical student will be talking to her about her smoking.

**Your role is to portray a smoker who has very little interest in stopping. She has the mistaken impression that smoking around the baby is not harmful. In addition you feel that you need to smoke to deal with being a single parent.**

<u>**Presentation/Emotional Tone**</u>

Megan is generally respectful and eager to do what is best for her baby. She is quite worried about Amy’s inner ear infection. She is not upset at talking about smoking, however, she is somewhat defensive when discussing smoking/quitting. She has many excuses about why she has to continue to smoke and does not want to be told what to do.

When the student asks why you have come to the office, reply,

**“Amy's doctor said something about how my smoking was causing her ear infections. What does smoking have to with my baby’s ears?”**

<u>**Your Smoking History**</u>

You began smoking when you were twelve and have been a pack-a-day smoker ever since. You never tried to quit even though your obstetrician said you “had to.” You continued to smoke through the entire pregnancy.

Your mother has told you that smoking helps her deal with her problems, so you feel that smoking will help you cope with the stress of being a single mother.

<u>**Family Smoking History**</u>

Mother has smoked two packs a day for over thirty years. Father smokes but is not in the household. Parents are divorced.

Mother’s current boyfriend is a smoker and frequently stays at the house for long periods of time.

Your baby’s father is also a smoker.

<u>**Alcohol/Drug Use**</u>

You use no other drugs and only drink wine coolers occasionally.

<u>**Patient Interview**</u>

If the student begins the interview by answering your question about why the baby has an inner ear infection, he or she should ask how much smoke the baby is exposed to:

**“Well, I smoke. My mom smokes too and we live with her. My mom’s boyfriend, Joe, stays with us a lot and he smokes cigarettes and cigars.”**

He or she should then explain that being exposed to all that second hand smoke significantly increases the risk of such infections. As the student does so, become somewhat defensive,

**“My mother smoked the whole time she was pregnant with me and when I was little. Nothing happened to me. I don’t understand why I can’t smoke around Amy.”**

If the student does not directly answer your initial question ask it again,

**“I just don’t understand why Amy gets these infections. This is the third time it’s happened. What’s going on?”**

If the student warns you of long- term health risks of smoking (ex. Smoking causes heart disease, cancer, and lung disease), say:

**“Amy has already been around it a lot, so there’s nothing I can do now.”**

If the student warns you of short term risks (ex. Bad smell, cost, ear infections for Amy):

**“It’s just something I do to relax; everyone I know does it. Of course I’m concerned about Amy’s ear infections, but I’d be a worse mom because of the stress if I quit”**

If the student directly advises you to quit smoking again ask,

**“I could never do that! First of all, it’s hard not to smoke with my mom and Joe smoking all the time.”**

**“Besides, I work in a bar. Everybody smokes there. Am I supposed to quit my job?”**

If the student advises reducing Amy’s exposure ask,

**“How am I supposed to do that? It’s my mother’s house. I can’t ask her not to smoke in her own house. You’re going to have to tell her that!”**

If the student brings up the nursing issue and advises you not to smoke because you are nursing say,

**“OK, I’ll just stop nursing.”**

Finally agree to **consider** quitting, (not to try), and only if student has not “pushed” you to quit. They can advise only, give you information, and help you to think of reasons to quit. Things that might make you think about quitting are:

Amy’s ear infections and the increased risk of her becoming a smoker in the future

Cost

Lung development of her child

Both of their health in the long run

**“If it’s that important for Amy’s health I’ll think about it.”**

Student should refer you to additional counseling and schedule a follow-up session with you. If they keep pushing, just say:

**“Well, I’m really not convinced**. Or **“Okay, whatever.”**

<u>***Student Should Counsel You on the Following Points***</u>

Exposure to second hand smoke greatly increases the chance of inner ear infections in infants and children.

Smoking in another room or in a part of the house away from the baby does not reduce risk/exposure. The smoke will eventually circulate to the area occupied by the baby. The only safe alternative is to have no smoking whatsoever in the home.

Smoking mothers who nurse transmit nicotine and other harmful chemicals to the baby via their milk.

A nursing mother should stop smoking, not stop nursing.

Just because you had no ill health effects from your mother smoking, does not mean that Amy will not have any ill health effects as a result of your smoking. She is already experiencing problems (inner ear infections).

Smoking around an infant/toddler significantly increases the risk of contracting a whole host of illnesses including SIDS.

Smoking is also risky for the mother.

Smoking is not an effective way to deal with life’s problems.

Student should offer to explain to your mother the importance of not exposing Amy to second hand smoke and devise some equitable way of keeping the home smoke free.
