**CASE 4A**
David Zimmerman,

<u>**Case Summary**</u>

David is a 25 year old male whose father is scheduled for removal of a lung nodule. He is quite concerned about the nodule and is worried that it might be malignant. As such, he has promised his father that he would quit smoking. His father will have the procedure in two weeks. In preparation he has already cut back from two packs a day to about half a pack a day.

David has done this before. He has cut back three other times but never seems to be able to get any lower than half a pack a day. He can maintain that level for a few weeks but eventually increased to his usual two packs a day. David feels that he is more motivated now so that tapering will work. However, he does have some lingering concerns about quitting this way since it did not work in the past.

David smokes mostly in the evenings now, especially when he is studying, because he thinks it helps him concentrate. He also smokes a lot when he goes out to bars with his friends (two or three times a week). His days are quite full as he is involved in many activities.

David is a graduate student at Carnegie Mellon University where he is in a PhD program studying computer science. He is starting to date a woman who hates that he smokes, and has told him that she would never get serious with a smoker. His father has been smoking "for as long as I remember."

**Your role is to portray a smoker who is** <u>**definitely interested in quitting**</u>**. You are quite motivated to quit due to the nodule on your father's lung. You have tried tapering in the past but that method did not work. Now you want the MD to tell you the best way to quit.**

<u>**Presentation/Emotional Tone**</u>

David is a straight forward, no nonsense individual. Since he works with computers he is just interested in concrete facts and is not comfortable with expressing emotions. When he makes up his mind to do something, he just wants to do it. If he doesn’t know something, he looks to experts to teach him the necessary skills and knowledge. He views MDs as very knowledgeable and experts in their field.  He has always been very compliant with MD recommendations.

However, David is scared about his father’s health. He is concerned that the nodule may be malignant. His fear surfaces from time to time.

When the student asks you why you have come to the office today, you reply,

**“When my father told me that he might have lung cancer, I promised him that I would quit smoking. But I’m just not sure how to do it. Can you help me?”**

<u>**Your Smoking History**</u>

You began smoking at age 13 with your friends in eighth grade. Once you started college, you quickly worked your way up to two packs a day and have maintained that amount for the last 5 years. You have tried to cut down several times in the past (after graduation, and most recently when you became interested in a non-smoker). You could get down to around ten a day but could only maintain that for a few weeks. You quickly returned to your two-pack-a-day habit. You have never tried nicotine replacement. You are somewhat reluctant to do so now as you are on a fixed income and feel the products are quite costly.

<u>**Family Smoking History**</u>

Your father smoked for "as long as you remember."

<u>**Drug/Alcohol Use**</u>

You go out to the restaurant/nightclub district regularly with your friends, but have never used any other drugs.

<u>**Medication History**</u>

You are currently taking no medications.

<u>**Patient Interview**</u>

If the students asks you about past quit attempts, you may explain that you tried tapering three times in the past and explain what happened:

**“I can get down to about ten cigarettes a day. That’s tough to get to that point, but I can do it. I really work hard at staying at that level but something always happens. Last time I tried to quit, I had trouble cutting down when I was studying. Without even realizing it, I started to climb right back up to my regular amount (two packs a day). I can concentrate so much better when I smoke.”**

**“I’m down to about ten a day now since I talked to my father. I don’t want the same thing to happen again. What do I do?”**

If the student does not ask you about past attempts, ask,

**“What’s the best way to quit? I can cut down to about ten a day but can’t seem to get past that. Can you give me a plan to follow?”**

The student should advise you to pick a specific quit day and stop altogether on that day, using a smoking cessation medication and making behavioral changes. If the student does not give this advice or something similar, ask,

**“One of my friends quit cold turkey. Should I try that? He didn’t** <u>**do**</u> **anything. He just stopped.”**

If the student asks you when you smoke the most, reply,

**“I smoke a lot when I go out with my friends and when I study in the evening.”**

**“I’m active during the day with classes. When I’m busy I really don’t think about cigarettes. But when I study, I end up smoking one cigarette after another then without even knowing it.”**

**“The problem with going out is that everyone smokes. They are always smoking in front of me or offering me cigarettes.”**

If the student begins to discuss behavioral coping strategies, go along with any reasonable suggestions he or she gives you. If the student does not go in this direction, ask,

**“I can't concentrate if I'm not smoking while I study. What else could I do to help with my concentration?"**

**“What else could I do when I go out? That's the only fun I have, I certainly don’t want to stop.”**

If the student seems stuck on this,

**"Well, now that I think about it, I guess I have other friends who go out but don't smoke. Maybe I'll hang out with them for a while."**

If the student asks you to set a quit date,

**"I really like to enjoy my weekend, so I think I will quit on a Monday."**

**"My fathers’ surgery is two weeks from Monday, so maybe I will quit then as a present for him."**

If the student suggests using a smoking cessation medication, reply,

**“I’m on a fixed income right now. I’m a poor grad student. I’m not so sure that I can afford the medication. Do you really think I need it?”**

**“Besides, I’ve never used anything before. I have to admit I’m a little afraid of them.”**

As you discuss the issue of using a cessation medication, ask,

**“I like the idea of the gum. I know one thing for sure; I’m going to miss having something in my mouth and hands once I quit. Will that make a difference in how effective the gum will be?”**

Go along with whatever reasonable suggestions the student gives you. If they ask about things you could put in your mouth instead of cigarettes,

**"My roommate is a health nut, and is trying to get me to eat more vegetables, that might be something to try."**

Other pertinent questions you may ask during the interview if the occasion presents itself:

**“Do you think it’s ok if I just smoke about five to seven cigarettes a day? Someone told me that smoking less than seven a day isn’t harmful.”**

**“I’m twenty five. Is there really any point in me quitting now? Won't it take decades for the damage to occur?”**
