<u>**Case Summary**</u>

Lindsay is a 23 year old graduate student in public health. She has been smoking for ten years, since age 13. She is going on the Semester At Sea program and has just learned that she cannot smoke on the ship. Lindsay wants to go on the trip very much, but doesn't think she will be able to because of the smoking rules. The only reason she came to the appointment today was that she had made the appointment before she relapsed, and figured she may as well keep it. She is not interested in quitting now, but admits she would really like to go on the four-month trip. She does not want the hassle of trying to sneak all over the ship to find a place to smoke. Besides, her roommate on the trip, an old friend, is an adamant nonsmoker and will not allow her to sneak cigarettes in the room.

Knowing that she could not smoke on this trip, Lindsay quit smoking last week. She only made it three days before relapsing.

Lindsay has always been rather quiet and reserved. She frequently has to be prompted to speak when in groups and describes herself as “shy.” She rarely makes eye contact when speaking and generally prefers to be alone.

Lindsay is coming to the office for the physical required by all SAS students. She has no physical complaints and the exam proved routine. She currently takes oral birth control pills but no other medications.

**Your role is to portray a** <u>**relapsed**</u> **smoker who cannot quit because she becomes depressed every time she tries. You have a strong belief that smoking “completes you.” You also believe that you don’t have the wherewithal to quit because you have tried many times in the past and have failed.**

<u>**Presentation/Emotional Tone**</u>

Lindsay presents as a very quiet, shy, young lady. She avoids eye contact during most of the conversation and has to be prompted to answer most questions. She rarely smiles and has a rather flat affect.

When the student asks why you have come to the office, you reply,

**“I made this appointment a few weeks ago to have a physical to go on the Semester At Sea program, but I don't think I will go. I figured I may as well get the physical anyway because I was feeling yucky, since I tried to quit smoking last week."**

**"We are not allowed to smoke on the ship. I quit last week so I could go, but I started smoking again a few days ago. That’s about the tenth time that’s happened to me.”**

**“I think I will cancel the trip since I can’t seem to quit. I’m just too weak.”**

<u>**Your Smoking History**</u>

You began smoking at age 13. From the start, you noticed that you felt better when you smoked. You seemed to have a little more energy and just felt “more complete” as you’ve described it. You smoke about fifteen cigarettes a day pretty much evenly spaced throughout the day.

You have tried to quit five or six times prompted by many different things: information about the ill health effects you learned in 11th grade health class, a boyfriend who did not like smoking, a bout of bronchitis, etc.

Every time you have quit in the past you have become moody and cried at the drop of a hat. You become lethargic and lose interest in many of your regular activities. You also have trouble sleeping and feel “just yucky.” The longest you have ever gone without smoking is five days when you had bronchitis. When you can’t deal with the feelings anymore, you have returned to smoking. Within a day or so you notice that you feel better.

This pattern repeated itself last week when you tried to quit. You are now angry with yourself and feel quite defeated. You are close to canceling the trip because you feel that you are not going to be able to quit and you know the trip will be difficult if you try to smoke.

You have never used any type of smoking cessation medication. In fact, you know nothing about any of the products.

<u>**Family Smoking History**</u>

Both your mother and father smoke, as well as two of your three siblings. Your mother is 55 and is currently diagnosed with chronic bronchitis and your father is 60. He has no current medical problems. You still live at home as do your siblings.

No one in the family is very supportive of you quitting. Smoking is a “family” thing and no one else has the slightest interest in quitting. In fact, this last quit attempt was complicated by the fact that your 18-year-old brother kept offering you cigarettes just to see if he could tempt you.

<u>**Drug/ Alcohol Use**</u>

You drink quite a bit on weekends (six to eight beers) but nothing during the week. You have used Ecstasy two or three times at parties but really did not enjoy the experience.

<u>**Patient Interview**</u>

If the student begins by asking you to define “yucky” you may describe the following symptoms but *do not use the word depressed.* Speak slowly and quietly and avoid looking directly at the student.

**Lethargic, moody, easy to cry, sleepy, loss of interest in everyday activities.**

You may also describe yourself as a **failure** (“**I just can’t seem to quit…I’ve probably tried fifteen times”)** and **weak (“I’m just not strong enough to quit”).**

If the student asks you to describe what happened during your last quit attempt, reiterate the following but *avoid using the word depressed*. Speak slowly and quietly and avoid looking directly at the student.

**“I became very lethargic and moody. I seemed to be crying all the time even over silly things. I couldn’t find the TV remote and started to cry. Besides, by the second day, I was very sleepy and could barely get out of bed in the morning. I just seemed to lose interest in everything. I felt completely yucky.”**

If the student asks you, <u>directly</u>, if you have ever been depressed, say,

**“Just a little. But I feel that way more when I quit smoking. I get sad then and don’t feel like doing anything. When I start to smoke again I feel better.”**

If you are asked if you have ever been <u>diagnosed</u> with depression, say,

“**No but my mom was at Western Psychiatric for a week years ago. We were told she was depressed. I think she takes something now but she doesn't talk about it, and I don’t know what it is.”**

**“I just get really down whenever I try to quit. I just think I’m a weak person.”**

In either case, ask the student,

**“What makes you think I’m depressed? I’m fine when I smoke. I only have these feeling when I quit.”**

If the student asks if you have ever taken an antidepressant simply say, **“No.”**

If the student questions you at all about suicide attempts or thoughts, reply,

“**Oh no, I’m never that down. I just get tired and lose interest in things. I’ve never thought about hurting myself.”**

If the student asks you if you are interested in quitting now,

**"No, I really don’t feel up to it right now."**

If the student asks you about smoking cessation medication use, reply,

**“What’s that? I don’t know what you are talking about.”**

The student may suggest that you take Zyban at this point. If he or she begins discussing this option, question the student about side effects, dosing, etc. Be somewhat hesitant about taking it but finally agree.

**“What’s it going to do to me? How does it help you stop smoking?”**

If the student says nothing about medication use bring up the subject yourself,

**“Is there anything I can take to help me quit? I saw an ad on TV for this pill. Do you think that might help? ”**

If the student discusses behavioral support/coping strategies, ask,

**“I really think I’m going to need lots of support to do this. And my family isn’t going to help…all of them smoke. Can you refer me to a group or something like that?”**

**“If I need to talk to you again, can I call you?”**

**“What should I do on the weekends when I want to go out and have some drinks? You know, everywhere you go people smoke. I don’t want to sit at home for the rest of my life or never have another drink.”**

**“Besides, what’ll I do when I’m home and see everyone smoking? I don’t think I can handle that.”**

<u>***Student Should Counsel You on the Following Points***</u>

It appears that you may be mildly depressed and that smoking helps to relieve your symptoms just enough that you feel ok.

When you have quit in the past, you slip into a mild depression and feel badly. You then go back to smoking to feel better.

Your inability to quit has nothing to do with you being “weak.” It has to do with the withdrawal symptoms you experience.

Smoking is not a good way to deal with these symptoms.

There is a smoking cessation medication, Zyban, which is indicated for people who are mildly depressed. If you use this medication you should be able to quit smoking successfully.

Student should be supportive and encourage you to quit again.

Student should refer you to a behavioral support program and offer to do some type of personal follow-up.

Student should offer suggestions about what to do about being with other smokers and dealing with urges while drinking. If student does not offer specific suggestions, he or she should generally address these issues and direct you to resources to learn targeted coping techniques.
