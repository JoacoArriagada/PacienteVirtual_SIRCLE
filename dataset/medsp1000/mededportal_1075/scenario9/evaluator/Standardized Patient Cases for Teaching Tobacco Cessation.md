<u>**Case Summary**</u>

Joanne is a 55 year old executive for a major department store in the city. She has been smoking for over forty years but recently learned through her human resources department that smoking significantly increases health care costs for her company. As an example for the rest of the company and to encourage her employees to stop, she has now decided to quit. She has quit several times in the past but each time returned to smoking due to some major stressor, usually at work.

Currently she has been quit for over two months. Her quitting has already prompted five other coworkers to stop. She is very pleased with herself and her boss has already hinted that she will be in line for a year-end bonus because of this initiative. However, she is doubtful that she can stay quit, as she has relapsed every other time she has quit.

Joanne was in the office to discuss the results of her yearly physical. Everything was fine except for the pulmonary function tests she had done last week. They show that she has mild COPD. She is quite upset about the diagnosis and cannot understand why her lung functions are so low. She claims to have barely inhaled when she smoked and she always smoked Ultra Lights. She tries to blame the COPD on the fact that she grew up near the mills here in Pittsburgh.

The news upset her so much that she actually thought about smoking again. However, she talked herself out of it. She feels there is too much at stake at work for her to return to smoking. This fits her prior quitting/relapse pattern. She is fine until some unexpected stress hits.

Joanne has a very high stress job but has never learned any worthwhile stress management techniques. She never exercises and rarely walks anywhere. She tried going to a health club (her company would pay for it) but was intimidated by “all the young bodies.” She has never studied any relaxation, meditation, or visualization techniques.

**Your role is to portray a recently quit smoker who has successfully** <u>**maintained**</u> **her quit for two months. But she is concerned about how she will deal with sudden stress. She feels somewhat vulnerable now, as she just discovered that she has mild COPD. She mistakenly believes that smoking helped her deal with stress.**

<u>**Presentation/Emotional Tone**</u>

Joanne presents as a well-educated, personable, corporate executive. She is confident and in command. However, the news about the COPD unnerved her somewhat and she now has some increased anxiety about being able to stay quit. She admits to not being able to deal with sudden stress.

When the student asks why you have come of the office say,

**“I just quit smoking two months ago. Everything was fine until I found out last week that I had mild COPD. That got me so upset that I thought about starting to smoke again. But I didn’t. I’ve relapsed in the past when I got into stressful situations. Can you prescribe Xanax for me to help me deal with the stress?”**

<u>**Your Smoking History**</u>

You began smoking in your early twenty’s while attending business school at Penn State. You felt it was very chic to smoke and that smoking fit your image of a career woman. However, you never smoked on the street and not in front of certain relatives. You only smoked when you were with your work colleagues. In fact, some of your family has never known that you smoked.

You smoked Ultra Lights for the last fifteen years as you thought they were “safer.”

As more and more of the health warnings were published you became convinced that you should quit. You have been successful for years at a time, but every time some sudden major stress occurred you went back (your boss died unexpectedly, the department store was bought by a conglomerate, and you feared for your job). The longest you have been quit is three years.

Now, you are increasingly aware of the increased costs smoking brings to corporate health care. Since you are a high ranking executive and lead by example, you felt this was the time to quit for good.

You used the new lozenge and found them to be quite effective. You stopped using them after the recommended usage period. You also enrolled in the CQ program that accompanied the lozenge and made many of the behavioral changes that were suggested.

However, when you got the news about the COPD you felt very stressed and started to think about smoking again. The only thing that kept you from relapsing is the embarrassment you would experience at work. You feel you need some help to maintain the quit.

<u>**Family Smoking History**</u>

Both parents smoked and both died of emphysema, your mother at age 66 and your father at age 70. Your husband has never smoked. He is very supportive of your quit attempt.

<u>**Drug/Alcohol Use**</u>

You drink socially especially while entertaining clients. You do so at least once a week and usually have two or three Cosmopolitans during client dinners. You have never used any other drugs.

<u>**Medication History**</u>

You have taken Xanax in the past to help you deal with anxiety/stress but have not used it for more than a month at a time. You currently do not have an active prescription.

<u>**Exercise History**</u>

Although your company will pay your health club membership, you have not taken advantage of this perk. You are intimidated by the idea of going into a health club with lots of young, well built individuals. You used to play tennis and golf but have not done so in years due to work demands.

<u>**Patient Interview**</u>

If the student asks you how you have dealt with stress or if you’ve taken Xanax in the past, answer,

**“I have always smoked to deal with stress. That helped a lot. It gave me the time to think things out and then make a decision. I don’t know what else to do other than smoke.”**

**“I did take Xanax a few times in the past especially for the month after my boss died suddenly. That helped but I don’t want to take it all the time.”**

**“Can you suggest anything else I could do?”**

If the student suggests going to a stress management class or meditation program, agree with that.

**“My friend does meditation and yoga. Do you think yoga would be good? There’s a new studio near where I live. They are having a free introductory offer. My friend has invited me to join her. What do you think?”**

If the student suggests exercise say,

**“I’m really reluctant to go to a gym. All those young, good-looking people intimidate me. Besides I don’t have the time to exercise.”**

**“What else could I do besides going to a gym?”**

If the student suggests some other form of exercise, reply,

**“I used to play tennis when I was younger. Do you think if I start again that might help?”**

If the student prescribes Xanax, accept the prescription. If not, ask for it again. If he or she does not want to give you a prescription, ask why.

At some point, if the student has not explained the difference between the mistaken idea that there is some chemical in cigarettes that gets rid of stress and the real behavioral aspects inherent in the <u>act</u> of smoking that actually do prompt relaxation, ask,

**“Doctor, why does smoking get rid of stress? What is the chemical in cigarettes that calms you? Is that what nicotine does? I’ve done some reading and I’m confused about this issue.”**

Throughout the interview reiterate your concern about relapsing if something really stressful happens in the future.

**“Is there someone I could call if I get especially stressed?  I know that if I talk to someone right in the moment, I could get through it.”**

<u>***Student Should Counsel You on the Following Points***</u>

Smoking does not get rid of stress: it causes it. (The “hit”/withdrawal cycle)

Withdrawal (irritability, anxiousness, restlessness and impatience) feels like stress. Smoking gets rid of withdrawal. Smokers mix up relieving withdrawal with getting rid of stress.

There is no ingredient in a cigarette that will get rid of stress or calm the smoker.

Most ex-smokers report feeling calmer and less stressed than smokers.

There are many healthy alternatives to help one deal with stress. The student should recommend or refer you to something:

Formal Exercise

Getting Active—Walking, Tennis, Golf, etc

Stress Management/Meditation Classes

Talking to friends/relatives/clergy

Positive self-talk/visualization

Visualization techniques

Relapse prevention techniques: Anticipate/Plan/Rehearse

Student should set up some type of follow up with you.
