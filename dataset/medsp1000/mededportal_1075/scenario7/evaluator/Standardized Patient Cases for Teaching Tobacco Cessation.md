<u>**Case Summary**</u>

David is a 65 year old male who has been referred to the internist prior to a thorascopic removal of a lung nodule. He is quite concerned about the nodule and is worried that it might be malignant. As such, he has decided that he should quit smoking. He will have the procedure in two weeks. In preparation he has already cut back from two packs a day to about half a pack a day.

David has done this before. He has cut back three other times but never seems to be able to get any lower than half a pack a day. He can maintain that level for a few weeks but eventually increased to his usual two packs a day. David feels that he is more motivated now so that tapering will work. However, he does have some lingering concerns about quitting this way since it did not work in the past.

David smokes mostly in the evenings now, especially when he is relaxing at home watching T.V. He also smokes a lot while golfing (two or three times a week). His days are quite full as he is involved in many activities. However, his evenings are relatively empty.

David recently retired from Carnegie Mellon University where he ran the computer labs. He is considering moving to Myrtle Beach to be nearer his daughter and grandchildren. He has a good relationship with his wife and family. No one else in the family smokes. His wife is very supportive of him quitting. She has told him that she will help in whatever way she can.

**Your role is to portray a smoker who is** <u>**definitely interested in quitting**</u>**. You are quite motivated to quit due to the nodule on your lung. You also have exciting retirement plans and want to be healthy to achieve those plans. You have tried tapering in the past but that method did not work. Now you want the MD to tell you the best way to quit.**

<u>**Presentation/Emotional Tone**</u>

David is a straight forward, no nonsense individual. Since he worked with computers he is just interested in concrete facts and is not comfortable with expressing emotions. When he makes up his mind to do something, he just wants to do it. If he doesn’t know something, he looks to experts to teach him the necessary skills and knowledge. He views MDs as very knowledgeable and experts in their field.  He has always been very compliant with MD recommendations.

However, David is scared. He is concerned that the nodule may be malignant. His fear surfaces from time to time.

When the student asks you why you have come to the office today, you reply,

**“After they told me that I had a nodule in my lung I thought I’d better quit smoking. But I’m just not sure how to do it. Can you help me?”**

<u>**Your Smoking History**</u>

You began smoking at age 13 with your friends in eighth grade. You quickly worked your way up to two packs a day and have maintained that amount for the last 50 years. You have tried to cut down several times in the past (when your daughter was born and in your early fifties when you started to notice you were “slowing down”). You could get down to around ten a day but could only maintain that for a few weeks. You quickly returned to your two-pack-a-day habit. You have never tried nicotine replacement. You are somewhat reluctant to do so now as you are on a fixed income and feel the products are quite costly.

<u>**Family Smoking History**</u>

No one else in the family smokes. Your wife and daughter are very supportive of you quitting.

<u>**Drug/Alcohol Use**</u>

You rarely drink (a glass of wine here or there) and have never used any other drugs.

<u>**Medication History**</u>

You are currently taking Lisinopril, Lipitor and Viagra.

<u>**Patient Interview**</u>

If the students asks you about past quit attempts, you may explain that you tried tapering three times in the past and explain what happened:

**“I can get down to about ten cigarettes a day. That’s tough to get to that point, but I can do it. I really work hard at staying at that level but something always happens. Last time I tried to quit, I got bored at night just sitting watching TV. Without even realizing it, I started to climb right back up to my regular amount (two packs a day).”**

**“I’m down to about ten a day now since I’m going to have surgery. I don’t want the same thing to happen again. What do I do?”**

If the student does not ask you about past attempts, ask,

**“What’s the best way to quit? I can cut down to about ten a day but can’t seem to get past that. Can you give me a plan to follow?”**

The student should advise you to pick a specific quit day and stop altogether on that day, using a smoking cessation medication and making behavioral changes. If the student does not give this advice or something similar, ask,

**“One of my friends quit cold turkey. Should I try that? He didn’t** <u>**do**</u> **anything. He just stopped.”**

If the student asks you when you smoke the most, reply,

**“I smoke a lot when I play golf and in the evening.”**

**“I’m active during the day. When I’m busy I really don’t think about cigarettes. But in the evenings, I tend to just sit and watch T.V. at night. I end up smoking one cigarette after another then without even knowing it.”**

**“The problem with golfing is that two of the other guys smoke. They are always smoking in front of me or offering me cigarettes.”**

If the student begins to discuss behavioral coping strategies go along with any reasonable suggestions he or she gives you. If the student does not go in this direction, ask,

**“What else could I do in the evenings? I can’t seem to think of anything else to do but watch TV and smoke.”**

**“What else could I do when I golf? I certainly don’t want to stop.”**

If the student seems stuck on this,

**"Well, not that I think about it, I guess I have other friends who golf but don't smoke. Maybe I'll call them and see if I can join their group."**

If the student asks you to set a quit date,

**"I really like to enjoy my weekend, so I think I will quit on a Monday."**

**"Two weeks from Monday is a good time, that should give me enough time to get ready."**

If the student suggests using a smoking cessation medication, reply,

**“I’m on a fixed income right now. I’m retired. I’m not so sure that I can afford the medication. Do you really think I need it?”**

**“Besides, I’ve never used anything before. I have to admit I’m a little afraid of them.”**

As you discuss the issue of using a cessation medication, ask,

**“I like the idea of the gum. I know one thing for sure; I’m going to miss having something in my mouth and hands once I quit. Will that make a difference in how effective the gum will be?”**

(The best method is one that the patient will use, so if the gum makes sense to you, they should encourage you to use it.)

Go along with whatever reasonable suggestions the student gives you. If they ask about things you could put in your mouth instead of cigarettes,

**"My wife is trying to get me to eat more vegetables, that might be something to try."**

*Student should offer to develop an individualized quitting plan with you.* You should discuss the following elements:

Review prior quit attempts to pinpoint problem areas

Set a specific quit date

Reinforce motivations

Strategize about how to break down barriers

Discuss behavioral coping strategies

Review medication use

Schedule follow-up

Other pertinent questions you may ask during the interview if the occasion presents itself:

**“Do you think it’s ok if I just smoke about five to seven cigarettes a day? Someone told me that smoking less than seven a day isn’t harmful.”**

**“I’m sixty five. Is there really any point in me quitting now? Isn’t the damage already done?”**

<u>***Student Should Counsel You on the Following Points***</u>

In general, tapering is not a good way to quit smoking. Just as in your case, most people cannot get below a certain number before they begin to escalate again.

In order to quit successfully, a smoker must break the physical addiction as well as the behavioral connections that trigger urges. The best way to do that is to stop completely on a specific day.

Then the quitter should combine a smoking cessation medication with a behavior modification program. Research shows that this is the best way to quit smoking.

Concrete behavioral suggestions as to how you could deal with urges in the evening and while golfing. If no specific suggestions are made, the student should make reference to where you can get this information.

If you discuss the cost of cessation medications, the student should advise you that the short-term cost of any of the products far outweighs the long-term costs of continued smoking. The average smoker in PA spends about \$2,000 a year on cigarettes. The average cost of a course of medication is \$200 or less.

Quitting at any age is beneficial. Research done on smokers in the seventies and eighties shows definite health benefits to quitting even at that age.

There is no proof that smoking just a few cigarettes a day is safe. The only safe number of cigarettes is zero!

**Note: Smoking is one of the leading causes of impotence in men. Since you are already on Viagra, the student should mention this fact to you. Quitting could possibly reverse any problems you are experiencing.**
