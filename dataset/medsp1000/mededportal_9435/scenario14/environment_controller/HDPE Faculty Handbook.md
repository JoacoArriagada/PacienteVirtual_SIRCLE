# Context

The set of complaints, differential diagnoses, and clinical scenarios contained in this handbook were developed as part of a hypothesis-driven approach to assessing the complete, head-to-toe physical exam in order to better integrate the technical and cognitive aspects of the physical examination. In a nutshell, the students are asked to learn the physical exam maneuvers in the context of chief complaints, limited differential diagnoses, and discriminating features. When tested on their physical exam skills, the students are presented with a short history and an accompanying differential diagnosis consisting of two alternative diagnoses; the history is kept short because the emphasis here is on anticipating and performing a physical exam with specific discriminating signs in mind. Their task is then:

a) To anticipate the positive physical findings for each diagnosis in the differential and identify the discriminating findings between the diagnoses;   
b) To perform the physical exam maneuvers on a patient instructor exhibiting certain positive findings (Patient instructors are highly trained standardized patients who not only simulate clinical findings but also provide instruction to the students when needed, for example, correcting a poorly or incorrectly executed physical exam maneuver);   
c) To interpret the findings by proposing a most likely diagnosis;   
d) To correct any physical exam maneuvers, as prompted by the patient instructor if needed, that were incorrectly executed or omitted initially and revise their working diagnosis if needed; and   
e) To document the findings associated with their physical exam.

The approach is intended for medical students during their first encounter with the physical exam. For an introductory course to the physical exam, we selected 160 physical exam maneuvers that were grouped according to 19 chief complaints (e.g., blurred vision, shortness of breath) and 60 distinct diagnoses. Typically, there are three to four differential diagnoses for each chief complaint. The main purpose is to learn the maneuvers in the context of a differential diagnosis and discriminating features. Special attention was paid to include frequent or prototypical diagnoses. As part of their learning experience, the students are given, up front, the diagnoses and the list of maneuvers associated with each complaint (see Hypothesis-driven Physical Exam - Student Handbook). Then, on their own as a homework assignment, they look up the maneuvers associated with each diagnosis and identify the discriminating findings associated with various pairs of diagnoses. Although the chief complaints may refer initially to a specific anatomical region (e.g., the neck or lungs), other anatomical structures and systems may be involved as dictated by the differential diagnosis. For example, if the student is examining the lungs for congestive heart failure, the neck (JVD) and the lower limbs (pedal edema) are also included in that chief complaint. The maneuvers and findings associated with two diagnoses accompany each clinical scenario in this document. These are provided simply as examples; other scenarios and pairs of diagnoses are possible.

# How to use this handbook

In the scenarios provided as examples in this handbook, the diagnosis that is portrayed by the patient instructor is highlighted in bold letters. For the sake of brevity in the tables that accompany each scenario, the description of each maneuver was abbreviated. For a more detailed description of each maneuver, see standard textbooks of physical examination such as Bates, DeGowen, Novey, Schwartz, or Sapira (see references at the end of this section). To ensure standardization, Novey’s Rapid Access Guide to Physical Examination (1992) is used as the reference document by the students and instructors across sites at the University of Illinois at Chicago College of Medicine.

The following acronyms accompany certain maneuvers:

- (Sim) indicates items simulated by the patient instructor;   
(Mod) indicates items simulated with the use of a simulator or model that accompanies the patient instructor when the patient instructor is unable to simulate the finding (e.g., an Eye-head model for certain fundoscopic findings or a Lung-thorax simulator for lung sounds);   
(D) and yellow shading indicate a discriminating finding for the set of diagnoses used in the example scenario.

Some maneuvers can generate more than one sign (designated by the prefix S or SA); for example “MA20: Palpate both ankles” can generate two signs in the case of infectious arthritis namely, “SA20a: Diffuse tenderness both lateral & medial side of the ankle” and “SA20b: Ankle warm to touch.” In that case, the signs have the same number as the maneuver with an added lettered suffix (e.g., SA20a, SA20b…).

2- At examination time:

(a) Before seeing the patient instructor, have the student read the short clinical scenario and anticipate the positive findings for each diagnosis using an openended question. The answers can be captured either on paper or through a computer keyboard (e.g., using WebSP $\textsuperscript { \textregistered }$ )

(b) Then the student enters the exam room and examines the PI. When the student performs a maneuver incorrectly, the PI does not give the sign.

(c) When a simulator (model) is used, the student first examines the PI in its entirety and, when finished with the PI, goes to the simulator

(d) Following the encounter, the student documents his/her findings.

Because the students are told that all patient findings are to be considered in their examination, it is important to check each PI prior to each exam for unintended findings they may have that are not related to the case.
