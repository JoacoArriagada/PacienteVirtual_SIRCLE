5. If Problem Recognized and critical actions taken: (outline chain of events as patient improves)

o 2 Large bore IV Peripherally
o Start Bolus of Normal Saline IVF Bolus for hypotension while awaiting blood products
o Type and Screen & Labs (CBC & Coags) o Cross for Blood
o Rectal Exam & NG Tube placement (Bright red blood from NGT)
o Give unmatched blood if patient unstable
o IV Meds: o IV Octreotide - Octreotide is a vasoactive protein that causes selective vasoconstriction of the splanchnic vasculature. Blood flow to the upper GI tract is decreased thereby reducing blood loss. It is given as a bolus followed by a drip. o IV Proton Pump Inhibitor - decrease the acidity of the stomach and enhance clot stability
o Emergent Gastroenterology and Surgical Consult
o Consider pressors if patient still hypotensive despite IVF and Blood Transfusions

6. If Problem Not Recognized or critical actions not taken: (outline chain of events as patient worsens) - No change in patient status/vital signs. If learner does not take appropriate actions, will be prompted by the facilitator/faculty.

# 7. Teaching Points:

o Hypovolemic Shock- Early recognition of hypovolemic shock and its vital sign presentations. Need for 2 large bore IV insertion for below teaching point.
o Volume Resuscitation in a GI Bleed- Normal Saline or LR initially for fluid resuscitation, need for blood products (including the use of uncrossed O- blood initially) very quickly once it's recognized the hypovolemia is secondary to blood loss.
o Blood Transfusion and Consent- Proper techniques for verbal consent to give blood products (risks and benefits) should be emphasized with the students. Issues such as patient refusal, capacity for decision, next of kin or health care proxy will be discussed, as well, during the debriefing period.

# Upper GI Bleed Differential:

A)Mallory Weis

Mallory-Weiss syndrome is UGI bleeding secondary to a longitudinal mucosal tear in the cardioesophageal region. The classic history is repeated vomiting followed by bright red hematemesis, but extreme Valsalva maneuvers, such as from coughing or seizures also have been reported as causes.

B) Boerhaave's Full Thickness Esophageal Perforation

C) Peptic Ulcer Disease

Peptic ulcer disease, including gastric, duodenal, esophageal, and stomal ulcers, is the commonest cause of UGI bleeding.

D) Erosive Gastritis and Esophagitis

Erosive gastritis, esophagitis, and duodenitis together are responsible for approximately $1 3 \%$ of all cases of UGI hemorrhage. Predisposing factors include alcohol, salicylates, and NSAIDs.

E) Esophageal and Gastric Varices

Esophageal and gastric varices result from portal hypertension and, in the U.S., are most often a result of alcoholic liver disease. Although varices account for only about $7 \%$ of all cases of UGI hemorrhage, they are most likely to rebleed and carry a $1 6 \%$ mortality rate. Nevertheless, many patients with end-stage cirrhosis never develop varices, many patients with documented varices never bleed, and many patients with a documented history of varices presenting with UGI bleeding will actually be bleeding from nonvariceal sites.

# F) Others:

Stress ulcer, arteriovenous malformation, and malignancy are other causes of UGI hemorrhage. Ear, nose, and throat sources of bleeding can also masquerade as GI hemorrhage. An aortoenteric fistula secondary to a preexisting aortic graft is an unusual but important cause of bleeding to keep in mind. Classically, this presents as a self-limited "herald" bleed, which precedes massive hemorrhage.
