| PHYSICAL EXAM | |
| Students may perform a focused physical exam. They may listen to the SP’s heart on the right and left of sternum/breast bone and at the lower left of sternum/breast bone (4 places). They may listen to lungs on the left and right of chest and on the upper left, lower left, upper right, and lower right on the back (2+4). No action is required of the SP as findings should be normal.  Students may assess cognitive function. Students may ask for name, current location, and date/time – or may go into more detail (such as current president, etc…); students may assess ability to follow multiple step commands. In response to current location, the SP should state “hospital” rather than specific name of hospital. The SP is oriented to person (full name) and month/year but unable to recall specific date or day of the week. If more specific questions are asked, the SP may recall some but not all (this may be due to inability to express speech versus cognitive impairments). The SP has difficulty following 3 step commands (can remember two of the three) (i.e. lift your right arm, touch your nose, and bring your hand back to your lap). Speech is mildly slurred. The patient has some word-finding difficulty (e.g. can’t remember name for clock, so describes it as “the thing that tells time”).  Students may assess cranial nerve function. They may assess pupil responses to light; students may do an oculomotor exam (ask patient to follow finger); test facial expressions (raise eyebrows, close eyes, purse lips, smile); finger rub to ear (gross test of hearing); stick out tongue; elevate shoulders to resistance or turn head and resist to manual pressure. The SP should respond normally to all tests while being mindful of right-sided weakness (i.e. difficulty raising right arm).  Students may assess sensory function. This involves light touch (with finger) or sharp/dull with instrument; they may also perform proprioceptive testing of big toe or finger (all done with eyes closed, multiple times). Proprioceptive testing will involve moving toe/finger up or down with eyes closed. The patient will be asked to report position of toe/finger or indicate when they feel light touch (may also be asked to localize where); for sharp dull will be asked to report “sharp” or “dull”. For the SP, proprioceptive testing will be intact in the legs, but has difficulty sensing sharp and dull touch on the right side of face and arm. No neglect present (able to feel when both sides are touched at the same time). Students may draw a letter on the palm of the SP’s hand. The SP can determine what the letter is without difficulty.  Students may assess the SP’s skin for abnormalities. All is normal and no action required of the SP.  Students may assess coordination. This may involve finger to nose (patient touches nose with their index finger and then rapidly touches the examiner’s finger held in front of the patient). With right hand, rather than touching the tip of finger to the tip of nose, the SP will only be able to place their entire finger across the nose. Students may also ask the SP to perform rapid alternating movements (moving hands into supination/pronation- this means that patient may be asked to hold arm at 90 degree angle and turn forearm so palm is facing up and then down). Rapid alternating movements with right arm will be slower than left and deliberate. In addition, the SP will lose tempo with the right side (e.g. asymmetric movement with left). Students may also ask the SP to perform heel to shin movements (asking the patient to place their heel to their opposite shin and move up and down as quickly as possible). The heel to shin activity with right leg will be a slower than left side.  Students may test reflexes using a reflex hammer on various sites of arm and leg. The SP should be deliberate in making reflexes less pronounced on right side.  Students may assess range of motion. They may passively and then actively assess flexibility of arm and leg at all joints. They may use goniometer to measure deficient ranges. The SP is unable to fully move right arm throughout available range due to weakness (e.g., would be able to move arm freely when guided by student, but when asked to perform this alone, the SP is unable to move through the same range due to weakness). The SP will be weaker in right hand/wrist and elbow than shoulder. The SP’s right leg is able to move within same approximate range as left leg (only minimal weakness of right leg as it has gotten much better since initial presentation)  Students may assess the SP’s strength by manually applying resistance to arms and legs at various locations (therapist will be testing major muscle groups at each joint). The SP’s left arm and leg have full strength. The right leg has just mild weakness at the ankle (give way when moderate resistance is applied) and very mild strength limitations at the knee (give way when maximal resistance is applied); right hip has full strength. On the other hand, the right arm is weak at hand and wrist (able to move but unable to resist any manual pressure); able to resist minimal pressure at the elbow and shoulder. There are 2 types of strength assessments: (1) the SP may be asked to hold a position while resistance is applied and the student pushes against it or (2) the SP may be asked to push against the students’ resistance.  Students may assess motor control and functional mobility through specific analysis of bed mobility (rolling left and right; moving from supine [lying down] to sit and back to supine), transfers (moving to/from sit to stand), and walking/ambulation (with/without a device such as walker or cane).  For Bed Mobility:  • The SP is able to roll to right and left side of bed without assistance although may have to grab right arm with left due to weakness. Or, when patient attempts to use right arm to push up off the bed, the patient realizes the weakness in that side and uses left arm to push up instead. The patient is able to sit on edge of bed with close supervision/contact guard (hands on but no assistance) of therapist .  For Transfers:  • The patient is able to stand (albeit more slowly than normal) with assistance from therapist.  For Walking:  • The therapist may or may not use an assistive device (such as a cane or rolling walker); with a rolling walker, the patient is able to walk with contact guard of the therapist; with a cane, the patient would need minimal assistance initially but progresses to contact guard after walking for a few feet; if walking without a device, the SP would need hand held assistance of the therapist (so minimal assistance throughout the walk with patient bearing some weight through the therapist’s hand). After walking for a few feet, the SP will exhibit fatigue of right leg resulting in occasional toe drag and slight knee buckling when weight bearing.  Students may assess functional mobility/transfers (from sit to stand and transfer to bedside chair). The SP is able to stand with therapist close but needs some help from the student to place the right hand in weight bearing on right knee as the SP begins to stand up. The SP can pivot to a chair with minimal assistance.  Students may assess the motor control of the SP’s upper extremity/trunk. The SP sits in a posterior pelvic tilt (rounded hips) position with more weight distributed to the right buttock. Essentially, this means that the SP tends to sit with more weight on his/her right side; shoulders are hunched forward. Even after the student straightens the SP, the SP slumps back into this position. With hands-on facilitation from the therapist, the SP can sit taller, more upright, and with more symmetry. When asked to move arms in a total pattern (e.g., moving at shoulder), the SP has some right side incoordination. The SP has difficulty opening the right hand, but less difficulty closing it. Students may ask the SP to do the “sword to pizza” movement where the SP makes a fist and reaches the right arm across and down to left side of body (as if retrieving a sword) and then opens and flattens palm of hand while holding it over right shoulder as if holding a pizza tray). The SP is not coordinated enough to do this with the right arm.  Students may assess sensation of the upper extremity. The SP can feel light touch in the right hand but not always able to accurately localize/tell where the touch is. The SP is able to identify direction of movement (e.g., the SP can differentiate if the students move one of their fingers/wrist up or down).  Students may assess visual perception/attention. The SP is able to look to midline and cross midline in functional tasks. The SP is able to retrieve items from background (e.g., able to pick up a white washcloth that is on a white towel). The SP can accurately place blocks or bedside items in response to command; there may be some weakness in doing so but conceptual understanding is intact.  Students may perform a swallowing assessment. With some light postural (upper and lower back) help, the SP is able to sit upright, and if asked to take a bite of food, the SP would be able to do that with much hand over hand assist with the right upper extremity. It would take the SP longer than normal to chew (e.g. the SP has to think about this formerly automatic task). The SP can take a sip of drinking water from a cup, but should allow a bit of water to dribble from right side of mouth due to persistent sensory deficits. The SP would need help from the therapist to hold the cup and coordinate the process of getting the cup to mouth. The SP will initiate cup and spoon use with the dominant, right, albeit weaker side.  Students may assess the SP’s ability to perform activities of daily living (self-feeding, grooming, hygiene). The SP cannot incorporate the right hand, without help from therapist. | |
