For Standardized Patient

**Standardized Patient (SP) Instructions:**

**Presentation:**

You are Pat Franklin, a 43-year-old. You were eating a snack yesterday, when you suddenly felt weak and numb on the right side of your body. Your spouse walked in the room while you were eating the snack and noticed you were uncomfortable. Your speech was slurred and your spouse decided to bring you to the emergency department. Around 4pm, you arrived at the emergency room.

Overall, you are cooperative but have a depressed mood. You are lying in the bed in a hospital gown and attached to the monitors. You keep hearing the doctors coming in and out. They speak about your movements and speech. You are scared of what happened. You are unsure whether you will ever be “back to normal”. You are concerned about what this incident will mean for your future, your mobility, your spouse, and your job. You are right-handed.

**Medications and Medical History:**

High blood pressure medication: Lisinopril/Hydrochlorothiazide 20/25 mg by mouth once daily (this is a combination blood pressure medication- two meds are combined into one single tablet)

Diabetes medication: Metformin 1000 mg one tablet by mouth twice daily. If anyone asks you about your medications you believe that you take them how they are supposed to be taken, but you may miss a few doses here or there, especially if you work late. You don’t check your blood sugars regularly. You don’t know your current Hemoglobin A1c or fasting blood sugars.

You don’t take any other medications (no herbals, no over the counters, no vitamins).

You have been on all these medications for about 5-6 years. You see your family doctor every year or so. You have had okay health overall prior to this incident. You were told you have high cholesterol but you told your doctor you would manage it by eating well and exercising. However, you haven’t done well at making lifestyle adjustments. Since it is only you and your spouse, you often eat fast food or order out (you have no children).

You had a few medical conditions prior to this hospitalization. You knew you had high blood pressure, diabetes, and high cholesterol.

You have smoked 1 pack of cigarettes daily for the past 23 years. You drink alcohol occasionally – no more than a couple beers a week.

High blood pressure runs in your family. Your father died at age 47 from a heart attack. You are concerned you will “end up like him”.

You remember being hospitalized twice in your lifetime (tonsillectomy at age 7, appendectomy at age 16).

You are allergic to penicillin. You got a rash once and haven’t taken it since you were a child.

**Physical Findings:**

The students will want to assess which side of your body is weak. You will have right-sided weakness (lower face, arm, legs). You have some right-sided weakness/sensory abnormalities in your lower face and arm (more than your lower leg). The leg weakness has resolved for the most part since yesterday but is still not back to baseline. The weakness/sensory issues in your lower face and arm have improved since yesterday but are still moderately weak/affected. Essentially, for any “strength”-type of assessment that students ask you to do with your arms or legs, the “right” response should be weakened. (e.g., SPs may be asked to “squeeze a student’s fingers”- in this case, the grip strength should be stronger on the left hand compared to the right).

These are examples of things that you still have difficulty with regarding the right side of your body:

1.) Occasional toe drag with right leg (if asked to walk)

2.) Occasional slight knee buckling with weight bearing or walking exercises with right leg (although not complete loss of knee control)

3.) Difficulty with fine motor tasks with right arm/hand (e.g., using a utensil)

You have some lack of feeling in the lower right side of your face and arm. Although the lack of sensation in your face and arm are still moderate, the sensation has improved since yesterday. You should not be able to differentiate between sharp and dull touch on the right side of face. You can feel something touching you, but you can’t differentiate between a sharp pain (e.g., being jabbed with a toothpick) or light touch (e.g., stroked with a cotton swab). The lack of feeling in your lower right leg has resolved for the most part since yesterday. Students may use a cotton-tipped applicator to check your sensation of “light touch”; they may break the applicator and use the “splintered ends” to assess your ability to sense a “pin-prick”. Again, your sensation should be less on the right side, and you can’t differentiate between the different sensations. You may also be unsure of the “localization” (aka, the exact location) of the sensation on the right side. Essentially, between the right and left side, when touched on the right side, you can feel pressure but it just doesn’t feel the same as the left side.

You have trouble speaking. Your speech is slightly slurred and slow. Your spouse (who is not present in the room at the current moment) tells you that the slurring has improved since yesterday. You express concern that your “speech will never be normal again.”

Essentially, when students ask about dysfunction on your right side, let them know that it was relatively severe at first, but it is getting better. Most importantly, the face and arm on the right side should be weaker than the left and should be the weakest of all body systems assessed. Your leg and foot should also be weak on the right side, but NOT as weak or with sensory disturbances as severe as the face and arm.

You may describe certain objects rather than name them (this is because you aren’t able to recall the name of the object) (e.g. you cannot think of the “name” for clock; instead you point and describe it as “the thing on the wall that tells time”. When you have difficulty identifying objects, you begin to become frustrated. You may stop mid phrase out of frustration. If students ask you to remember the names of 3 objects (e.g. key, pencil, watch), you can only recall two of them.

You do not have any dizziness or vision changes. You do not have chest pain, shortness of breath, headache, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, or sweating. You have not had any sickness recently, nor were you in contact with anyone else who was ill. You have weakness and numbness of your right arm and face. You are feeling depressed and useless now.

**Social situation:**

You live in a 3 story townhome with multiple steps.

The only full bathroom is on the top floor. Your bedroom is upstairs as well.

You have had no trouble with stairs in the past but are unsure how you will climb them after this incident; you haven’t been out of bed yet, so you haven’t attempted to walk much less go up a flight of stairs and you are worried about weakness in your legs.

You are a factory worker. Your spouse works full-time as an administrative professional for a local accounting firm but does not have access to health insurance. You have good insurance through your job, but there were financial concerns prior to this event and now money will be even more of a problem if you cannot continue your employment. You are very worried about health insurance if you lose your job.

**Questions/statements you may ask students: (ask with slightly slurred speech)**

“When will I be able to be back to normal?”

“Will I ever be able to work again?”

“I feel so bad for my spouse…”

“I wish I could do more for myself. My spouse has a full-time job and now has to take care of me, but I am the only one that can get health insurance.”

“How will we ever be able to pay the bills?”
