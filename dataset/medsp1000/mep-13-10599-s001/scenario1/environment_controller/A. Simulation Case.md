| **Appendix A: MedEdPORTAL Simulation Case Template**  **SIMULATION CASE TITLE:** Managing the Complex Issues of Pediatric Non-accidental Trauma: a simulation-based case of a critically injured child.  **AUTHORS:** Matthew Ryan, Peggy White, Sean Kiley, Heather Reed and Chris Giordano | |
| **PATIENT NAME:** Pat  **PATIENT AGE:** 16 months  **CHIEF COMPLAINT:** Non-responsive | |
| **Brief narrative description of case** | The patient is a 16-month-old child who was found unresponsive on the kitchen floor by her mother who just came home from work. The patient was left in the care of the mother’s boyfriend while her mother was working. The mother has previously witnessed the boyfriend’s impatience with her child but never said anything to him. When the mother found her child on the floor, she called emergency medical services (EMS). The mother’s boyfriend was sleeping on the couch. Upon EMS arrival, the patient was assessed to have a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 6. EMS was unable to intubate the patient at the scene and brought the patient to the ED with a laryngeal mask airway in place and noted the patient was getting more difficult to oxygenate using an ambubag. Upon arrival, non-accidental trauma (NAT) may not be initially evident but should become apparent when the patient is exposed; the patient has multiple abrasions and areas of injury evident upon exposure about the forehead, trunk and thigh. While no one on the team knows exactly what happened, the back story is the boyfriend grabbed the child by the left arm and threw the patient into a wall in the kitchen because the child spilled food on the floor. He then kicked the child several times. He left the kitchen and fell asleep on the couch. If management is correct, the patient’s condition will improve. If management is incorrect, without addressing the patient’s critical issues, the patient will have PEA arrest and with some interventions via ATLS and PALS, the patient should recover. |
| **Learner Preparation** | Learners should be 4th year medical students or residents. No specific pre-requisites are needed otherwise. The learners should be informed of the resources at their disposal throughout the simulation. This includes human resources such as nursing staff, as well as a full set of medicines, equipment, and diagnostic testing capabilities. |

| **Overall Appearance** | The patient is on a stretcher with EMS standing by. The patient is being bagged with increased difficulty to maintain an oxygen saturation of greater than 90%. EMS was called by the mother when she found the child unconscious on the kitchen floor. EMS could not intubate the patient (if asked why, you can tell trainees they had an equipment error or the patient’s airway was too anterior to definitively see or for fear of aspiration). The patient is not wearing a cervical-collar as EMS did not have an age-appropriate. The patient however was transported to the hospital on a backboard. The patient arrives with a GCS 6. The patient’s blood glucose per finger stick was 80 mg/dL in the field. En route, EMS noted tachycardia and an initial blood pressure (BP) of 70/40. EMS also notes rapid shallow respirations (base rate of 50) and stated the patient feels cold. EMS was able to place a 20-guage intravenous catheter in the right antecubital fossa and initiated a weight-based bolus of normal saline (patient weight = 11 kg). The mother and her boyfriend will arrive about 10 minutes into the simulation (this time can change depending on how the trainees perform, e.g., longer if the simulation team needs more time). The boyfriend will be unmoved emotionally regarding the patient’s condition. He simply states the patient was fine when he last saw her. The team will have to decide how to engage the mother and the boyfriend. | | |
| **Actors and roles in the room at case start** | Confederates are typically played by faculty. The roles are as follows.   * Paramedic who brings in patient. Paramedic provides a concise recap of the situation and interventions undertaken prior to arrival. * Mother. The mother is not initially present. The mother’s role is one of concern and while she is upset, she is cooperative and does not obstruct the medical care of her child in anyway. She wants to stay with her child but she does not want to interfere with the care she is receiving. The mother engages if addressed by team. * Boyfriend. The boyfriend is not initially present and arrives with the mother. The boyfriend is indifferent and if asked, he denies knowing anything that happened to the child.   Note, one actor can play multiple roles. For example, the paramedic can simply state the mother (or boyfriend) is in the waiting room and then leave to bring the family member into the trauma bay. At that time, they assume the role of the family member.  We found it convenient when having limited actors to assist with simulations to make ID badges (or a labeled piece of tape) that have in large type the role of each character, for example, MOTHER, BOYFRIEND, EMS, SURGEON. | | |

| *Get report from EMS at the beginning of the case* | The patient arrives via EMS and is currently being bagged with increasing difficulty to maintain an oxygen saturation of greater than 90%. The patient is not wearing a cervical collar. A 20-guage intravenous catheter in the right antecubital fossa placed by EMS is noted. | EMS was called by the mother when she found her child unconscious on the kitchen floor. EMS could not intubate the patient. If asked why, EMS can tell trainees they had an equipment error or the patient’s airway was too anterior to definitively see or for fear of aspiration. EMS also did not have the appropriate size cervical collar. |
| *The team should perform a primary survey and recognize respiratory distress.* | The team should recognize the patient is critically injured and will need to be intubated based on the patient’s unstable vital signs and clinical presentation including a GCS = 6. Following ATLS and PALS, the team should secure an airway; airway assessment should be performed and equipment and medications for rapid sequence intubation should be prepared. Also, an IV fluid bolus should given and an age appropriate cervical collar applied. | The initial vital signs are heart rate of 145, blood pressure of 70/40 respiratory rate = bag-valve-mask rate (BVM) with a baseline rate of 50, temperature of 33.5 °C and oxygen saturation of 90% via BVM. The team should describe the basics for setting up for rapid sequence intubation, including equipment needed (appropriate sized endotracheal tube, laryngeal scope blade size, back up laryngeal scope handle, endotracheal tube stylet, syringe for cuff inflation, suction at the ready, and medications for sedation and paralytic agent). Broselow tape or similar adjunct is available. The team may ask for prompts as needed. |
| *5 minutes into the case: secure patient airway* | The intubation should go smoothly if basic equipment and medications as noted are at the bedside. If not, a nurse, respiratory therapist or other confederate should provide some guidance and prompts as needed. | Vital signs post-intubation are heart rate = 140, blood pressure of 80/60, and temperature of 33.5 °C with a respiratory rate equal to the ventilator rate and oxygen saturation of 99%. Sinus tachycardia is noted on the monitor. The team may use Broselow tape or similar weight based adjunct, e.g., smart phone application, for assistance with weight based doses of medications and age and size appropriate equipment. |
| *Confirm endotracheal tube placement* | The team will need to confirm endotracheal tube (ET) placement via capnography, direct observation of the tube passing the vocal cords and other indicators (chest wall rise, condensation in the ET tube, breathe sounds). | Vital signs post-intubation are heart rate of 125, blood pressure of 80/60, and temperature of 33.5 °C with a respiratory rate = ventilator rate and oxygen saturation of 99%. Here a prompt may be needed to ask how tube placement was confirmed. If technically possible, the use of end-tidal carbon dioxide monitor is available otherwise it can simply be stated end-tidal carbon dioxide level is 40. A chest x-ray is available. Note consistent with the fictional contract of simulations, the instructor will have to say the tube is properly positioned as verified by post-intubation CXR. |
| *The mother and boyfriend arrive and are available to answer questions.* | The team should engage the mother and boyfriend to elicit more information about what happened to the child. The mother will state she found her child unresponsive on the kitchen floor and called 911 when the patient would not respond. The boyfriend will deny any knowledge of what happened indicating he fell asleep on the couch and that the child was fine when he saw her. | The team should become concerned for NAT and call social work or relevant child protective services. A nurse or confederate should prompt the team down this path if needed. A team member can attempt to separate the mother and boyfriend to obtain further information from each. At this point, the mother can state she has been working a lot lately and her boyfriend has become more impatient with her daughter. The boyfriend will continue to deny any knowledge of the events leading to the patient’s injuries. |
| *The mother asks if she can stay during the resuscitation.* | The team will have to decide it they want to have the mother stay for the resuscitation. As the patient is critically injured, the team will need to address and articulate their decision as to whether the mother can stay or not. | While there is no right or wrong answer, the team will have to decide how best to manage the mother of the patient. Either way, a team member should be stay with the mother for a few minutes to explain to her the situation and the need for emergent care. The mother is noted to be concerned but cooperative. |
| *Recognize on physical exam as part of the primary survey decreased breathe sounds on the left side and signs of a pneumothorax.* | The patient will have decreased breathe sounds and decreased chest wall rise on the left side. Following ATLS and PALS guidelines, the clinical signs and symptoms of a tension pneumothorax should be evident on exam. A chest x-ray if requested will show multiple left-sided rib fractures and concern for pneumothorax given clinical signs and symptoms. The team will need to place a chest tube. Needle decompression of the pneumothorax is an accepted 1st line treatment but the team will still need to place a chest tube. Trauma surgery is unavailable at this time. | The team should articulate their interpretation of the chest x-ray findings which may be then confirmed by a discussion with radiology. The instructors may ask here for the team to describe chest tube placement. An ultrasound can also be used to confirm a pneumothorax. Post chest tube placement, vital signs improve to heart rate of 105, blood pressure 105/75, respiratory rate = ventilator rate, temperature 33.5 °C and oxygen saturation of 99%. |
| *10-15 minutes into the case with no chest tube – PEA arrest.* | Without timely chest tube placement after intubation, the patient will go into PEA arrest. The team will work through PALS for one to two cycles per instructor. Needle decompression or chest tube placement of the left chest will restore spontaneous circulation. | No measureable vital signs at this point of the case. While running through PALS secondary to PEA arrest. Here, a prompt regarding absent left-sided breath sounds may be given. |
| *Post-needle decompression or chest tube placed while patient is in PEA arrest.* | The patient will have improved vital signs but still remains hypothermic. The team will now need to rewarm patient. | Patient vital signs improve to heart rate of 105, blood pressure 105/75, respiratory rate = ventilator rate, temperature 33.5 °C and oxygen saturation of 99%. |
| *10-15 minutes into case. Warm patient.* | The team should use warm IV fluids and make attempts to directly warm the patient by increasing the room temperature and through warmed blankets and active forced air-warming. | With warming, vital signs improve to heart rate of 90, blood pressure of 115/75, respiratory rate = ventilator rate, temperature of 36.7 °C and oxygen saturation of 99%. The monitor shows sinus rhythm |
| *10-15 minutes into case. Warming of patient does not occur which leads to PEA arrest.* | Without rewarming, the patient will go into PEA arrest. The team will work through PALS for one to two cycles per instructor. The team will need to recognize and treat the patient’s hypothermia. With initiation of treatment and proper PALS, the patient will recover. | No measureable vital signs at this point of the case. While running through PALS, a prompt regarding the patient’s core body temperature may be needed. After the team successfully resuscitates the patient, with warming, vital signs improve; for example, heart rate of 90, blood pressure of 115/75, respiratory rate = ventilator rate, temperature of 36.7 °C and oxygen saturation of 99%. The monitor shows sinus rhythm. |
| *Perform secondary survey of patient once the patient is stabilized.* | The team should in an organized fashion complete a head to toe secondary survey. This information will be important when calling consult services. | Pediatric and orthopedic surgery and PICU consults may ask details of the secondary survey. If not performed, the team will need to go back and reassess the patient. Computed tomography imaging of the head, cervical spine, abdomen and pelvis obtained after the child is stabilized will all be normal. |
| *Consults need to be called: pediatric surgery, orthopedics, the PICU and social work.* | The team needs to discuss the case with the appropriate consults for evaluation and continued medical management. Pediatric surgery needs to be consulted for management of the patient’s injuries and the PICU needs to be consulted for admission of an intubated critically injured patient. The team will also need to discuss the concern for NAT with social work or other appropriate authority. They must articulate the reason for their concerns. | The PICU and surgery consults need to be emergent – both consultants need to see patient in the ED - while the orthopedic consult can occur as an inpatient. The team can now transition care to the admitting service which will conclude this single patient encounter. |
