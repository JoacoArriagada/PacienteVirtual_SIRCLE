Slide 10:

All of these infants should have CBC, Blood culture and Catheterized urine sample for urinalysis and culture. Most experts advocate for these children to receive a lumbar puncture with CSF studies. The Rochester protocol is the only protocol that specifically addresses infants in this age group. (Pass out Table 1 from Meehan, et al. 2010 article for protocol reference)

A recent study assessed the week-by-week analysis of serious bacterial infection in febrile neonates. They studied all febrile neonates presenting to their ED over a 9 year period and found an overall serious bacterial infection rate of 19.4%. The highest risk of SBI was in the 2nd week at 26.1%. This risk decreased each week to 12.1% in the 4th week of life. The authors concluded that low risk criteria are not sufficiently reliable to exclude the presence of SBI and recommended all febrile neonates be hospitalized and undergo a full sepsis evaluation. In most practice settings, the majority of febrile neonates will require a full sepsis evaluation, antibiotics and admission. However, in some practice locations, the local standard of care supports discharging neonates with a negative evaluation if they have reliable follow-up in 24 hours.

What antibiotics are you giving and what organisms are you covering?

The most common organisms are still from the birth canal. Ampicillin (to cover L. monocytogenes) and gentamicin or cefotaxime (preferably not ceftriaxone for neonates because it competes for bilirubin binding sites and may lead to hyperbilirubinemia) to cover GBS and E Coli. What about Acyclovir? Definitely give if ill-appearing, pleocytosis with negative gram stain, vesicles, or seizures.

Slide 13:

What are the types of HSV? There are 3 types isolated skin, eye, mucus membrane (~45%), CNS (~33%), or disseminated (~25%). HSV can be neurologically devastating or fatal if not treated in the early stages of disease.

Slide 14:

Neonates born to a Mom with active HSV infection are at high risk of neonatal HSV.

Unfortunately, in the majority of patients with neonatal HSV, there is no maternal history of HSV.

Slide 15:

There is no consensus regarding which infants should receive empiric antiviral therapy. The AAP Red Book suggests suspecting neonatal HSV up to 6 weeks for infants with fever, hypothermia, sepsis, or CNS disease. Infants with suspected HSV can be tested with DNA PCR in the blood or CSF, collecting vesicle fluid for culture and direct fluorescent antibody testing, and by swabbing nasopharynx, conjunctiva, and rectum for culture. They may also have thrombocytopenia or transaminitis.
