Slide 17:

In a retrospective study of infants < 2 months with a history of fever who received an evaluation and were admitted to the hospital 92% of the infants who had rectal temperature at home had subsequent fever in next 48 hours. So, even if they are afebrile on presentation you can trust the rectal temperature at home.

How would your management change if it was a forehead temperature?

Slide 19:

There is no evidence to support the use of forehead strip thermometers. Temporal thermometers are a little different. These are infrared devices that measure the heat radiating from the skin on the forehead as the sensor head is moved across the forehead and then placed behind the ear lobe. During this movement, the device rapidly measures skin temperatures, locking in on the highest temperature. Using a manufacturer-devised calculation, the device then predicts core temperature. In a recent study temporal artery temperature was 0.46 less +/- 0.5. In spite of evidence in support of temporal thermometers, rectal temperatures are preferred in this age group and the majority of published data use rectal temperatures.

Slide 20:

Axillary temperatures were significantly lower than rectal temperatures but there was not a reliable discrepancy. So, you can’t just “add a degree” to get the core temperature. If a child has a fever by axillary measurement (without adding a degree) it is an accurate representation of fever.
