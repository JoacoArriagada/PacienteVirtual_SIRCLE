**Case 2- Inappropriate Medicine**

**Training Materials for Standardized Patient**

**Family Member Profile:**

1. Age: 30-40
2. Gender: Male or Female
3. Affect (mannerisms, behavior): Initially sad and accepting, eventually distrustful and angry
4. Social History/Lifestyle: lives in town, healthy
5. Occupation: working as a teacher
6. Marital Status: married
7. General appearance: neat in appearance

**Scenario:**

Your father, Eddie Cabot, was brought to the emergency department by an ambulance yesterday evening after a fall at home. You had eaten dinner together last night before leaving to go to your own house. A few hours later, you received a fall from your father, who stated that he tripped and hit his head. You immediately called an ambulance and went back to his house. You know he is at increased risk for head bleeds because of his medications, and his doctor was very clear that your father needed to be seen by a doctor any time he hits his head.

Eddie’s head CT in the emergency department last night showed a small head bleed, and he was admitted to the ICU overnight for close monitoring. You went home to sleep after Eddie was admitted, because you had to work this morning. You received a call while at work that your father had worsened overnight and was headed to surgery to control the bleeding in his brain.

You are worried and feel guilty for leaving. You were told by the doctors overnight that Eddie was at risk for worsening bleeding, but both you and your father felt that he looked well enough for you to leave to get some sleep. You are now waiting in Eddie’s hospital room while he recovers from surgery. The neurosurgery team has stopped in to tell you that the surgery went well, but that Eddie will continue to have a breathing tube in for the time being. You are grateful for the reassuring news but are lost in thought when the intensive care unit resident comes to speak with you. After it is disclosed that Eddie received two medications this morning that could have worsened his bleeding, you transition from feelings of sadness and guilt to confusion and accusation. You are not aggressive in your responses, but ask multiple, detailed questions to get a straight answer from the resident.

**Instructions for the Family Member**

1. **How the family responds to the physician’s initial inquiry**
   1. When the medical student enters the room, thank them for taking the time to speak with you, and ask, “How is Eddie doing? Any updates?”
2. **The family member’s demeanor at the beginning and throughout the encounter (affect, non-verbal behavior)**
   1. You are sad and feeling guilty for leaving your father when you were told he could get worse. As the details of the event unfold, you become progressively more confused, then accusatory.
3. **The family member’s concerns regarding his/her understanding**
   1. You are torn between relief that your father’s surgery went well but are concerned that Eddie is receiving care at a hospital that continued to give him medications that could make his bleeding worse. Ask questions about this accordingly, but do not escalate to insisting Eddie be transferred. You may request that a different medicine team takes over his care.
4. **How the family member will respond to different interviewing styles**
   1. If the student is evasive and does not mention that an error occurred, continue to express your guilt over leaving your father last night despite being informed of this risk. Continue to express that you are surprised by this outcome because of how well Eddie looked before you left.
   2. If the student declines to offer an apology or empathetic statement, become angrier and more accusatory
5. **Questions the family member will consistently ask during the encounter**
   1. “Who is responsible for this?”
   2. “How could this happen?”
   3. “Is he going to be alright?”
   4. “Who is going to pay for this?”
   5. \*\*In response to medical student inquiry about further questions:
      1. “How do I know this won’t happen again?”
6. **The challenges the family member will present to the physician**
   1. You are struggling to understand how your father transitioned from a small head bleed to requiring brain surgery. You also may not be able to answer all the physician’s questions.
