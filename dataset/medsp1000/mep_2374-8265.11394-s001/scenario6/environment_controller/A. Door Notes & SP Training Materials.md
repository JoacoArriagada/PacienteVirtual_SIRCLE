**Case 5- Inaccurate Sign-Out**

**Medical Student**

**Patient’s Name:** Ellie Danan

**Medical Profile:** 50-year-old female with a past medical history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary artery disease (CAD), and chronic tobacco use who presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath.

**Background:** Ms. Danan was initially seen by your emergency medicine colleague and her care was signed over to you pending a repeat troponin and EKG. At the time of sign-out, Ms. Danan had already received a chest x-ray that **YOUR COLLEAGUE PERSONALLY REVIEWED AND REPORTED TO YOU AS NEGATIVE,** and basic lab work including a CBC (significant for mild leukocytosis of 12.3), a BMP (no significant abnormalities), and a troponin (<0.03 ng/mL). Her first electrocardiogram (ECG) is unchanged from priors. Ms. Danan was updated by your colleague of the plan and was told she would likely go home with a presumed diagnosis of COPD exacerbation.

Ms. Danan’s second troponin is also negative, and a repeat ECG shows no dynamic changes. Prior to discharge, you review her chest x-ray and notice what appears to be a **MODERATE-SIZED PNEUMOTHORAX**. You open the radiology read, which confirms a 30% right-sided pneumothorax with no mediastinal shift. You return to the room to inform Ms. Danan of the change in plans. She is upset with this news, repeatedly stating that “the other doctor said I could go home,” and “they said my pictures were fine.” After an extensive conversation, you consent her for a chest tube and admit her to the hospital.

**Patient’s Present Condition:** Ms. Danan undergoes placement of a right-sided pigtail catheter and is admitted to the floor. She remains hemodynamically stable and is not requiring supplemental oxygen.

Your colleague receives sign-out from you the next morning and asks how the shift went.

You have 15 minutes to inform your co-resident of the error and answer any questions he/she may have.

**Case 5- Inaccurate Sign-Out**

**Training Materials for Standardized Patient**

**Co-Resident Profile:**

1. Age: 20s-30s
2. Gender: Male or Female
3. Affect (mannerisms, behavior): Tired, stressed
4. Social History/Lifestyle: Healthy
5. Occupation: Resident physician
6. Marital Status: Single
7. General appearance: dressed in scrubs or office-appropriate clothing

**Scenario:**

Ellie Danan is a 50-year-old female with a past medical history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary artery disease (CAD), and chronic tobacco use who presented to you in the emergency department with shortness of breath. You ordered lab work and a chest x-ray, which you reviewed prior to signing the patient out to your colleague at the end of your shift. You believe everything looked normal, and you planned for the patient to go home after her lab work. You went home after sign-out, had a restful night, and are now returning for your morning shift and to receive sign-out from the same colleague you saw last night.

The learner is approaching you to discuss findings on the patient’s chest x-ray that were missed on your initial review. These findings suggest the patient had a pneumothorax—air that escaped the lung and stays in the chest cavity. This often requires a procedure to remove the air. In this case, the patient had a small chest tube placed, and had to be admitted to the hospital.

**Instructions for the Resident**

1. **How the resident responds to the physician’s initial inquiry**
   1. You are friendly and unsuspecting when your colleague approaches you and ask, “are you ready to sign out?”
2. **The resident’s demeanor at the beginning and throughout the encounter (affect, non-verbal behavior)**
   1. You are initially friendly, then progressively more defensive and embarrassed, but relieved the error was caught before the patient became sicker.
3. **The resident’s concerns regarding his/her understanding**
   1. You are worried, both about how you could have missed an important finding, and how your colleagues see you.
4. **How the resident will respond to different interviewing styles**
   1. If the student is evasive and does not mention an error has occurred, become progressively more confused and ask if the patient is okay/did you do anything wrong.
   2. If the student makes excuses on your behalf/continues to be evasive, accept the “out,” and thank them for bringing it to your attention
   3. If the student acts in an accusatory manner, become more defensive and shut down.
5. **Questions the resident will consistently ask during the encounter**
   1. “But is she’s going to be okay, right?”
   2. “Well the radiologist didn’t call me about the finding, so it’s their fault, right?”
   3. “Anyone could have made this mistake though, right?
   4. “Are you going to tell anyone about this? Who all knows?”
   5. “What are we supposed to do now?”
6. **The challenges the resident will present to the physician**
   1. You had not given this patient another thought after leaving the hospital and are surprised by this development. You are embarrassed and will try to blame other people/the system for this error.
