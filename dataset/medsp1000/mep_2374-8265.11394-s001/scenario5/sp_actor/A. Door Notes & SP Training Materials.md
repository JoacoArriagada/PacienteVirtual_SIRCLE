**Case 4- Misdiagnosed Sign-Out**

**Training Materials for Standardized Patient**

**Co-Resident Profile:**

1. Age: 20s-30s
2. Gender: Male or Female
3. Affect (mannerisms, behavior): Tired, stressed
4. Social History/Lifestyle: Healthy
5. Occupation: Resident physician
6. Marital Status: Single
7. General appearance: dressed in scrubs or office-appropriate clothing

**Scenario:**

Ms. Jennifer Wright is a 22-year-old previously healthy female who presented to you with palpitations and shortness of breath. You diagnosed her with supraventricular tachycardia, and abnormal heart rhythm, and gave her medication (adenosine) to treat this. Unfortunately, Ms. Wright then went into a deadly heart rhythm, ventricular fibrillation, which required cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). You were able to restore a normal heart rhythm and admitted the patient to the intensive care unit (ICU) with a cardiology consult. You are now wrapping up at the end of your shift when you are approached by your co-resident, who is currently rotating on the cardiology service.

The learner is approaching you to discuss findings on the patient’s heart tracing (electrocardiogram/ECG) that were missed on your initial review. These findings suggest the patient actually was presenting with Wolf-Parkinson-White syndrome (WPW), and the medication that you gave the patient most likely led to her deadly heart rhythm.

**Instructions for the Resident**

1. **How the resident responds to the physician’s initial inquiry**
   1. You are friendly and unsuspecting when your colleague approaches you.
2. **The resident’s demeanor at the beginning and throughout the encounter (affect, non-verbal behavior)**
   1. You are initially friendly, then progressively more defensive and embarrassed, then worried about what could have happened, should the patient have died.
3. **The resident’s concerns regarding his/her understanding**
   1. You are worried, about both the patient and how your colleagues see you.
4. **How the resident will respond to different interviewing styles**
   1. If the student is evasive and does not mention an error has occurred, become progressively more confused and ask if the patient is okay/did you do anything wrong.
   2. If the student makes excuses on your behalf/continues to be evasive, act humbly but proud of how you managed the patient’s deadly heart rhythm, and the fact that she survived.
   3. If the student acts in an accusatory manner, become more defensive and shut down.
5. **Questions the resident will consistently ask during the encounter**
   1. “But is she’s going to be okay, right?”
   2. “Anyone could have made this mistake though, right?
   3. “Are you going to tell anyone about this? Who all knows?”
   4. “What are we supposed to do now?”
6. **The challenges the resident will present to the physician**
   1. Initially, you were feeling confident with how you handled this patient’s case and were happy with her “good” outcome. You feel like your clinical decisions are now being challenged and embarrassed about being approached about this.
