**Case 3B- Missed/Delayed Diagnosis**

**Training Materials for Standardized Patient**

**Co-Resident Profile:**

1. Age: 20s-30s
2. Gender: Male or Female
3. Affect (mannerisms, behavior): Tired, stressed
4. Social History/Lifestyle: Healthy
5. Occupation: Resident physician
6. Marital Status: Single
7. General appearance: dressed in scrubs or office-appropriate clothing

**Scenario:**

You are an emergency medicine resident on your sixth shift of six in a row. During your shift, you received a text message from your co-resident asking if you could stay late to meet for coffee in the hospital cafeteria. While you know your co-resident well, you are not close friends, so you are already apprehensive about this meeting.

The learner will be discussing a poor outcome from a patient you saw yesterday. You barely remember the patient, as it was a busy shift. You vaguely recall seeing a young boy for nausea and vomiting who reported some abdominal pain. His abdominal exam was unimpressive, and he felt better after ibuprofen and nausea medicine, and you sent him home with appendicitis return precautions, like you do with all of your pediatric abdominal pain patients. You are unaware that the patient returned with a dead testicle and underwent emergency surgery to have it removed.

**Instructions for the Resident**

1. **How the resident responds to the physician’s initial inquiry**
   1. You are friendly but cautious when the medical student enters the room and exchange basic pleasantries before asking “what did you want to talk about?”
2. **The resident’s demeanor at the beginning and throughout the encounter (affect, non-verbal behavior)**
   1. You are initially hesitant in your conversation, then surprised with the news, then progressively more withdrawn and the gravity of the situation settles in.
3. **The resident’s concerns regarding his/her understanding**
   1. You are worried, both about the patient and also your status as a resident.
4. **How the resident will respond to different interviewing styles**
   1. If the student is evasive and does not mention an error has occurred, become progressively more confused and ask if the patient is okay/did you do anything wrong.
   2. If the student makes excuses on your behalf/continues to be evasive, act relieved that you did not make an error, and express sadness on behalf of the patient’s misfortune.
   3. If the student acts in an accusatory manner, become more defensive and shut down.
5. **Questions the resident will consistently ask during the encounter**
   1. “But is he going to be okay?”
   2. “How are his parents?”
   3. “Are you going to tell anyone about this? Who all knows? Can we keep this quiet?”
   4. “What are we supposed to do now?”
6. **The challenges the resident will present to the physician**
   1. You really enjoy your job, but you are a little insecure about your clinical knowledge base, and are embarrassed to be approached about an oversight, which makes you defensive. You are also feeling guilty about the poor outcome and are concerned about a lawsuit.
